What Are Fixed Prepositions?

A fixed preposition is a preposition that always travels with one particular word. The two work as a single unit, so grammar books also call them dependent prepositions, and many teachers simply call them word partners. Take "afraid of". You don't pick "of" because it makes sense. You use it because English has always paired those two words.

Here are three quick examples:

  • She is afraid of lizards. (not afraid from)
  • We are interested in the new course. (not interested on)
  • The result depends on your preparation. (not depends of)

Notice that the meaning of the sentence doesn't help you choose the preposition. Nothing about fear points to "of" rather than "from". That's exactly why examiners love this topic. They know a student can understand the whole sentence and still lose the mark.

Why Do Fixed Prepositions Feel So Random?

Most students try to solve these by logic or by translating from their mother tongue. Both methods fail here, and there are three reasons.

First, there's no rule. These pairs grew out of centuries of habit, so the dictionary is the only authority. Second, translation misleads. A word that takes one preposition in your language often takes a different one in English. "Married with" feels natural to many learners, yet English says married to. Third, one preposition can't be tied to one idea. "Of" is right after afraid but wrong after depend, so you can't give each preposition a fixed job in your head.

Here's the good news. Because these pairs are habits and not rules, you can build the habit on purpose. That's what the rest of this guide is for.

Fixed Prepositions vs Prepositions of Time and Place

Don't mix this topic up with the in, on and at you use for time and place. They look alike but work differently.

Why Fixed Prepositions Matter for Your Score

Think about how many questions in a single English paper can hinge on one small word. An error spotting sentence may be correct in every part except one preposition. A fill in the blanks question may offer four prepositions that all look plausible. A cloze passage may hide five of these pairs in a single paragraph. Each question is worth the same as a hard vocabulary question, yet it takes less time once you know the pair.

That makes fixed prepositions one of the best returns on your study time. You don't need a huge vocabulary or deep grammar theory. You need a few hundred pairs, learned in a smart way, and the confidence to answer fast. Students who ignore this topic often lose marks they could have collected in seconds.

6 Memory Tricks to Stop Forgetting Fixed Prepositions

Memorising a long list in alphabetical order is the slowest way to learn this topic. These six tricks work much better.

Trick 1: Learn the pair, never the preposition alone

Don't write "of" in your notebook. Write "afraid of". Say it aloud as one chunk, the way you'd say a phone number. When the chunk sounds familiar, the right answer jumps out in the exam.

Trick 2: Use word families

Many partners come in groups. Learn the family once and you've learned five pairs.

  • Fear = OF: afraid of, scared of, terrified of, frightened of
  • Skill = AT: good at, bad at, clever at, quick at, excellent at
  • Answers = TO: solution to, answer to, key to, reply to. They all point to something.
  • Crime = OF: accuse of, suspect of, convict of. The police accuse, suspect and convict, and all three take of.

Trick 3: Make a picture

Your brain remembers pictures better than rules. For depend on, imagine a small child standing on her father's shoulders. She depends on him. For insist on, picture someone planting both feet on the ground and refusing to move. For interested in, see your attention going in to the topic. Silly pictures work best, because silly pictures stay.

Trick 4: Remember the DREAM verbs

Some verbs need no preposition at all, and adding one is a very common error. Remember DREAM:

  • Discuss: discuss the plan (not discuss about)
  • Reach: reach Pune (not reach to Pune)
  • Enter: enter the hall (not enter into the hall)
  • Attend: attend the meeting (not attend to the meeting)
  • Marry: marry her (not marry with her)

One caution: "attend to" does exist, but it means to take care of something, as in "The nurse attended to the patient". For a meeting, class or function, use attend with no preposition.

Trick 5: Write one sentence of your own

Textbook examples are easy to forget. A sentence about your own life isn't. "I'm bad at cooking, but I'm proud of my tea." That single line locks in two pairs, because it belongs to you.

Trick 6: Learn the contrast pairs together

Some words change partners depending on what follows. Angry with a person, angry at a thing or event. Agree with a person, agree to a proposal. And always prefer X to Y, never "prefer X than Y". Put the contrast in one note and you'll rarely mix them up.

5 Mistakes That Cost Marks

These five slip-ups appear again and again in error spotting questions.

MistakeWrongRight
Choosing by meaning, not habitHe is interested on music.He is interested in music.
Adding a preposition to a DREAM verbWe discussed about the budget.We discussed the budget.
Using than after preferI prefer tea than coffee.I prefer tea to coffee.
Dropping a needed prepositionPlease listen the instructions.Please listen to the instructions.
Ignoring changed meaningLook the word in the dictionary.Look the word up in the dictionary.

Many of these have both a wrong version and a correct one that sounds almost identical. So slow down and check the pair, not the whole sentence.

The 6-Step Method to Make Them Stick

Here's a simple routine you can follow every day. Keep it short, because ten minutes daily beats two hours once a week.

  1. Collect only your misses. Keep a small notebook and write down the pairs you got wrong, not every pair you meet.
  2. Sort them into families. Fear, skill, answers, crime, DREAM. Put each new pair in a family.
  3. Attach a trick. Add a picture, a rhyme or a family label to each pair.
  4. Write your own sentence. One sentence per pair, about your own life.
  5. Revise on a schedule. Look again after 1 day, 3 days, 7 days and 21 days.
  6. Test with exam-style questions. Do a few error spotting and fill in the blanks questions every day.

Here's how a 15-minute day can look. Spend five minutes revising yesterday's pairs from your notebook. Spend five minutes on the new pairs, with a trick and a sentence for each. Spend the last five minutes on exam-style questions. That's all. On the days you feel lazy, do only the notebook revision, because skipping a whole day makes it hard to restart.

How Exams Test Fixed Prepositions

Fixed prepositions show up in error spotting, fill in the blanks, sentence improvement and cloze tests in SSC CGL, bank, railway and state PSC papers. Here are three questions in the usual style.

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: Error Spotting
The manager insisted (A) / for the immediate (B) / submission of the report. (C) / No error (D)
Answer: A. It should be "insisted on".
Common Exam Pattern: Fill in the blank
She is quite good ___ solving puzzles.
Answer: at.
Common Exam Pattern: Sentence improvement
The teacher discussed about the topic with the students.
Answer: discussed the topic.

A quick way to attack an error spotting question

Don't read the whole sentence slowly. Scan for the word that usually has a partner: an adjective, a verb or a noun. Then ask, "What's its partner?" In the first question above, the word "insisted" jumps out. Its partner is "on", but the sentence says "for". The error is found in about five seconds, and you never needed to understand the full sentence.

Expert Tips

  • Read an English newspaper daily and underline every word-plus-preposition pair you notice. Seeing pairs in real sentences builds the habit faster than lists.
  • Don't trust "it sounds right". Your ear may have been trained on wrong usage. Check a good dictionary, which shows the partner in its example sentences.
  • Revise in the morning. A five-minute recall before you start other work works better than a late-night cram.
  • Learn opposites together: different from, similar to, superior to, inferior to. Contrasts help memory.
  • For exams, play safe. Where two forms exist, such as different from and different to, choose the one most grammar books accept: different from.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are fixed prepositions in English grammar?
Fixed prepositions are prepositions that always go with a particular adjective, verb or noun, such as afraid of, depend on and solution to. They are also called dependent prepositions or word partners.

Are there rules for fixed prepositions?
There's no single rule that covers them all. They're based on usage, so the best way to learn them is by pairs, families and memory tricks.

How many fixed prepositions should I learn for competitive exams?
There's no official number, but a few hundred common pairs cover most questions. Focus on the ones that repeat in exam practice, and learn about 10 to 13 a day.

What's the difference between fixed prepositions and phrasal verbs?
In a fixed preposition pair, the verb keeps its basic meaning, as in listen to or depend on. In a phrasal verb, the meaning changes, as in look up (search) or give up (quit).

Is different than wrong in exams?
Different than is accepted in some varieties of English, but exam papers and most grammar books treat different from as the correct form. Use different from in exams.

Can I learn fixed prepositions in 30 days?
Yes, if you study a small number of pairs daily, revise on a schedule and practise exam-style questions. Consistency matters more than long sessions.

Final word. Fixed prepositions aren't a talent some people are born with. They are a habit you build, one pair at a time.
