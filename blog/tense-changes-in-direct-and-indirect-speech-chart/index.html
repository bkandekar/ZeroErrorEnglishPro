Tense Changes in Direct and Indirect Speech: Chart with Memory Tricks

Learn the tense changes in direct and indirect speech with a complete chart, 4 memory tricks, exceptions, common mistakes and a 12-question practice set.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
SAME FAMILY, PAST ADDRESS | PAST GOES PERFECT | C-M-W modals | ONE STEP BACK

Read this sentence: She said, "I have finished my work." Now report it. Is it "She said that she has finished her work", "She said that she finished her work", or "She said that she had finished her work"?

Only one of these is the answer an examiner expects, and it's the third. But many students pick the wrong one, not because they're weak, but because the tense chart has too many rows and too many exceptions to hold in the head at once.

Here's the secret that changed my classroom after 22 years of teaching this topic: you don't need to memorise a chart of twenty rows. You need to see three families, one staircase and one small set of modals. Once you see the pattern, the chart rebuilds itself in your mind in under a minute.

In this guide, I'll give you the complete tense-change chart, an example for every row, memory tricks that make it stick, the exceptions, the mistakes examiners love and a practice set with answers. Why does the tense change at all?

When someone says something, they speak from their own moment in time. When you report it later, you speak from a moment further ahead. So whatever was "present" for the speaker has become "past" for you, and the verb moves one step back to show that.

Think of a train station. The speaker is standing at Station A. By the time you report the sentence, you're standing at Station B, one stop ahead. From where you stand, Station A is behind you. That's why is becomes was and will becomes would. The tense changes only when the reporting verb is in the past (said, told, asked). If the reporting verb is in the present or future, the tense stays as it is.

Memory trick 1: Three families, one address

Look at the first three rows of the chart again. Present simple becomes past simple. Present continuous becomes past continuous. Present perfect becomes past perfect. Do you see it? Each tense stays in its family. Only the address changes from present to past.

Memory shortcut: SAME FAMILY, PAST ADDRESS. Simple stays simple, continuous stays continuous, perfect stays perfect. Just move the address from present to past.

That one idea covers six rows of the chart, because the "perfect continuous" tenses follow the same rule: present perfect continuous becomes past perfect continuous.

Memory trick 2: The past simple climbs to perfect

Now look at the past rows. Past simple becomes past perfect. Past continuous becomes past perfect continuous. These two rows break the "same family" rule, because there's no past of the past in the simple or continuous form. So the verb climbs one step up into the perfect form.

Memory shortcut: PAST GOES PERFECT. If it's already past, add "had". Past simple becomes had + V3. Past continuous becomes had been + -ing. And past perfect, already at the top step, doesn't move.

Memory trick 3: The staircase

Picture a staircase going down into the past, with three steps:

StepTenseExample
Step 1 (where the speaker stands)PresentI play
Step 2 (one step down)Past simplehe played
Step 3 (two steps down, the bottom)Past perfecthe had played

Each time you report, you take one step down. A present tense lands on step 2. A past simple, which already stands on step 2, lands on step 3. And a past perfect is already on step 3, the bottom, so there's nowhere left to go. That's why it doesn't change.

Memory shortcut: ONE STEP BACK, and the bottom doesn't move. Same as reversing a car: when you hit the wall, you stop.

Memory trick 4: The modals share a first letter

Modals are where students panic. Here's a trick so simple that you'll smile when you see it.

DirectIndirectClue
cancouldC to C
maymightM to M
willwouldW to W
shallwould (or should in questions of advice)shall steps back to would in statements
mustmust (or had to)often stays
Memory shortcut: C-M-W, same first letter. Can becomes Could. May becomes Might. Will becomes Would. The first letter never changes.

And the modals that are already in the "past" form don't change at all: could, would, should, might, ought to and used to stay as they are.

The 20-second method for any tense question

When a conversion question appears, run this routine before you write a single word:

  1. Check the reporting verb. Is it past (said, told)? If it's present or future, don't change the tense.
  2. Check for a universal truth. A scientific fact or a proverb stays as it is.
  3. Name the tense family. Simple, continuous or perfect? Move it to the past in the same family. If it's already past, make it perfect.
  4. Handle the modal. Use C-M-W: can to could, may to might, will to would.

Four checks, about twenty seconds. After a week of practice, you'll do them without thinking.

When the tense does NOT change

Every good chart has exceptions. These are the ones examiners test most.

  • The reporting verb is in the present or future tense. He says, "I am busy." becomes He says that he is busy. Only the pronoun changes.
  • The statement is a universal truth or a habitual fact. The teacher said, "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius." becomes The teacher said that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.
  • The verb is already past perfect. They said, "We had finished." becomes They said that they had finished.
  • The modal is already in its past form. She said, "I could swim well." becomes She said that she could swim well.

A quick word on a question I hear often: "Can I keep the present tense if the situation is still true?" In everyday English, yes. For example, "She said that she lives in Pune" is acceptable if she still does. But in exam answers, the safe approach is the backshift unless the statement is clearly a universal truth. If your options include both, pick the backshifted one.

A closer look at the past simple

The past simple needs a special mention, because it creates the most doubt. The standard exam rule is: past simple becomes past perfect.

  • He said, "I visited Mumbai." becomes He said that he had visited Mumbai.
  • She said, "I bought a bike last week." becomes She said that she had bought a bike the week before.

You'll occasionally read that the past simple may stay unchanged when the sentence has a clear time word like "in 2010". Modern grammar allows that in some cases. But in competitive exams, follow the chart and use had + V3 unless an option clearly keeps the simple past. When in doubt, climb to the perfect.

Frequently asked questions

Why does "will" become "would" in indirect speech?

Because "will" is the present form of the future, and in reported speech it takes one step back. Its past form is "would". The same logic turns "can" into "could" and "may" into "might".

What happens to the present perfect tense in indirect speech?

The present perfect becomes the past perfect. For example, "I have finished" becomes "he had finished". The present perfect continuous follows the same pattern and becomes the past perfect continuous.

Does the past perfect change in indirect speech?

No. The past perfect is already the furthest step back in the chart, so it stays as it is.

Should "shall" become "should" or "would"?

In statements, "shall" becomes "would". In questions that ask for advice or a suggestion, "shall" may become "should", as in "He asked if he should open the window".

Can I keep the present tense if the statement is still true?

In everyday English, yes. In competitive exams, backshift unless the statement is a universal truth or a habitual fact, because answer keys usually follow the chart.

What is the easiest way to remember the chart?

Use the shortcuts: SAME FAMILY, PAST ADDRESS for the first rows, PAST GOES PERFECT for the past tenses and C-M-W for the modals. Say them aloud for three days, and the chart will stay with you.
