15 Subject-Verb Agreement Mistakes That Cost You Marks in SSC CGL (And How to Fix Them)

Why Examiners Love Testing Subject-Verb Agreement

Why does this one topic show up so often? Because it's the perfect trap. The examiner doesn't need a difficult rule. All they need is a plural noun placed right beside a singular subject, and your ear does the rest.

Think about how we speak. We rarely say a sentence with full grammar in our heads. We hear the last word before the verb and match the verb to it. That works in daily talk, and it fails in exams, where setters deliberately stack the sentence against that habit.

How to Use This List

Before we start, here's the habit that makes every mistake easier to catch. I call it the Cross-Out Test.

  1. Find the verb.
  2. Ask: “Who or what is doing this?” The answer is the subject.
  3. Cross out every phrase that starts with of, in, with, along with, as well as, or sits between commas.
  4. Decide: is the subject one or many?
  5. Match the verb to that, and check the trap list below.

You'll use this method in every section. If you'd like the full speed version for exam day, read my post on the 5-second method to spot agreement errors.

Family 1: The Hidden Subject (Mistakes 1 and 2)

These two mistakes happen when extra words stand between the real subject and the verb. The extra words are decoys, and your job is to ignore them.

Mistake 1: The Subject Hidden Behind an Of-Phrase

Wrong and Right
Wrong: The quality of these mangoes are good.
Correct: The quality of these mangoes is good.

The subject is quality, a singular noun. The phrase “of these mangoes” only describes the quality. It isn't the subject, so it can't decide the verb.

Memory trick
Strike out the of-phrase and read what's left: “The quality is good.” If it sounds right now, your verb was right all along.

Mistake 2: Along With, As Well As and Together With

Wrong and Right
Wrong: The captain, along with his players, are arriving.
Correct: The captain, along with his players, is arriving.

Words like along with, as well as, together with, in addition to and besides do not join subjects. They add extra information. The real subject is still captain, and the verb agrees with it.

Memory trick
Commas are brackets. If the phrase between the commas could be removed and the sentence still works, ignore it when choosing the verb.

Family 2: Singular Words That Feel Plural (Mistakes 3 to 5)

Each, either, neither and one all point to a single item, even when a plural noun follows. These three mistakes catch students who listen to the plural noun instead of the pointer word.

Mistake 3: Each of the... Followed by a Plural Verb

Wrong and Right
Wrong: Each of the boys have a bag.
Correct: Each of the boys has a bag.
Memory trick
Add the word one in your head: each one of the boys has a bag. The hidden ONE makes the singular verb feel natural.

Mistake 4: Either and Neither With a Plural Verb

Wrong and Right
Wrong: Either of the two answers are correct.
Correct: Either of the two answers is correct.

Either means one of the two. Neither means not one of the two. Both talk about one item at a time, so both take a singular verb.

You'll hear “Neither of them are late” in casual speech, and it even appears in some books. In competitive exams, though, it's marked wrong. Stick to the singular verb and you'll never lose a mark here.

Memory trick
Either and neither are twins. Both are hiding the word “one”: either one, neither one.

Mistake 5: One of the Students... Followed by a Plural Verb

Wrong and Right
Wrong: One of the students are absent.
Correct: One of the students is absent.

This is perhaps the most popular trap in all of error spotting. The subject is the word one. The phrase “of the students” only tells you which one.

Memory trick
Cover up “of the students” with your finger. “One ___ absent.” The verb is obviously is.

Family 3: Who, Nor and Number Traps (Mistakes 6, 7, 11 and 12)

These four mistakes depend on a connector or counting word. They're easy to fix once you know the pattern, and easy to miss when you're racing the clock.

Mistake 6: One of Those Who

Wrong and Right
Wrong: He is one of those students who works hard.
Correct: He is one of those students who work hard.

Now the rule flips. Here the verb belongs to who, and who points back to students, which is plural. So the verb must be plural.

Compare: “He is the only one of the students who works hard.” Because only one person works hard, the verb is singular again. The words “the only” change the meaning, and the verb follows the meaning.

Memory trick
“One of those who” means many people share the quality, so go plural. “The only one who” means a single person, so go singular.

Mistake 7: Either...Or and Neither...Nor

Wrong and Right
Wrong: Neither the teacher nor the students was present.
Correct: Neither the teacher nor the students were present.

When two subjects are joined by or, nor, either...or, neither...nor or not only...but also, the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it. Here the nearer subject is students, so the verb is were.

Flip the order and the verb flips too: “Neither the students nor the teacher was present.” Same words, different order, different verb. That's why examiners love this pattern.

Memory trick
Think of the nearest neighbour. The verb only listens to the subject standing next to it.

Mistake 11: A Number of and The Number of

Wrong and Right
Wrong: A number of students is absent.
Correct: A number of students are absent.

A number of means many, so it takes a plural verb. The number of means one count or total, so it takes a singular verb: “The number of applicants is growing.”

Both phrases look almost identical, and one small word, a or the, flips the verb. Examiners know that students skim past small words.

Memory trick
A number = many (plural). The number = one count (singular).

Mistake 12: Many a and More Than One

Wrong and Right
Wrong: Many a student have failed.
Correct: Many a student has failed.

Many a sounds plural because of the word many, but the noun that follows is singular, and the verb follows the noun. It describes one student at a time, many times over.

Memory trick
Many a and more than one are the sneaky singulars. The noun is singular, so the verb is singular.

Family 4: Nouns That Fool Your Ear (Mistakes 8, 9, 10, 14 and 15)

Some nouns look plural but act singular, and some look singular but act plural. These five mistakes come from trusting how the word looks instead of what it means.

Mistake 8: Collective Nouns: Unit or Split

Wrong and Right
Wrong: The committee have submitted its report.
Correct: The committee has submitted its report.

A collective noun names a group as one thing: committee, team, jury, family, staff. If the group acts as one unit, use a singular verb and its. If the members act separately, use a plural verb and their: “The jury are divided in their opinions.”

Also remember that police, cattle, people and poultry are always plural: “The police have arrested the thief.”

Memory trick
UNIT = its, singular. SPLIT = their, plural. When the sentence shows no split, treat the group as a unit.

Mistake 9: Plural-Looking Singular Nouns

Wrong and Right
Wrong: Mathematics are my favourite subject.
Correct: Mathematics is my favourite subject.

Subjects of study such as mathematics, physics, economics and politics end in -s but are singular. So are news, measles and athletics. The -s is a decoy.

Memory trick
If it's a school subject or the word news, the -s is a decoy. Singular verb every time.

Mistake 10: Money, Time and Distance as One Amount

Wrong and Right
Wrong: Ten kilometres are a long distance.
Correct: Ten kilometres is a long distance.

When an amount of money, time or distance is seen as one total, it takes a singular verb. “Fifty rupees is a small amount.” “Two years is enough time.”

Memory trick
Ask yourself: am I thinking of one total? If yes, go singular.

Mistake 14: Pair Nouns: Scissors, Trousers, Spectacles

Wrong and Right
Wrong: The scissors is sharp.
Correct: The scissors are sharp.

Some objects have two parts joined together, and their names are always plural: scissors, trousers, jeans, spectacles, binoculars, pliers. They take plural verbs.

With the phrase a pair of, the verb follows pair: “A pair of scissors is on the table.”

Memory trick
Two blades, two legs, two lenses. Two parts, plural verb.

Mistake 15: The + Adjective

Wrong and Right
Wrong: The poor is always in need.
Correct: The poor are always in need.

When the comes before an adjective such as poor, rich, young, old or injured, it names a whole group of people, and the verb is plural: “The rich are not always happy.”

Memory trick
Read it as “the poor people”. Plural people, plural verb.

Family 5: When the Verb Comes First (Mistake 13)

Mistake 13: There Is and There Are

Wrong and Right
Wrong: There is many reasons for this.
Correct: There are many reasons for this.

In sentences that begin with There or Here, the real subject comes after the verb. Flip the sentence in your head: “Many reasons are there.” Now the subject, reasons, is plural, so the verb is are.

Memory trick
There and here are empty words. Look past them and find the subject that follows.

Case Study: One Question, Five Seconds

Let's put the Cross-Out Test to work on a full exam-style question. This is an SSC CGL exam pattern practice question, written for practice.

Question
The manager along with his two assistants (A) / have decided to (B) / postpone the meeting to next week. (C) / No error (D)
  1. Find the verb: have decided (part B).
  2. Ask who: Who has decided? The manager.
  3. Cross out the noise: “along with his two assistants” is only extra information.
  4. Count: manager = one person, singular.
  5. Check the trap list: along with does not make the subject plural (Mistake 2).

The answer is B. It should be “has decided”. That took about five seconds. Without the method, the plural “assistants” pulls the eye toward “have” and the sentence sounds fine.

5 Expert Tips From 22 Years of Teaching

Tip 1: Read the subject and the verb aloud, nothing else

Say only the two words together: “quality is” or “students were”. If the pair sounds clumsy, you've picked the wrong subject.

Tip 2: Trust the rule, not your ear

Your ear was trained on everyday speech, and exams punish that. When your ear and the rule disagree, go with the rule. It will feel odd for a week, and then it'll feel natural.

Tip 3: Slow down on long sentences

The longer the sentence, the more likely the verb is far from its subject. Long sentences are rarely harder grammatically. They just hide the subject better.

Tip 4: Practise with a timer

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does subject-verb agreement appear so often in SSC CGL error spotting?
Because it lets examiners hide a mistake inside a perfectly normal-looking sentence. A plural noun near the verb makes the wrong verb sound natural, so careless readers miss it.

How do I quickly find the subject of a long sentence?
Strike out phrases that begin with a preposition, and anything between commas. Then ask who or what is doing the action. The answer is the subject.

Is “The committee have decided” ever correct?
In some British writing, yes. In competitive exams, treat a collective noun as singular unless the sentence clearly shows the members acting separately.

Why is “Neither of the answers are correct” marked wrong in exams?
Neither means not one of the two, so it takes a singular verb. Casual speech often uses the plural, but exam answer keys follow the formal rule.

What is the difference between “a number of” and “the number of”?
A number of means many and takes a plural verb. The number of refers to a single total and takes a singular verb.

How many subject-verb agreement sentences should I practise daily?
Ten to twenty is enough if you review every mistake. Quality matters more than quantity, so always write down why a sentence was wrong.

Can I master these 15 mistakes in one week?
Yes, most students can follow the 7-day plan above and see clear improvement. Keep revising your Mistake Notebook after the first week so the rules stay fresh.

Final word. Fifteen mistakes may sound like a lot, but they're only five families, and each family has one simple habit behind it. Cross out the noise, find the real subject, then choose the verb.
