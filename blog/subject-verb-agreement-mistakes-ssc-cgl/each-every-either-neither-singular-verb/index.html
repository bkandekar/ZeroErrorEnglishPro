Each, Every, Either, Neither: Why These Tricky Words Always Take a Singular Verb

Learn why each, every, either and neither take singular verbs. Clear rules, exam traps, solved examples and practice questions for SSC and bank exams.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Each / Every / Either / Neither (+ of + plural noun) = SINGULAR verb.

“Each of the players have received a medal.” Read that sentence again. Does it sound wrong to you? For most students it doesn't, and that is exactly the problem.

Over the years I've checked thousands of error spotting answers, and these four words, each, every, either and neither, cause more silent mistakes than almost any other group. Students know the rule when you ask them directly. But inside a sentence, the plural noun that follows the word makes the plural verb sound natural.

Here's the good news. Once you see how these words work, the mistake becomes almost impossible to make. In this guide you'll get the rule, the reasoning behind it, every special case examiners use, ten exam traps in one table, and a ten-sentence practice set with answers.

The Rule in One Line

The Rule
Each, every, either and neither always take a singular verb. This stays true even when a plural noun follows (each of the boys, neither of the answers).
Memory trick: the Hidden ONE
Add the word one in your head: each one, every one, either one, neither one. “Each one of the players has received a medal” sounds right immediately.

That's the whole rule. Everything else in this post explains why it holds and where examiners hide the traps.

Why These Four Words Feel Plural

These words refer to groups of people or things, so our mind says “plural”. But look at what each word actually does. It picks out one member at a time from the group. Each looks at the players individually. Either chooses one of two roads. Neither rejects both, one by one.

The second reason is sound. In “Each of the players have received a medal”, the word right before the verb is players, and plural nouns pull the verb towards a plural form. Examiners know your ear works this way, and they build the sentence around it.

So the fix is simple: stop listening to the noun beside the verb. Find the real subject, which is the pointer word, and match the verb to that.

Each: One by One

Each means “every one of two or more, taken separately”. It can appear in four patterns, and all four are tested.

  1. Each + singular noun: Each student has a library card.
  2. Each of + plural noun: Each of the students has a library card. The subject is each, so the verb is singular.
  3. Each joined by and: Each boy and each girl was given a prize. Because each comes before both nouns, they're counted one at a time.
  4. Each after the subject: The students each have a library card. Here the subject is students and each is an extra word, so the verb is plural.

Pattern four is the one that surprises people. The rule is simple: if each starts the subject, the verb is singular. If each comes after a plural subject, the verb agrees with that plural subject.

Every: All, But One at a Time

Every works like each, but it never stands alone before “of”. You can't say “every of the boys”. You must say “every one of the boys”, and that again takes a singular verb.

  • Every child needs love.
  • Every one of the answers is correct.
  • Every man, woman and child was rescued.
  • Every student and every teacher was present.

Look at the last two. Even though two or more persons are joined by “and”, the word every before each noun keeps the verb singular. Without every, the verb would be plural: “The student and the teacher were present.”

The compound words everyone, everybody, everything, everywhere follow the same rule: “Everyone is waiting.” “Everything was ready.” The same goes for anyone, someone, nobody and nothing.

Either: One of Two

Either means one of two. It can't be used for three or more items. For those, use any. That's a classic exam trap: “Either of the three options” is wrong, and “Any of the three options” is correct.

  • Either of the roads leads to the city.
  • Either of my parents is coming to the meeting.
  • You can take either book. Either one is fine.

When either is used with or (“Either the manager or the clerks”), a different rule applies, the proximity rule. The verb follows the nearer subject. I've explained that fully in my guide to the proximity rule.

Neither: Not One of Two

Neither is the negative twin of either. It also means “not one of the two”, and it also takes a singular verb. Like either, it's used for two items only. For three or more, use none.

  • Neither of the answers is correct.
  • Neither of them was late.
  • Neither plan is practical.
Exam Note
You will hear “Neither of them are late” in casual speech, and some dictionaries even allow it. Competitive exams, however, follow the formal rule. Stick to the singular verb and you'll never lose a mark.

The Pronoun Partner Trap

Getting the verb right is only half the job. The pronoun that refers back to the subject must also be singular. Examiners often keep the verb correct and hide the error in the pronoun.

WrongCorrect
Each of the girls brought their book.Each of the girls brought her book.
Every boy must carry their ID card.Every boy must carry his ID card.
Neither of the men has finished their work.Neither of the men has finished his work.

When the gender isn't known, formal writing uses “his or her”: “Everyone must bring his or her ID.” Modern English often uses “their”, but for exam practice, match the pronoun to the singular subject.

Special Cases That Confuse Students

Each and every together

“Each and every student has to sign.” Both words are singular, and together they're still singular.

Each of us, either of us, neither of us

These also take a singular verb: “Each of us has a role.” “Neither of us was ready.” The plural pronoun us doesn't change the rule.

Each other

“Each other” is not a subject, so it doesn't control the verb. “The two friends help each other.” The verb agrees with friends.

Everyone vs every one

Everyone (one word) means all people. Every one (two words) points to each item in a group: “Every one of the books is torn.” Both take singular verbs.

How Examiners Disguise the Trap

Examiners rarely write the trap in its plain form. They disguise it in four common ways, and once you know them you'll recognise each at a glance.

  1. A long phrase after the pointer word: Each of the new employees recruited last month has received a laptop. The verb is far from each, so students match it with employees.
  2. A relative clause in the middle: Every student who passed the test was given a certificate. The words who passed make the sentence feel plural, but the subject is still every student.
  3. An inverted order: Neither of the two proposals was accepted by the board. Starting with neither, then a long phrase, then the verb. Stay calm and match the verb to neither.
  4. A split sentence: Either of the two candidates, despite their differences, is likely to win. The phrase between commas contains a plural pronoun, their, but the subject is either.

In all four, the Cross-Out Test works the same way. Remove the phrase, look at the pointer word, and the verb becomes obvious.

The Plural Twins: Both, Few, Several, Many

Here's a contrast that often appears in exams. Words like both, few, several, many and a few are plural pointers. They take plural verbs, just as each, every, either and neither take singular ones.

Singular pointersPlural pointers
Each of the boys has a bag.Both of the boys have bags.
Either of the answers is correct.Several of the answers are correct.
Neither of the books was useful.Few of the books were useful.
Every one of the seats is taken.Many of the seats are taken.

Think of the singular pointers as picking one at a time, and the plural pointers as picking a group. The pointer word decides the verb, and the noun after of never does.

3 Mistakes Students Make While Revising

  1. Learning the rule without the pronoun. Students memorise has and is, then lose marks on his and her. Always check the pronoun when each or every is the subject.
  2. Forgetting the two-item limit. Either and neither are for two. When a question mentions three items, check whether the examiner has swapped in any or none.
  3. Trusting the sentence sound. If a plural verb sounds fine, that's the trap working. Trust the rule even when your ear disagrees.

Fix these three habits and this whole topic becomes a safe source of marks.

Case Study: Spotting the Error in Five Seconds

Here is an SSC CGL exam pattern practice question, written for practice.

Question
Each of the candidates (A) / have submitted (B) / their application form. (C) / No error (D)
  1. Find the verb: have submitted.
  2. Ask who: Who has submitted? Each of the candidates.
  3. Count: each = one at a time, singular.
  4. Check the trap: pointer word each, plural noun after it. Classic trap.

The error is in B: it should be “has submitted”. Notice that part C is also slightly off if the sentence goes on to say “their”. In a real exam only one part is marked, but now you know to check both.

4 Tips From the Classroom

Tip 1: Spot the pointer word

The moment you see each, every, either or neither, circle it. It is almost always the subject, and the verb must be singular.

Tip 2: Cover the plural noun

Cover everything after of with your finger and read the sentence again. “Each has received a medal” sounds right.

Tip 3: Check the pronoun too

If the verb is correct, look at his, her or their. Many questions hide the slip there.

Tip 4: Remember the two-item limit

Either and neither are for two. If you see three items, the answer is likely any or none.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is “Neither of them are late” acceptable?
You'll hear it in casual speech, but exams mark it wrong. Use “Neither of them is late”.

What about “The students each have a book”?
The subject is students, and each comes after it. The verb agrees with students, so it's plural. The singular rule applies when each starts the subject.

Why is it “Every student and every teacher was present”?
Because every comes before each noun, the two persons are counted one at a time. That keeps the verb singular.

Is it “everyone” or “every one”?
Everyone means all people. Every one points to each item in a group, as in “every one of the books”. Both take a singular verb.

Can I use “either” with three options?
No. Either is for two. For three or more, use any: “Any of the three options is fine”.

Do each, every, either and neither always take singular verbs?
Yes, when they start the subject. The only exception is when each comes after a plural subject, as in “The boys each have a bag”.
