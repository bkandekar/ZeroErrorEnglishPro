Why reported speech deserves a complete book

Direct and indirect speech looks like one topic. It's really seven topics wearing one coat: tense changes, pronoun changes, time and place words, reporting verbs, sentence types, punctuation and mixed sentences. Each has its own rules and its own exceptions. A mistake in any one of them costs the whole answer.

And exams don't test the topic in just one form. You may be asked to convert a sentence, choose the correct option, spot the error in a narrated sentence or narrate a short passage. To be ready for all of these, you need three things that a single blog post can't give you: a clear order of learning, a large amount of practice and one place to revise from.

A good book gives you all three. It teaches the rules in sequence, so you never meet a hard idea before you're ready for it. It gives you drills right after each idea, so you test yourself while the idea is fresh. And it gives you one thing to open the week before an exam, instead of twenty browser tabs.

What to rememberMemory shortcut
Order of checksP-T-P: Person, Time, Place.
PronounsS-O-N: first person follows Subject, second person follows Object, third person No change.
Near and far wordsN-F: Near words become Far words.
Yesterday and tomorrowY-B, T-A: yesterday = before, tomorrow = after.
TensesONE STEP BACK, SAME FAMILY, PAST ADDRESS and PAST GOES PERFECT.
ModalsC-M-W: Can to Could, May to Might, Will to Would.
No tense changeFrozen facts don't move. Present stays present.
Reporting verbsMatch the MOOD. PORAT verbs take to + verb. SAD verbs take -ing.
QuestionsIF for yes/no, WH stays, and KILL THE Q.
CommandsCOMMAND = person plus TO.

Quick readiness check: 5 questions

Convert these without looking at anything. This is a quick check, not a test.

  1. She said, "I will come tomorrow."
  2. He said to me, "Where do you live?"
  3. The teacher said, "The sun rises in the east."
  4. The doctor said to me, "Do not eat oily food."
  5. She said, "Alas! I have lost my bag."
Answers:
1) She said that she would come the next day.
2) He asked me where I lived.
3) The teacher said that the sun rises in the east.
4) The doctor advised me not to eat oily food.
5) She exclaimed with sorrow that she had lost her bag.

If you got all five: excellent. Keep revising with the shortcuts, and use the book for extra practice and solved questions.
If you got three or four: you have a good base, with a few gaps. Focused practice is exactly what fills them.
If you got two or fewer: reread this week's free posts, then move to the book for the full system.

How to judge any grammar book (including mine)

Before you spend money on any study material, ask these four questions. I'd want you to ask them about my book too.

  1. Does it explain in simple language? If you need a dictionary to understand the rule, it's the wrong book.
  2. Does it give examples for every idea? A rule without a sentence is just a slogan.
  3. Does it let you practise, with answers? Reading builds understanding. Practice with solutions builds accuracy.
  4. Does it fit your exam? A general English book is different from one built around the patterns your exam uses.

Open the Amazon page, read the description and the sample, and judge against these four questions. That's how a smart student buys study material.
