Direct and Indirect Speech Rules with Examples: A Simple Guide for Competitive Exams

If you hesitated, you're in good company. This one small question trips up many students because direct and indirect speech looks simple until you try to convert a sentence under exam pressure. Then the tense, the pronoun, the time word and the connector all need your attention.

Quick answer: how to convert a statement from direct speech into indirect speech
  1. Remove the quotation marks and use that as the connector.
  2. Change the pronouns according to the speaker and the listener.
  3. If the reporting verb is in the past, usually move the tense one step back.
  4. Change time and place words when the reporting context requires it.
  5. When said to introduces a statement and names a listener, use told.

Example: He said, "I am tired." becomes He said that he was tired.

What are direct and indirect speech?

Direct speech gives the exact words of the speaker, inside quotation marks. Indirect speech (also called reported speech) tells us what the speaker said, without quotation marks and with any necessary changes.

  • Direct: Rahul said, "I like cricket."
  • Indirect: Rahul said that he liked cricket.

Every direct speech sentence has two parts. The first is the reporting clause, which tells us who is speaking (Rahul said). The second is the reported speech, which gives the exact words (I like cricket). When we convert the sentence, the reporting clause usually remains, while the reported speech may change.

Exams test this topic in several forms: converting direct speech into indirect speech or vice versa, choosing the correct option, and spotting errors in a reported sentence. All of them rely on the same basic rules.

The 5-step method (for statements)

Let's convert this sentence together: Meera said to her friend, "I will send you the notes tomorrow."

Step What you do Result
1Identify the reporting verb and reported words.Reporting verb: said to. Reported words: I will send you the notes tomorrow.
2Remove the quotation marks, use that, and change said to into told.Meera told her friend that ...
3Change the pronouns according to the speaker and listener.I becomes she; you becomes her.
4Backshift the tense because the reporting verb is in the past.will send becomes would send.
5Adjust the time expression if the reporting context has changed.tomorrow becomes the next day.

Final answer: Meera told her friend that she would send her the notes the next day.

Practise this routine on a few sentences, and it will become easier. Now let's look at each rule properly.

Rule 1: The reporting verb

For statements, said is used when no listener is mentioned. When the direct sentence uses said to + listener, it is commonly changed to told + listener.

  • He said, "I am ready." becomes He said that he was ready.
  • He said to me, "I am ready." becomes He told me that he was ready.

"He said me that he was ready" is incorrect in standard English, as is "He told that he was ready". Remember: told normally needs a person or listener after it.

Note: Questions, commands, requests and exclamations use different reporting structures. The simple said to → told pattern on this page is for statements.

Rule 2: Pronoun changes

Pronouns change according to who is speaking and who is listening. A useful exam rule is:

  • First person (I, we, my, our) changes according to the subject of the reporting verb.
  • Second person (you, your) changes according to the object of the reporting verb.
  • Third person (he, she, they, it) usually remains unchanged.

Example: Anil said to Sunil, "I will help you."

"I" is first person, so it refers to Anil. "You" is second person, so it refers to Sunil. Result: Anil told Sunil that he would help him.

Memory shortcut: S-O-N. First person follows the Subject. Second person follows the Object. Third person has No change. Always check the meaning of the sentence so that the pronouns remain clear.

Rule 3: Tense changes (one step back)

When the reporting verb is in the past (for example, said or told), the tense in the reported clause commonly moves one step back. This is called backshift. The change is not automatic in every real-life context; the exceptions are explained below.

Direct speech Indirect speech
Present simple (He plays)Past simple (He played)
Present continuous (He is playing)Past continuous (He was playing)
Present perfect (He has played)Past perfect (He had played)
Past simple (He played)Past perfect (He had played)
will / can / maywould / could / might
Memory shortcut: ONE STEP BACK. When backshift is required, move the tense one step into the past: for example, is becomes was, and will becomes would.

Rule 4: Time and place words

Time and place expressions may change when the speaker's original time or place is no longer the same from the reporter's point of view. They do not have to change if the context remains the same.

Memory shortcut: N-F (Near to Far). Near words such as now, here and this often become then, there and that when the reporting point of view changes.

When the tense does NOT change

The one-step-back rule has exceptions. These are especially useful to remember for competitive exams.

1. The reporting verb is in the present or future tense

  • He says, "I am busy." becomes He says that he is busy.
  • She will say, "I need help." becomes She will say that she needs help.

The reporting verb is not in the past, so backshift is normally unnecessary.

2. The reported speech expresses a universal truth

  • The teacher said, "The sun rises in the east." becomes The teacher said that the sun rises in the east.
  • He said, "Honesty is the best policy." becomes He said that honesty is the best policy.

A statement that remains universally true normally stays in the present tense. For a habit or situation that may have changed, consider the context before deciding whether to backshift.

3. The verb is already in the past perfect tense

  • He said, "I had finished the work." becomes He said that he had finished the work.

The past perfect does not normally undergo a further tense change.

4. Certain modal verbs do not change

Would, could, should, might, ought to and used to generally remain unchanged. For example, "She said, 'I could swim well.'" becomes "She said that she could swim well." The modal must may remain unchanged when the obligation still applies; depending on the meaning and context, it can also become had to.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between direct and indirect speech?

Direct speech repeats the exact words of the speaker inside quotation marks. Indirect speech reports the meaning without quotation marks, making any necessary changes to tense, pronouns and time expressions.

Why does the tense change in indirect speech?

When the reporting verb is in the past, the tense in the reported clause commonly moves one step back. This is called backshift. There are exceptions, such as universal truths and reporting verbs in the present tense.

When should I use "said" and when "told"?

Use "said" when no listener is mentioned. When "said to" is followed by a listener in a statement, it is commonly changed to "told": "He told me that..." is correct, while "He said me that..." is not.

Does the tense always change in indirect speech?

No. Backshift is normally unnecessary when the reporting verb is in the present or future, when the statement expresses a universal truth, and when the reported verb is already in the past perfect. Certain modal verbs also remain unchanged.

Is "that" always needed in indirect speech?

For statements, "that" is a common connector. It can sometimes be omitted in everyday English, but including it is a clear and useful approach for exam practice.

How can I remember all the rules?

Use the shortcuts: ONE STEP BACK for tense backshift, S-O-N for pronouns and N-F for time and place expressions. Say them aloud and practise a few sentences every day.
