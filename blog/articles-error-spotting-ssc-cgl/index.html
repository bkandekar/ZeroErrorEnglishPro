Articles Error Spotting in SSC CGL: 10 Common Mistakes and a Quick Checking Method

Spot article errors fast in SSC CGL. Learn 10 common mistakes with A, An, The and zero article, a simple checking method, and practice questions with answers.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Noun type -> singular or plural -> specific or general -> sound of the next word

Why Article Errors Hide in Plain Sight

Error spotting questions split a sentence into four parts and ask you to find the one that's wrong. Article mistakes are especially good at hiding inside that format, for three reasons.

  • They're one letter or one word. "A" versus "an" is a single letter. "The" missing before a superlative is one missing word in a ten-word sentence. Your eye naturally focuses on verbs and bigger phrases, and skips past these.
  • They sound fine out of habit. Casual spoken English drops or adds articles all the time, so a wrong sentence can still sound "normal" to an ear trained on conversation rather than grammar.
  • The correct choice depends on meaning, not on a fixed pattern. "Go to school" and "go to the school" are both grammatically valid. Only the context tells you which one the sentence needs. That kind of judgment call is exactly what a rushed, exam-pressure read skips over.

The fix isn't reading faster. It's reading with a method, so your eye knows exactly what to check instead of hoping the error jumps out.

The 4-Step Checking Method

Use this sequence on every underlined article, or on every part of a sentence when you suspect the error is hiding there.

  1. Noun type. Is the noun singular countable (book, officer), plural (books, officers), or uncountable (advice, furniture)? Uncountable nouns can never take a or an.
  2. Singular or plural. If it's singular countable, it almost always needs some article, a, an or the. A bare singular countable noun ("She bought pen") is nearly always wrong.
  3. Specific or general. Is the sentence talking about one known, particular thing, or about the idea in general? Known and particular means the. General, with a plural or uncountable noun, usually means no article at all.
  4. Sound, for a or an. If you've landed on an indefinite article, say the very next word aloud. Vowel sound, use an. Consonant sound, use a. Spelling is not the test.

Try it: (A) He is / (B) an honest officer / (C) and best / (D) leader in the department.

Check part (C). "Best" is a superlative, and a superlative points to one particular top example, so it needs the. Answer: (C), it should read "and the best leader".

10 Common Mistakes That Cost Marks

These are the mistakes that appear again and again, across fill in the blanks, error spotting and sentence improvement alike. Read each pair aloud. Hearing the difference helps as much as reading it.

Mistake 1: A or An Chosen by Spelling

Incorrect: He works in a European company as a honest employee.

Correct: He works in a European company as an honest employee.

Why: "European" begins with a "y" sound, a consonant sound, so it takes a. "Honest" has a silent h, so it starts with a vowel sound and takes an. Sound decides, not the first letter.

Mistake 2: Missing Article Before a Singular Countable Noun

Incorrect: She is teacher in a government school.

Correct: She is a teacher in a government school.

Why: "Teacher" is a singular countable noun that names her role in a general sense, so it needs the indefinite article a. A bare singular countable noun almost never stands alone.

Mistake 3: The Dropped Before a Superlative or Ordinal

Incorrect: He secured first rank in the state.

Correct: He secured the first rank in the state.

Why: Ordinal numbers like first, second and third point to one specific position, so they take the.

Mistake 4: The Wrongly Added Before Most Proper Nouns

Incorrect: The Ramesh works at the Infosys.

Correct: Ramesh works at Infosys.

Why: Most personal names and company names take no article. The is reserved for specific categories: rivers, oceans, mountain ranges, newspapers and a handful of other fixed groups, not names in general.

Mistake 5: A or An Used With an Uncountable Noun

Incorrect: The manager gave us an important information.

Correct: The manager gave us important information.

Why: Information is uncountable, so it never takes a or an. If you need to count it, use a phrase like "a piece of information". Advice, furniture and luggage follow the same pattern.

Mistake 6: The Added Before a General Plural or Idea

Incorrect: The honesty and the hard work always pay off.

Correct: Honesty and hard work always pay off.

Why: These are abstract ideas discussed in general, not one particular instance of honesty or hard work, so no article is needed.

Mistake 7: Institution Phrases Mixed Up

Incorrect: He was admitted to hospital after he broke the his leg.

Correct: He was admitted to the hospital after he broke his leg.

Why: In British-influenced Indian English, "in hospital" or "to hospital" without the can also be acceptable when it refers to being a patient, but SSC materials generally expect the hospital when a specific hospital or event is meant. The second half shows a separate, very common error: two determiners stacked together. "His" already does the job of an article, so "the his leg" is always wrong.

Mistake 8: Double Determiners

Incorrect: I misplaced the my identity card before the exam.

Correct: I misplaced my identity card before the exam.

Why: Words like my, this, that, these and those already point to a specific noun. Placing an article in front of them is always incorrect, regardless of the sentence's meaning.

Mistake 9: Meals, Languages and Transport Given an Article

Incorrect: He always travels by the train and speaks the Hindi fluently.

Correct: He always travels by train and speaks Hindi fluently.

Why: "By" plus a mode of transport, and the names of languages used in a general sense, both take no article. This is one of the traps that looks harmless because the sentence still sounds fluent when spoken quickly.

Mistake 10: A or An Missed in a "One-Word" Comparison

Incorrect: This problem is a unique one, not an usual one.

Correct: This problem is a unique one, not a usual one.

Why: Both "unique" and "usual" begin with a "y" sound when spoken, a consonant sound, so both take a. Students often assume every vowel letter needs an, and this pair is a classic trap for that habit.

Mistake Patterns at a Glance

Before moving on, scan this summary table. It groups the ten mistakes by the rule they break, which is a faster way to revise than reading each explanation again from scratch.

Rule broken Mistakes involved One-line fix
Sound, not spelling Mistakes 1 and 10 Say the next word aloud before choosing a or an.
Uncountable nouns Mistake 5 Advice, information, furniture never take a or an.
Specific vs general Mistakes 3, 4 and 6 Superlatives and ordinals need the; general ideas and most names don't.
Fixed phrases Mistakes 7 and 9 Institutions, meals, languages and transport follow their own pattern.
Determiner stacking Mistakes 2 and 8 A bare singular noun needs an article; my, this, that already act as one.

How These Errors Appear in Sentence Improvement

Sentence improvement questions give you a full sentence with one part underlined, then ask you to choose the best replacement, or "No improvement" if the sentence is already correct. Article errors show up here in a slightly different disguise: instead of spotting which of four parts is wrong, you're comparing the underlined phrase against four rewritten options.

SSC-style example: She is a European and speaks a excellent French.

The underlined part, "a European", is actually correct because of the "y" sound. The real error is later in the sentence, in "a excellent French", which should be "excellent French" since languages take no article. This is why you should read the whole sentence before judging only the underlined part.

The safest approach is the same four-step method from earlier. Apply it to the underlined phrase first, then quickly scan the rest of the sentence, because SSC sentence improvement questions sometimes place the real trap just outside the underline.

10 SSC-Style Error Spotting Questions

Each sentence below is split into four parts. Identify the part with the error, or mark (D) "No error" if the sentence is correct. Give yourself eight minutes for all ten.

  1. (A) He is an / (B) honest man / (C) and best officer / (D) in this department.
  2. (A) She gave me / (B) a useful advice / (C) about my career / (D) No error.
  3. (A) The Ganga is / (B) longest river / (C) in India / (D) No error.
  4. (A) He travels / (B) to office / (C) by the bus every day / (D) No error.
  5. (A) It was / (B) an one-sided match / (C) from the very start / (D) No error.
  6. (A) I lost / (B) the my pen / (C) during the exam / (D) No error.
  7. (A) The Ramesh works / (B) at a private company / (C) in Pune / (D) No error.
  8. (A) He is / (B) a university professor / (C) and a honest man / (D) No error.
  9. (A) Students should have / (B) good discipline and / (C) an regular habit of reading / (D) No error.
  10. (A) The more you practise / (B) the better you become / (C) at solving error spotting / (D) No error.
Show answers and explanations
  1. (C). Should be "and the best officer". A superlative needs the.
  2. (B). Should be "useful advice". Advice is uncountable and never takes a or an.
  3. (B). Should be "the longest river". A superlative needs the.
  4. (C). Should be "by bus". Transport after "by" takes no article.
  5. (B). Should be "a one-sided match". "One" begins with a "w" sound, a consonant sound.
  6. (B). Should be "my pen". "My" already functions as a determiner, so it cannot take an article too.
  7. (A). Should be "Ramesh works". Personal names take no article.
  8. (C). Should be "an honest man". "Honest" has a silent h and starts with a vowel sound.
  9. (C). Should be "a regular habit". "Regular" begins with a consonant sound.
  10. (D) No error. "The more..., the better..." is a correct comparative pattern.

If you missed more than two, go back to the mistake that matches your error and reread its explanation before moving on. For the underlying rules behind these questions, our complete guide to articles for SSC CGL covers a, an, the and zero article from the ground up.

Quick Revision

Before every error spotting attempt, check for:

  • A missing article before a singular countable noun standing alone.
  • A or an chosen by spelling instead of sound.
  • The missing before a superlative or ordinal number.
  • A or an attached to an uncountable noun such as advice or information.
  • Two determiners stacked together, such as "the my" or "a this".
  • An article added before a general plural, a language, a meal, or "by" plus transport.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How can I spot article errors quickly in SSC CGL?

A: Use the four-step method: check the noun type, whether it's singular or plural, whether it's specific or general, and finally the sound of the next word if you're choosing between a and an. Practising this in order, every time, builds speed faster than random guessing.

Q: What are the most common article errors in competitive exams?

A: The ten covered in this guide come up repeatedly: choosing a or an by spelling, missing articles before singular nouns, dropping the before superlatives, adding the before proper nouns, using a or an with uncountable nouns, adding the before general ideas, mixing up institution phrases, stacking two determiners, adding articles to meals and transport phrases, and missing the "y" sound in words like unique and usual.

Q: How is a or an error spotting different from other article questions?

A: In error spotting, the wrong article is buried inside a full sentence divided into parts, so you must first locate where the sound rule applies before you can judge whether it was applied correctly.

Q: Why do I still make article mistakes even after learning the rules?

A: Most students learn the rules in isolation but never practise spotting them inside full sentences under time pressure. The fix is deliberate error spotting practice, not re-reading the rules alone.

Q: Are uncountable nouns a common source of article errors?

A: Yes. Words like advice, information, furniture and luggage are uncountable and never take a or an, yet they sound like they should because they're often mistaken for ordinary countable nouns.

Q: Do proper nouns ever take "the"?

A: Most personal names, city names and country names take no article. The applies to specific categories instead, such as rivers, oceans, mountain ranges and newspapers, not to proper nouns generally.
