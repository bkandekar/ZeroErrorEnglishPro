Punctuation Error Spotting for SSC CGL and Bank Exams: Tricks, Examples and Practice

Learn 10 common punctuation errors asked in SSC CGL and bank exams, a CASE scan method, memory tricks and 10 practice questions with answers.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Check every CASE: Commas, Apostrophes, Semicolons-colons, Extras

Read this error spotting question and decide in ten seconds: (A) The manager was angry, / (B) the report had been submitted / (C) three days late. / (D) No error.

Most students pick (D). The sentence reads smoothly, and nothing looks wrong. But there’s an error hiding in part (A), and it has a name: the comma splice. Two complete sentences are joined by a comma and nothing else.

That’s the nature of punctuation questions in SSC CGL and bank exams. They don’t shout. The sentence sounds natural, the words are all correct, and one tiny mark quietly breaks the rule. Students who read for meaning miss it. Students who scan for punctuation catch it.

How punctuation is tested in exams

Punctuation rarely appears as a question labelled “punctuation.” It hides inside three familiar formats, and knowing them tells you where to look.

1. Error spotting. A sentence is split into parts (A), (B), (C), and (D) is “No error.” The punctuation mark sits at the end of one part, so it’s easy to read past.

2. Sentence improvement. One part is underlined, and you choose the best replacement. A semicolon, a comma or an apostrophe often separates the right option from the three wrong ones.

3. Cloze and fill in the blanks. Several blanks sit in a passage, and one may ask you to choose a punctuation mark or a form such as its or it’s.

A note on the examples in this post: every sentence is an original practice sentence on a common exam pattern. None of them is copied from a particular year’s paper, and I haven’t attached any year to them. The patterns are what repeat, and the patterns are what you should learn.

The CASE scan: one memory trick for every question

When the clock is running, you can’t recall twenty rules. You need one routine that tells your eyes where to go. Here it is.

“Check every CASE.”

C = Commas (splices, missing pairs, commas that split subject and verb)
A = Apostrophes (its/it’s, plural vs possessive, contractions)
S = Semicolons and colons (right job, right place)
E = Extras: end marks, quotation marks, hyphens and dashes

Run the four letters in order on every sentence, and say them silently. The routine takes about eight seconds, and it stops you from reading for meaning, which is exactly how these errors survive. 10 punctuation error patterns with examples

Pattern 1 (C): The comma splice

✗ The manager was angry, the report was late.
✓ The manager was angry because the report was late. (or a semicolon, or a full stop)

Two complete sentences with only a comma between them. Memory trick: “Count the subjects.” If each side has its own subject and verb, a comma alone can’t join them.

Pattern 2 (C): A comma between subject and verb

✗ The tallest boy in our class, won the gold medal.
✓ The tallest boy in our class won the gold medal.

Students put a comma where they breathe, usually after a long subject. The subject and its verb stay together, however long the subject is. Memory trick: “Never split a married couple.” The subject and the verb are married.

Pattern 3 (A): Its and it’s

✗ The company announced that it’s new policy will start in January.
✓ The company announced that its new policy will start in January.

It’s always means “it is” or “it has.” Its shows ownership. Memory trick: “Its is like his and hers, and his and hers have no apostrophe.” Another test: say “it is” in the gap. If it fits, use it’s.

Pattern 4 (A): The apostrophe in a plain plural

✗ All the student’s must submit their forms today.
✓ All the students must submit their forms today.

An apostrophe never makes a plural. Plurals just take an s. Memory trick: “The apostrophe belongs to an owner, not to a crowd.” Ask, “Does anything belong to this noun?” If not, no apostrophe.

Pattern 5 (A): Wrong possessive form

✗ The childrens’ park is closed.
✓ The children’s park is closed.
✓ The students’ notice board is damaged.

Write the owner first, then add the apostrophe, then the s. Children is already plural without an s, so it becomes children’s. Students is plural with an s, so it becomes students’. Memory trick: “Owner first, apostrophe next, s last, unless the owner already ends in s.”

Pattern 6 (S): A colon after a verb

✗ The ingredients are: flour, sugar and butter.
✓ The ingredients are flour, sugar and butter.
✓ She bought three things: flour, sugar and butter.

A colon must follow a complete sentence. “The ingredients are” isn’t complete, so no colon belongs there. Memory trick: “Full sentence, then colon.” If the words before the colon can’t stand alone, delete it.

Pattern 7 (S): A semicolon after a dependent clause

✗ Although it was raining; the match continued as planned.
✓ Although it was raining, the match continued as planned.
✓ It was raining; the match continued as planned.

A semicolon joins two complete sentences, never a dependent clause and a main clause. Memory trick: “Semicolon = two best friends of equal strength.” If one half starts with although, because, if or when, it’s not an equal friend, so use a comma.

Pattern 8 (E): A question mark after an indirect question

✗ She asked me where I was going?
✓ She asked me where I was going.

A question mark belongs to a question that someone is really asking. When you report the question, it becomes a statement and takes a full stop. Memory trick: “Asking takes a question mark. Telling about asking takes a full stop.”

Pattern 9 (E): The question mark and the quotation

✗ “Where are you going,” my mother asked as I reached the door.
✓ “Where are you going?” my mother asked as I reached the door.

When the quoted words are a question, the question mark replaces the comma inside the quotation marks. Memory trick: “The quote’s own mark stays inside.” This is also where the direct and indirect speech rules help, because they show how a question changes when you report it.

Pattern 10 (E): The missing hyphen

✗ She is a well known author.
✓ She is a well-known author.
✓ The author is well known.

Two words that work together as one adjective before a noun take a hyphen. After the noun, the hyphen usually goes away. Memory trick: “Before the noun, glue them. After the noun, let them go.”

The 5-step error spotting method

The CASE scan tells you what to check. This five-step routine tells you how to run the question from start to finish.

Step 1: Read the sentence once for meaning. Don’t correct anything yet. Just understand it.

Step 2: Run CASE on each part. Commas, Apostrophes, Semicolons and colons, then Extras. Put your finger on each mark as you check.

Step 3: Name the pattern. If something feels wrong, say which of the ten patterns it matches. If you can’t name it, it’s probably not an error.

Step 4: Test your fix. Rewrite the part in your head with the correction. Does it now follow the rule, and does it still mean the same thing?

Step 5: Only then choose “No error.” Treat it as a conclusion, never a default. If all four CASE checks pass, “No error” is the answer, and it’s a perfectly good one.

A 20-second time plan

Error spotting rewards speed only after it rewards accuracy. Here’s a plan that works for most students, and you can adjust the seconds to suit your pace.

0 to 5 seconds: read the sentence once.

5 to 13 seconds: run CASE on every part.

13 to 18 seconds: name the pattern and test your fix.

18 to 20 seconds: mark the answer and move on. If you’re stuck, skip it and return.

Don’t spend a minute on one question. A skipped question that you revisit with fresh eyes is often solved in five seconds. A pattern I see in almost every batch

Here’s something that repeats year after year in my classes. A student can explain the semicolon rule perfectly on the board, then picks “No error” on a sentence with a semicolon after “although.” The knowledge is there. The scan isn’t.

The fix is a habit, not more theory. Take ten sentences from any editorial, circle every punctuation mark, and name which CASE letter each one belongs to. Ten sentences a day for two weeks trains your eyes to land on the mark before your brain starts reading for meaning. That’s the whole skill.

Expert tips for exam day

Read the punctuation, not just the words. Your eyes should slow down at every comma, apostrophe, semicolon and colon.

Trust the rule over your ear. “It sounds fine” is the reason most punctuation errors survive.

Look at the end of each part. In error spotting, the mark that causes the error usually sits at the boundary between two parts.

Don’t over-correct. A sentence with correct punctuation is a valid answer, so “No error” appears often enough to deserve respect.

Keep an error log. Write down every pattern you miss, and revise that list for a week.

Practice set: 10 error spotting questions

Find the part with the error, or choose (D) if there’s none. These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. The manager was angry, (A) / the report had been submitted (B) / three days late. (C) / No error (D)
  2. The company announced (A) / that it’s new policy (B) / will start in January. (C) / No error (D)
  3. All the student’s (A) / must submit (B) / their forms today. (C) / No error (D)
  4. The ingredients are: (A) / flour, sugar, (B) / and butter. (C) / No error (D)
  5. Although it was raining; (A) / the match (B) / continued as planned. (C) / No error (D)
  6. She asked me (A) / where I was (B) / going? (C) / No error (D)
  7. “Where are you going,” (A) / my mother asked (B) / as I reached the door. (C) / No error (D)
  8. The tallest boy in our class, (A) / won (B) / the gold medal. (C) / No error (D)
  9. She is a well known (A) / author and a (B) / respected teacher. (C) / No error (D)
  10. My cousin, who lives in Mumbai, (A) / is preparing (B) / for the bank exam. (C) / No error (D)

Answers

1. (A): comma splice. Use “The manager was angry because the report was submitted three days late,” or a semicolon after “angry.”

2. (B): “it’s” should be “its” (ownership).

3. (A): “student’s” should be “students.” The apostrophe belongs to an owner, not a crowd.

4. (A): remove the colon. “The ingredients are” isn’t a complete sentence.

5. (A): replace the semicolon with a comma. A semicolon can’t follow a dependent clause.

6. (C): replace the question mark with a full stop. It’s an indirect question.

7. (A): replace the comma with a question mark: “Where are you going?” The quote’s own mark stays inside.

8. (A): remove the comma after “class.” Never split the subject and its verb.

9. (A): “well known” should be “well-known,” because it comes before the noun.

10. (D): no error. The extra information “who lives in Mumbai” is correctly set off by a pair of commas.

If you scored 9 or 10, you’re ready for harder questions. If you scored 6 to 8, revise the pattern you missed. If you scored below 6, go back to the CASE scan and the cheat sheet, and retake the set after two days. Frequently asked questions

How many punctuation questions come in SSC CGL and bank exams?

There’s no fixed number, because punctuation hides inside error spotting, sentence improvement and cloze questions rather than appearing as a separate section. Check the latest official notification for your exam pattern, and prepare punctuation as part of every error-based question.

Which punctuation mark is tested most often?

Commas and apostrophes are the usual suspects, because the rules around them are easy to confuse. Semicolons and colons come next, and they’re often placed where a comma belongs.

Should I choose “No error” if nothing looks wrong?

Only after you’ve run the CASE scan on every part. “No error” is a conclusion, not a default.

Is the Oxford comma tested?

Rarely as the only error. Examiners prefer clear rules such as comma splices and wrong apostrophes. If you do see it, consistency and clarity decide the answer.

How long does it take to improve at punctuation error spotting?

Most students see a clear change within two weeks of ten sentences a day with the CASE scan, plus an error log for the patterns they miss.
