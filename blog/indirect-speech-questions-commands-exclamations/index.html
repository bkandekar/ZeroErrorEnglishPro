Indirect Speech of Questions, Commands and Exclamations: Rules, Examples and Tricks

Learn indirect speech of questions, commands and exclamations with simple rules, memory tricks, 12 examples, common mistakes and a practice set with answers.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
IF for yes/no, WH stays | KILL THE Q | COMMAND = person plus TO | what a, how = very

Read this sentence: He said to me, "Where do you live?" Now report it. Most students write one of these: "He asked me where do I live" or "He asked me where did I live." Both look natural, and both are wrong.

The correct answer is: He asked me where I lived.

Questions, commands and exclamations are the three sentence types where indirect speech changes shape completely. A question stops being a question. A command turns into an infinitive. An exclamation loses its exclamation mark and gains a feeling word. Examiners know that these are the places where students lose marks, so they test them in almost every paper.

In this guide, I'll give you a clear method for each type, memory tricks that make the rules stick, twelve worked examples, the mistakes examiners love and a practice set with answers. If you haven't read the earlier posts, start with the basics in direct and indirect speech rules with examples. The examples here are original sentences on common exam patterns, not questions from a particular year's paper.

Part 1: Questions in indirect speech

A question in direct speech asks something. In indirect speech, we only report that a question was asked, so the sentence becomes a statement in disguise. That one idea explains every rule below.

Two kinds of questions

  • Yes/no questions start with an auxiliary or a modal (do, does, did, is, are, was, have, can, will). We join them with if or whether.
  • WH-questions start with a question word (what, where, when, why, who, how). We keep the same question word as the connector.

Yes/no questions

Direct speechIndirect speech
He said to me, "Are you ready?"He asked me if I was ready.
She said to him, "Do you like tea?"She asked him if he liked tea.
Ravi said to Sunil, "Have you finished the work?"Ravi asked Sunil if he had finished the work.
The teacher said to the boy, "Can you swim?"The teacher asked the boy if he could swim.
He said to her, "Will you come tomorrow?"He asked her if she would come the next day.

WH-questions

Direct speechIndirect speech
He said to me, "Where do you live?"He asked me where I lived.
She said to him, "What is your name?"She asked him what his name was.
The teacher said to the boy, "Why did you leave?"The teacher asked the boy why he had left.
Ravi said to Sunil, "When will you come?"Ravi asked Sunil when he would come.
He said, "Who broke the glass?"He asked who had broken the glass.
Memory shortcut: IF for yes/no, WH stays. A yes/no question takes if or whether. A WH-question keeps its own question word. And you never add "that".

The five steps to convert any question

  1. Change said to into asked (or enquired).
  2. Add if/whether for a yes/no question, or keep the WH-word.
  3. Write the rest in statement order: subject first, then verb.
  4. Remove do, does and did, and adjust the verb to match.
  5. Remove the question mark, and apply the usual changes for tense, pronouns, time and place.
Memory shortcut: KILL THE Q. No question mark, no do/does/did, and subject before verb. The question has to die so that the statement can live.

If or whether?

Both are correct for yes/no questions. But whether is better when the question has an "or": She said to him, "Do you want tea or coffee?" becomes She asked him whether he wanted tea or coffee.

Part 2: Commands, requests and advice

A command, a request or a piece of advice is an imperative sentence. In indirect speech, we choose a reporting verb that matches the force of the sentence, and the command becomes an infinitive (to + verb). If you want the full list of verbs, see my post on the reporting verbs list.

The pattern

Reporting verb + person + to + verb

Direct speechIndirect speech
The captain said to the soldiers, "Stand in a line."The captain ordered the soldiers to stand in a line.
He said to me, "Please wait here."He requested me to wait there.
The doctor said to the patient, "Take this medicine."The doctor advised the patient to take that medicine.
She said to her brother, "Do not shout."She told her brother not to shout.
The teacher said to the students, "Always speak the truth."The teacher advised the students to always speak the truth.

Three small rules to remember

  • No "that" and no tense change. The infinitive doesn't step back, because there's no tense to move.
  • Negative commands. "Do not" becomes "not to". For example, "Do not run" becomes "told us not to run".
  • "Please" and "kindly" turn the verb into "requested". If the sentence is a polite request, don't use "ordered".
Memory shortcut: COMMAND = person plus TO. A command becomes person + to + verb, and a negative one becomes person + not to + verb. There is no "that" and no tense.

Commands that begin with "Let"

When a command starts with "Let" and is about a third person, we use "should be allowed to": The captain said, "Let him go." becomes The captain ordered that he should be allowed to go. When "Let us" is a suggestion, we use suggested that we/they should, as in my reporting verbs post.

Part 3: Exclamations and wishes

An exclamation shows a feeling, such as joy, sorrow, surprise or disgust. In indirect speech, the exclamation mark and the interjection disappear, and the feeling moves into the reporting verb: exclaimed with joy, sorrow, surprise, wonder or disgust.

Interjection in direct speechReporting verb in indirect speech
Hurrah!exclaimed with joy
Alas!exclaimed with sorrow
Wow! / Oh!exclaimed with surprise or wonder
Ugh!exclaimed with disgust
Bravo! / Well done!applauded

"What" and "how" become "very"

Exclamations often start with "What a..." or "How...". In indirect speech, we turn the sentence into a statement and replace "what a" and "how" with very.

  • He said, "What a beautiful garden this is!" becomes He exclaimed with delight that it was a very beautiful garden.
  • She said, "How clever he is!" becomes She exclaimed with admiration that he was very clever.
Memory shortcut: FEEL the interjection, VERY the rest. The interjection tells you the feeling for the reporting verb. "What a" and "how" always become "very".

Wishes and prayers

A sentence that begins with "May" expresses a wish or a prayer. We report it with wished or prayed, followed by "that" and might.

  • The mother said to the child, "May God bless you!" becomes The mother prayed that God might bless the child.
  • He said to me, "May you succeed!" becomes He wished that I might succeed.

Sentences that mix types

Real exam questions often contain two sentences of different types. Treat each part by its own rule, and join them with "and". Example: He said, "I am late. Why are you waiting?" becomes He said that he was late and asked why I was waiting. The first part is a statement (that + tense change). The second part is a question (asked + WH-word + statement order).

For the tense and pronoun parts, if you need a refresher, see my guides on the tense changes chart and on pronouns, time and place words.

Frequently asked questions

Do I use "that" in indirect questions?

No. A question is joined with "if" or "whether" for yes/no questions, and with the same WH-word for other questions. "He asked me that where I lived" is wrong.

Should I use "if" or "whether"?

Both are correct for yes/no questions. Use "whether" when the question has an "or", as in "whether he wanted tea or coffee".

Why do we remove do, does and did in indirect questions?

Because the indirect question is now a statement, and statements don't use these helping verbs. The verb takes the correct tense instead: "Where do you live?" becomes "where I lived".

How do I report a request with "please"?

Use "requested" (or "begged" for a very strong request) followed by person + to + verb. For example, "Please help me" becomes "He requested me to help him".

How do I report "What a beautiful picture!"?

Choose a reporting verb with the feeling (exclaimed with delight or wonder), then write a statement with "very" in place of "what a": "He exclaimed with delight that it was a very beautiful picture".

Can I use "said" for a question?

No. In indirect speech, "said to" before a question must become "asked" (or "enquired"). Using "said" would hide the fact that a question was asked.
