Fixed Prepositions: 25 High-Frequency Verbs That Reject Intuition

Master 25 fixed prepositions after verbs, like depend on, insist on and discuss, with memory tricks, examples and practice for SSC, bank and UPSC exams.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
ON/OF lean on it | TO parcel to an address | FOR chase it | IN/FROM/WITH/AT mixed team | NONE the naked five

Read this sentence: “We discussed about the plan.” Does it sound right? To most of us it does, and that’s exactly the problem. It’s wrong, and it has cost students marks in error spotting for years.

Fixed prepositions are the one grammar area where your intuition works against you. You can’t reason your way to “depend on” or “differ from.” The English language simply decided, and you have to know. The good news is that exams don’t test hundreds of these. A small set of high-frequency verbs shows up again and again, and in this guide you’ll learn 25 of them, sorted into five groups with a memory trick for each.

Why intuition fails with prepositions

There are two reasons, and both are fixable once you see them.

Reason 1: we translate. When a Hindi or Marathi sentence says “is baare mein baat karna,” our brain adds “about” and produces “discuss about.” But English built “discuss” to carry that meaning on its own. The preposition is already inside the verb.

Reason 2: we trust synonyms. “Talk” takes “about,” so we assume “discuss” does too. “Answer” takes nothing, but “reply” takes “to.” Two verbs with the same meaning can behave differently, and no rule predicts which.

So stop looking for logic. Treat each verb and its preposition as a single block, the way you’d learn a phone number. “Depend on” is one unit. “Listen to” is one unit. Once you store them that way, your intuition stops interfering.

Hindi and Marathi translation traps

Most of the errors in this guide come from translating word by word. Here are five patterns I see again and again. Notice how the “natural” English version is the wrong one.

Do you see the pattern? When your own language uses a small joining word, you carry it into English. When English hides the preposition inside the verb, you add one that isn’t needed. Neither habit is a flaw in your intelligence. It’s just how languages travel, and the five-box map is the antidote.

The 5-box memory map

Twenty-five verbs sound heavy until you drop them into five boxes. Each box has one picture to remember.

Box 1: ON / OF = lean on it, made of it
Box 2: TO = a parcel travelling to an address
Box 3: FOR = chasing something you want
Box 4: IN / FROM / WITH / AT = mixed team, one picture each
Box 5: NONE = the “naked five”, no preposition at all

Before you pick a preposition in the exam, name the box first. That one step stops most guessing. My Prepositions ebook uses the same box-by-box approach, with more verbs and plenty of practice.

The 25 verbs in five groups

Box 1: ON and OF (5 verbs)

1. depend on ✓ Your result depends on your daily practice. ✗ depends of / depends from
2. insist on ✓ She insisted on paying the bill. ✗ insisted to pay
3. consist of ✓ The committee consists of nine members. ✗ consists from / is consisted of
4. approve of ✓ My father doesn’t approve of late-night study. ✗ approve for
5. congratulate on ✓ I congratulated her on her selection. ✗ congratulate for

Memory trick: “ON is for leaning, OF is for parts and opinions.” You lean on a person (depend, insist, and congratulate someone on an achievement). A thing is made of parts, and you have an opinion of something (consist of, approve of). Note that “consist” never takes the passive. Also, “approve of” is about opinions, but when an authority sanctions something you write “approve the proposal” with no preposition.

Box 2: TO (5 verbs)

6. listen to ✓ Listen to the instructions carefully. ✗ listen the instructions
7. reply to ✓ He replied to my email within an hour. ✗ replied my email
8. object to ✓ Many parents object to extra fees. ✗ object on / object for
9. belong to ✓ This notebook belongs to Meena. ✗ is belonging to
10. prefer … to ✓ I prefer tea to coffee. ✗ prefer tea than coffee

Memory trick: “A parcel travels TO an address.” Words, sounds, ownership and preference all travel toward a target. “To” here is a preposition, so a verb after it takes -ing: “She objects to being interrupted.” Also, “answer” takes no preposition at all (“He answered my email”), which is a classic trap beside “reply to.”

Box 3: FOR (5 verbs)

11. wait for ✓ We waited for the bus for an hour. ✗ waited the bus
12. apply for ✓ She applied for the post of clerk. ✗ applied the post
13. search for ✓ The police searched for the missing child. ✗ searched the missing child
14. ask for ✓ He asked for a glass of water. ✗ asked a glass of water
15. thank … for ✓ I thanked him for his help. ✗ thanked to him

Memory trick: “Wise Aspirants Search, Ask, Thank, Wait.” The first letters (W, A, S, A, T) cover Wait, Apply, Search, Ask, Thank. Picture yourself chasing something you want, and the chase always takes FOR. One careful point: you search a place for a thing (“searched the room for his keys”), but you search for the thing itself when the place isn’t mentioned.

Box 4: IN, FROM, WITH and AT (5 verbs)

16. participate in ✓ Over 200 students participated in the quiz. ✗ participated on / participated the quiz
17. succeed in ✓ She succeeded in clearing SSC CGL. ✗ succeeded to clear
18. differ from ✓ British spelling differs from American spelling. ✗ differs than
19. agree with / to ✓ I agree with you. The board agreed to the plan. ✗ I agree to you.
20. laugh at ✓ Don’t laugh at his accent. ✗ laugh on his accent

Memory trick: “IN is inside an event.” You take part in a quiz and succeed in an exam because you’re inside the process. “Differ FROM” keeps things at a distance, and you laugh at someone the way you point a finger AT them. For “agree,” remember: with people, to plans.

Box 5: NONE, the naked five

These verbs take no preposition. Adding one is the most common error of all.

21. discuss ✓ We discussed the plan. ✗ discussed about the plan
22. mention ✓ She mentioned the deadline. ✗ mentioned about the deadline
23. marry ✓ He married a doctor. ✗ married with a doctor
24. resemble ✓ She resembles her mother. ✗ resembles to her mother
25. reach ✓ We reached Pune at noon. ✗ reached to Pune

Memory trick: “Dear Mom Makes Rice Rolls.” D-M-M-R-R gives you Discuss, Mention, Marry, Resemble, Reach. Think of them as naked verbs: they’re already complete, so don’t dress them in a preposition. The same group includes “enter,” “attend,” “approach” and “contact.” With “marry,” the preposition appears only in “get married to someone,” and with “reach,” you switch to “arrive at a place” or “arrive in a city.”

Verbs with two correct prepositions

Some verbs change meaning when the preposition changes. Examiners love these, because intuition can’t help you.

agree: with a person or opinion, to a proposal, on a matter. “We agreed on a date.”

apply: for a job, to an organisation. “She applied to three banks for a clerk’s post.”

apologise: to a person, for a mistake. “I apologised to him for the delay.”

differ: from means “is unlike,” and with means “disagrees with.” “I differ with you on this.”

The trick is to ask yourself what comes next. A person or a thing? An action or a reason? Let the object choose the preposition.

Noun and adjective forms keep the same bond

Here’s a useful bonus. When a verb turns into a noun or adjective, the preposition often stays attached, so learning one form helps you with the others.

depend on → dependent on, independent of

differ from → different from

participate in → participation in

succeed in → success in

approve of → approval of

insist on → insistence on

reply to → a reply to

Two exceptions to remember. The noun “discussion” can take “about” or “of,” even though the verb never does. And “independent” takes “of,” not “from,” which examiners enjoy testing. So “She is dependent on her parents, but he is independent of his.”

5 common mistakes in exams

1. Adding “about” to the naked five. ✗ He mentioned about the delay. ✓ He mentioned the delay.

2. “Prefer than.” ✗ I prefer walking than driving. ✓ I prefer walking to driving.

3. “Congratulate for.” ✗ I congratulate you for your success. ✓ I congratulate you on your success.

4. Skipping “to” after “listen.” ✗ She listens the radio daily. ✓ She listens to the radio daily.

5. “Marry with.” ✗ She married with an engineer. ✓ She married an engineer.

Common exam pattern: error spotting sentences where all four parts look smooth, but one carries a wrong preposition after a high-frequency verb. If you notice a verb from this list, check its box immediately before reading further.

The 5-step preposition check

Step 1: Spot the verb. Underline it and name its box.

Step 2: Test the naked five. If it’s discuss, mention, marry, resemble or reach, delete any preposition.

Step 3: Match the box. ON/OF, TO, FOR, or the mixed team.

Step 4: Check the object. A person or a plan? That decides “agree with” or “agree to.”

Step 5: Check what follows. After a preposition, use -ing: “insist on going,” “succeed in passing,” “object to being late.”

That fifth step catches the “insisted to go” error, and it’s worth ten seconds in the paper.

A pattern teachers see every year

Students often learn these verbs in a list, recognise them in a list, and still get them wrong in a passage. The reason is that list-learning trains recognition, not retrieval. In a real sentence, the verb arrives with all its surrounding words, and your brain falls back on translation.

The fix is small. Pick five verbs a day, write one sentence for each from your own life, and say each sentence aloud. “My brother depends on me for notes.” “I waited for the train.” Your own sentences stick far better than textbook ones, and in five days you’ve covered all 25.

The 5-day revision plan

You don’t need a month. Spend about twenty minutes a day, one box at a time.

Day 1: Box 1 (ON/OF). Write five sentences with depend, insist, consist, approve and congratulate.

Day 2: Box 2 (TO). Write five sentences, and add one with an -ing form after “object to.”

Day 3: Box 3 (FOR). Use “Wise Aspirants Search, Ask, Thank, Wait” and write five sentences.

Day 4: Box 4 (IN/FROM/WITH/AT). Write sentences for each verb, including “agree with” and “agree to.”

Day 5: Box 5 (NONE). Recite “Dear Mom Makes Rice Rolls,” then write five sentences with no preposition.

On day six, test yourself with the practice set below, and keep a list of every verb you got wrong. Revisit that list for a week. Most learners find that only three or four verbs keep fooling them, and those few deserve extra attention.

Expert tips for exam day

Learn in chunks, not words. “Depend on,” never “depend” alone.

Trust the box, not the sound. “It sounds right” is how “discuss about” survives.

Watch for noun forms. “Discussion about” is fine, but the verb “discuss about” is not.

Revise mistakes in pairs. Write the wrong and right versions side by side: reply to / answer.

Keep a personal error list. Most students repeat the same five mistakes. Find yours and fix them.

Frequently asked questions

Is “discuss about” ever correct?

No. As a verb, “discuss” takes no preposition. The noun is different: “a discussion about the budget” is correct.

Can I say both “depend on” and “depend upon”?

Yes. “Upon” is more formal, and both are accepted. In exams, “on” is the usual answer option.

Why is it “prefer to” and not “prefer than”?

English fixed the pattern as “prefer X to Y.” “Than” belongs to the comparative structure “would rather walk than ride.”

How many fixed prepositions should I learn for SSC and bank exams?

Start with the 25 high-frequency verbs in this guide, then add more by category. Depth beats breadth: knowing these cold is worth more than half-knowing 200.

What’s the fastest way to remember them?

Use the five boxes, write one personal sentence per verb, and say it aloud. Five verbs a day finishes the list in five days.
