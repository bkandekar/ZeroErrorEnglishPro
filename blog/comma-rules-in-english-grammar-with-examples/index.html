Comma Rules in English Grammar: 8 Easy Rules with Examples and a Memory Trick

By Balu Kandekar · Published 2026-10-20 · 13 min read

“Let’s eat, grandma.” Or: “Let’s eat grandma.” Same words, one tiny mark, and a very different dinner. 😄

That joke has been around for years because it’s true. A comma isn’t decoration. It changes meaning, and in a competitive exam it changes your score. SSC CGL, IBPS PO, UPSC and State PSC papers keep returning to commas in error spotting, sentence improvement and cloze passages, and most students lose those marks for one simple reason: they place commas by feeling instead of by rule.

Here’s the good news. English has only eight situations where a comma really matters. Learn those eight, add a couple of memory shortcuts, and you’ll stop guessing. That’s exactly what this guide does, with examples you can test yourself on.

What a comma actually does

A comma does two jobs. It tells the reader where to take a short breath, and it tells the reader which words belong together. That second job is the one examiners care about, because grouping decides meaning.

Try the pause test. Read your sentence aloud at normal speed. If your voice naturally dips or pauses at a spot, a comma might belong there. If it doesn’t, leave it out. The test isn’t perfect (some commas are pure grammar, and we’ll meet those below), but it fixes about half of the mistakes I see in student answer sheets.

Remember this line, because we’ll come back to it: a comma is a pause, not a stop. A stop is a full stop. A pause lets the sentence keep walking.

The memory trick: one sentence for all 8 rules

Eight rules sound like a lot until you hang them on one sentence. Read this slowly and screenshot it for your revision folder.

“Smart Candidates Insert Extra Addresses, Tags, Adjectives, Dates.”

Smart = Series (lists)
Candidates = Compound sentences (FANBOYS)
Insert = Introductory words and clauses
Extra = Extra information (non-restrictive)
Addresses = Addressing a person
Tags = Tag questions
Adjectives = Adjectives of equal rank
Dates = Dates, places and direct speech

Before you write a comma in an exam, ask yourself: “Which letter am I using?” If you can’t name one, the comma probably doesn’t belong. This single habit removes most unnecessary commas, and unnecessary commas are just as wrong as missing ones.

The 8 comma rules, with examples

Rule 1 (S): Commas in a series

When you list three or more items, separate them with commas. This works for words, phrases and whole clauses.

She bought pens, notebooks, and a calculator.
He woke up, brushed his teeth, and left for the coaching class.

The comma before “and” in the last item is called the Oxford comma. Both styles are accepted in standard English. What matters is consistency: pick one style and use it throughout. It earns its place when meaning could blur. “I thank my parents, Ravi and Meera” could mean your parents are Ravi and Meera, or that you’re thanking four people. “I thank my parents, Ravi, and Meera” leaves no doubt.

Memory shortcut: “Slash the list.” Every item gets a slash except the last one, and the slash is your comma. If you only have two items, there’s no list, so no comma: ✗ tea, and coffee ✓ tea and coffee.

Rule 2 (C): Commas in compound sentences

When two complete sentences are joined by a conjunction, put a comma before the conjunction. The seven conjunctions that do this job are easy to remember as FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So.

✓ He studied all night, but he still felt nervous.
✓ She wanted to join the course, so she paid the fee.

Here’s the catch. If the second part has no subject of its own, you don’t have two sentences, so you don’t need the comma: “He studied all night but still felt nervous.” Ask yourself: “Does the part after the conjunction have its own subject and verb?” If yes, comma. If no, no comma.

Memory shortcut: FANBOYS, and then the “two subjects” check. Two subjects, one comma.

Rule 3 (I): Commas after introductory elements

Anything that opens a sentence before the main subject usually gets a comma after it. That includes single words (However, Yes, Unfortunately), phrases (After the exam, Running late), and dependent clauses (Although he was tired, If you practise daily).

✓ However, the results were announced late.
✓ If you practise daily, you will improve.
✓ Although the paper was tough, she finished on time.

Flip the order and the comma disappears: “You will improve if you practise daily.” A dependent clause that comes after the main clause doesn’t need a comma in most cases.

Memory shortcut: “Opening act, then comma.” Whatever performs before the main subject walks in gets a comma when it leaves the stage.

Rule 4 (E): Commas around extra information

This is the rule that decides most error spotting questions. Extra information, which you could remove without breaking the sentence, goes between commas. Essential information, which you can’t remove without changing the meaning, takes no commas.

✓ My brother, who lives in Pune, is a doctor.
✓ My brother who lives in Pune is a doctor.

Both are correct, but they say different things. The first suggests you have one brother, and his location is a bonus detail. The second suggests you have more than one brother, and “who lives in Pune” tells us which one. Same words, different meaning, all because of two commas.

Two more points examiners like. First, extra information always needs a pair of commas. ✗ My uncle, who lives in Nashik is a farmer. The second comma is missing. Second, in formal writing “which” introduces extra information (with commas) and “that” introduces essential information (without commas): “The book that I bought yesterday is excellent.”

Memory shortcut: “Commas are brackets.” They come in pairs, and if you can delete the bracketed part and still have a sensible sentence, you needed the commas.

Rule 5 (A): Commas when addressing someone

When you speak directly to a person, set the name or title off with a comma.

✓ Rahul, please open the window.
✓ Thank you, sir.
✓ I hope you understand, Priya, why we chose this plan.

A name in the middle takes two commas, one on each side. A name at the end takes one comma before it. ✗ Good morning sir. ✓ Good morning, sir.

Memory shortcut: “Call the name, add a comma.” If you could shout the word to get someone’s attention, it needs a comma.

Rule 6 (T): Commas before tag questions

A tag question is the short question at the end of a statement. The comma goes before it.

✓ You’re coming tomorrow, aren’t you?
✓ She can’t swim, can she?

The pattern is simple. A positive statement gets a negative tag, and a negative statement gets a positive tag. Exams combine this with tricky cases such as “Let’s go, shall we?” and “Open the door, will you?”

Memory shortcut: “Opposites attract.” Positive pairs with negative, negative pairs with positive, and a comma sits between the two.

Rule 7 (A): Commas between adjectives of equal rank

When two or more adjectives describe the same noun equally, separate them with commas. When they build on each other, don’t.

✓ She wore a bright, colourful dress.
✓ They have three old wooden chairs.

How do you tell the difference? Use the Swap-or-And test. Can you swap the adjectives (“a colourful, bright dress”) or put “and” between them (“a bright and colourful dress”)? If yes, use a comma. Try the same on “three old wooden chairs.” “Wooden old three chairs” sounds wrong, so no commas.

Rule 8 (D): Commas in dates, places and direct speech

Use a comma between the day and the year in the style September 24, 2025, and another one after the year if the sentence continues. Use commas to separate a city from its state, and a speaker from the words spoken.

✓ The book was published on September 24, 2025, and it is still on sale.
✓ She lives in Pune, Maharashtra, with her family.
✓ He said, “I will come.” / “I will come,” he said.

Inside the quotation marks, the comma sits before the closing quote when the spoken words come first. Notice how the speaker’s tag (“he said”) is separated from the quote by a comma, not a full stop.

The comma splice: the biggest exam trap

A comma splice happens when you join two complete sentences with only a comma. It’s the most common comma error in student writing, and examiners love it.

✗ She was tired, she kept studying.

There are two complete sentences here (“She was tired” and “she kept studying”), and a comma alone can’t hold them together. A comma is a thread. Two full sentences need a rope. You have four ways to fix it:

1. Use a full stop: She was tired. She kept studying.
2. Use a semicolon: She was tired; she kept studying.
3. Use a FANBOYS word: She was tired, but she kept studying.
4. Make one part dependent: Although she was tired, she kept studying.

Common exam pattern: an error spotting sentence that looks smooth when you read it quickly, such as “The train was late, we missed the meeting.” When the grammar feels fine but something is off, count the subjects and verbs. Two complete ideas with only a comma between them is your answer.

5 common comma mistakes (and how to fix them)

1. The comma splice. ✗ It was raining, we stayed home. ✓ It was raining, so we stayed home.

2. A comma between subject and verb. ✗ The tallest boy in the class, won the prize. ✓ The tallest boy in the class won the prize.

3. Only one comma around extra information. ✗ My teacher, Mr. Sharma is strict. ✓ My teacher, Mr. Sharma, is strict.

4. A comma before “that” in an essential clause. ✗ The book, that I bought yesterday, is excellent. ✓ The book that I bought yesterday is excellent.

5. A comma between only two items. ✗ She likes music, and dance. ✓ She likes music and dance.

Mistake 2 deserves special attention. Students put a comma where they breathe, usually after a long subject. Don’t. The subject and its verb stay together, however long the subject is.

The 4-step comma check

When you’re stuck on a sentence, don’t stare at it. Work through it in the same order every time. I call it Before, Inside, After.

Step 1: Find the main clause. Underline the part that could stand alone as a sentence.

Step 2: Check what comes Before it. Is there an introductory word, phrase or clause? Add a comma after it.

Step 3: Check what sits Inside. Look for extra information, a name, or adjectives of equal rank. Commas go in pairs here.

Step 4: Check what comes After. Is there a tag question, a FANBOYS word with a new subject, or a trailing detail? Place the comma before it, and make sure no comma separates the subject from its verb.

It takes about ten seconds once you’ve practised it. In a timed paper, that’s a fair price for a mark you’d otherwise lose.

A pattern I see in almost every class

Here’s something teachers notice year after year. Weak commas rarely come from not knowing the rules. They come from not applying the rules under time pressure. A student reads the rule on Monday, nods, and then writes “My cousin, who studies in Mumbai is preparing for SSC” on Friday without noticing the missing second comma.

The fix isn’t more reading. It’s one short daily habit: take five sentences from any newspaper editorial, circle every comma, and name its letter from “Smart Candidates Insert Extra Addresses, Tags, Adjectives, Dates.” After two weeks, you’ll start seeing commas the way an examiner does. You’ll also spot the commas that have no letter, and those are the ones that don’t belong.

Expert tips for exam day

Read the sentence without the comma, then with it. If the meaning shifts, the comma is doing real work.

Hunt for pairs. If you see one comma around extra information, look for its partner.

Don’t punish short sentences. “Yes, sir.” “However, it rained.” Tiny openers still get a comma.

Trust rules over feelings. “It sounds right” fails you more often than any other reason in error spotting.

Stay consistent. In writing sections, whichever style you choose for the Oxford comma, keep it the same throughout.

Practice set: add, remove or fix the commas

These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. My friend who is a lawyer lives in Nagpur.
  2. After the interview he felt relieved.
  3. She was tired, she finished the work.
  4. You haven’t met my manager have you?
  5. He bought a small, red, car.
  6. Thank you sir for the guidance.

Answers

1. My friend, who is a lawyer, lives in Nagpur. (Rule 4, extra information: a pair of commas, assuming you have one such friend in mind.)

2. After the interview, he felt relieved. (Rule 3, introductory phrase.)

3. She was tired, but she finished the work. Or: She was tired; she finished the work. (Comma splice, fixed with FANBOYS or a semicolon.)

4. You haven’t met my manager, have you? (Rule 6, tag question.)

5. He bought a small red car. (Rule 7: “small” and “red” fail the Swap-or-And test, and a comma never separates the last adjective from its noun.)

6. Thank you, sir, for the guidance. (Rule 5, addressing: a comma on each side.)

If you scored 5 or 6, you’re ready for harder material. If you scored 3 or fewer, re-read Rules 3 and 4 and try again tomorrow. Frequently asked questions

Is the Oxford comma compulsory in exams?

No. Both styles are accepted. Exam questions rarely hinge on the Oxford comma alone, and when they do, the reason is usually ambiguity. Choose one style and stay consistent.

Do I put a comma before “because”?

Usually not. “She stayed home because she was ill” needs no comma. Add one only when “because” starts a contrasting afterthought, which is rare.

Should I always put a comma before “and”?

Only in a list of three or more (Rule 1), or when “and” joins two complete sentences with their own subjects (Rule 2). Otherwise, skip it.

Where does the comma go with quotation marks?

When the spoken words come first, the comma sits inside the closing quotation mark: “I will come,” he said. When the speaker comes first, a comma follows the speaker: He said, “I will come.”

How do I improve my comma accuracy quickly?

Use the eight-letter sentence, run the Before-Inside-After check, and practise five sentences a day. Two weeks of that beats two hours of rereading rules.
