Why a Checklist Beats Memorizing One Rule

The basic question-tag rule — flip the polarity, match the auxiliary — genuinely works for most sentences. The problem is that exam-setters specifically build questions around the sentences where it doesn't work cleanly. A single rule can't cover ten different exception categories; a checklist can.

The 10 Tricky Rules

1. Hidden negatives (nobody, nothing, no one) take a positive tag.
Wrong: Nobody called, didn't they?   Correct: Nobody called, did they?

2. "Never," "hardly," "scarcely," "seldom" also count as negatives.
Wrong: He never lies, doesn't he?   Correct: He never lies, does he?

3. "I am" takes the fixed tag "aren't I," never "am I not."
Wrong: I am right, am I not?   Correct (standard usage): I am right, aren't I?

4. Imperative sentences take "will you?" not a statement-style tag.
Wrong: Close the window, do you?   Correct: Close the window, will you?

5. "Let's" always takes "shall we?" regardless of the rest of the sentence.
Wrong: Let's begin, will we?   Correct: Let's begin, shall we?

6. "Everyone," "someone," "anybody" take "they" in the tag.
Wrong: Everyone agreed, didn't he?   Correct: Everyone agreed, didn't they?

7. "This" and "that" as subject take "it" in the tag.
Wrong: This is correct, aren't they?   Correct: This is correct, isn't it?

8. "These" and "those" as subject take "they" in the tag.
Wrong: These are expensive, isn't it?   Correct: These are expensive, aren't they?

9. Modal verbs (can, will, must, should) repeat in the tag — never switch to do/does/did.
Wrong: You can swim, don't you?   Correct: You can swim, can't you?

10. In compound sentences, the tag matches the nearest (second) clause, not the first.
Wrong: He is tired but he will finish, is he?   Correct: He is tired but he will finish, won't he?

The 3-Second Verification Habit

Before writing any tag, run this in order: (1) scan for a hidden negative word, (2) check if it's one of the three fixed forms (I am / imperative / let's), (3) check if the subject is a tricky pronoun (everyone/this/these), (4) check if a modal verb is present, (5) for compound sentences, tag the nearest clause. Five quick checks, run in that order, and every one of the 10 rules above gets caught automatically.

Expert Shortcuts Toppers Actually Use

1. Group the 10 rules into three families, not ten separate facts. Hidden negatives, fixed forms, and structure traps — three categories are far easier to recall under pressure than ten isolated rules.

2. Say the tag out loud during practice. Question tags are a spoken-language feature; reading them aloud catches errors a silent read-through misses.

3. Revise this list in the last 10 minutes before your exam, not days before. A short, high-density checklist like this is built exactly for last-minute recall, not long-term deep study.

4. Don't skip the compound-sentence rule just because it "feels" less important. It's one of the most repeated traps precisely because most study material treats it as an afterthought.
