Preposition Tricks for SSC and Bank Exams: Easy Formulas to Remember Fixed Prepositions

By Balu Kandekar · Published 2026-10-26 · 14 min read

Try this in ten seconds: (A) He is afraid / (B) from snakes / (C) since childhood. / (D) No error.

Most students read it, find nothing alarming and pick (D). “Afraid from” sounds almost right, because we say “scared from” in everyday talk, and the rhythm of the sentence hides the slip. The correct form is “afraid of.” One small word decided the answer, and that’s what preposition questions do to you.

The honest truth is that prepositions can’t be reasoned out. There’s no logic behind “good at” and “weak in.” You either remember the pair or you don’t. So the smart move isn’t to memorise a thousand pairs. It’s to learn short formulas that group many pairs at once, which is exactly what this guide does.

You’ll get 10 preposition tricks, each with a memory hook, a wrong sentence and its correction. You’ll also get a three-word routine called WORD, PAIR, CHECK, a 10-second time plan and a nine-question practice set with answers. How prepositions are tested in exams

In SSC and bank exams, prepositions appear in more places than students expect. Knowing the formats tells you where to look.

1. Error spotting. A sentence is split into parts (A), (B), (C), and (D) is “No error.” The wrong preposition sits at the start of one part.

2. Fill in the blanks. Four options, all prepositions. Only one forms the right pair with the word before the blank.

3. Cloze passages. A paragraph with several blanks, where each blank tests a fixed pair.

A note on the examples in this post: every sentence is an original practice sentence on a common exam pattern. None of them is copied from a particular year’s paper, and I haven’t attached any year to them. The patterns are what repeat, and the patterns are what you should learn.

The WORD, PAIR, CHECK routine

You can’t scan a list of two hundred pairs with the clock running. You need one routine that works on every sentence.

“WORD, PAIR, CHECK.”

WORD: spot the head word before the preposition (a verb, adjective or noun)
PAIR: recall its partner preposition from your trick groups
CHECK: compare the partner with the preposition in the sentence

Say the three words silently on every sentence. It takes about five seconds, and it stops you from judging the preposition by how it sounds.

10 preposition tricks with examples

Trick 1: Fear, Pride and Fondness Take OF

✗ He is afraid from snakes.
✓ He is afraid of snakes.

Adjectives of fear, pride, dislike and ability take of: afraid of, proud of, fond of, jealous of, capable of, aware of, guilty of, ashamed of. Memory trick: “Fear, pride and fondness are all OF.”

Trick 2: Skills Are AT, Subjects Are IN

✗ He is good in dancing but weak at mathematics.
✓ He is good at dancing but weak in mathematics.

Use at for a skill or an activity (good at, bad at, clever at). Use in for a school subject or a field (weak in, expert in, interested in). Memory trick: “Skills sit AT. Subjects sit IN.”

Trick 3: Different Comes FROM

✗ This pen is different than that one.
✓ This pen is different from that one.

Different always takes from in exam answers. Similar takes to, and same takes as. Memory trick: “Different comes from a different place.” For more fixed adjective pairs, see the list of appropriate prepositions with verbs, adjectives and nouns.

Trick 4: The Lean-On Verbs

✗ He depends of his parents for money.
✓ He depends on his parents for money.

Verbs of support and attention take on: depend on, rely on, insist on, focus on, concentrate on, count on, congratulate on. Memory trick: “Dependable, reliable, insistent: lean ON.”

Trick 5: The DERMA Verbs Need No Coat

✗ We discussed about the plan yesterday.
✓ We discussed the plan yesterday.

Some verbs take their object directly and need no preposition. Remember DERMA: Discuss, Enter, Reach, Marry, Attend. Memory trick: “DERMA is skin only. No coat, no preposition.” So “enter the room,” “reach Pune,” “marry her” and “attend the meeting.”

Trick 6: BY Is a Deadline, UNTIL Is a Duration

✗ Submit the form until Friday.
✓ Submit the form by Friday.

By means not later than a time. Until means continuing up to a time. Memory trick: “BY is a deadline. UNTIL is a duration.” Compare “Wait here until I return,” which describes how long you wait.

Trick 7: DURING Names the Event, FOR Counts the Time

✗ I stayed in Pune during two weeks.
✓ I stayed in Pune for two weeks.

Use for with a length of time (two weeks, three hours). Use during with a named event or period (the festival, the summer). Memory trick: “During names it. For counts it.” Learn more confusing pairs in confusing prepositions.

Trick 8: By Is the Method, In Is a Box, On Is a Ride

✗ He goes to office with bus.
✓ He goes to office by bus.

Use by for the method of travel, with no article (by bus, by train, by road). For a position, use in for small enclosed vehicles (in a car) and on for large ones (on a bus, on a train). Memory trick: “By is the method. In is a box. On is a ride you can stand in.” The in, on, at basics are in my in, on, at rules.

Trick 9: ARKS Go TO

✗ He found a solution of the problem.
✓ He found a solution to the problem.

Answer, Reply, Key and Solution all take to. Other nouns have their own partners, such as reason for and cause of. Memory trick: “ARKS go To.”

Trick 10: Sometimes TO Is a Preposition

✗ I look forward to meet you.
✓ I look forward to meeting you.

In look forward to, object to, be used to and in addition to, the word to is a preposition, so the -ing form follows. Memory trick: “Can you say ‘to it’? Then add -ing.”

Three trap sentences examiners reuse

Once you know the ten tricks, these three disguises are easy to spot.

Trap 1: The naked verb. “We reached to Pune at night.” Reach is a DERMA verb, so use “reached Pune.”

Trap 2: The wrong marriage partner. “She is married with a doctor.” The correct pair is “married to,” so use “married to a doctor.”

Trap 3: The hidden -ing. “I am looking forward to hear from you.” Here to is a preposition, so use “hearing.”

Notice the pattern. Each sentence reads smoothly, and the error is a single small word. That’s why WORD comes first. Find the head word, and the right partner is easy to recall.

The 5-step error spotting method

WORD, PAIR, CHECK tells you what to do. This routine tells you how to run it from start to finish.

Step 1: Read the sentence once. Underline every preposition.

Step 2: WORD. Find the head word before each preposition.

Step 3: PAIR. Recall the partner using your trick groups.

Step 4: CHECK. Compare the partner with the sentence and test your fix by reading the corrected version.

Step 5: Only then choose “No error.” It’s a conclusion, never a default.

A 10-second time plan

Here’s a plan that works for most students. Adjust the seconds to suit your pace.

0 to 2 seconds: read once and underline the prepositions.

2 to 5 seconds: find the WORD before each one.

5 to 8 seconds: recall the PAIR and CHECK it.

8 to 10 seconds: mark the answer and move on. If you’re stuck, skip it and return.

A pattern I see in almost every batch

Here’s something that repeats year after year in my classes. A student can recite the whole DERMA list, then picks “No error” on “We discussed about the plan.” The knowledge is there. The checking isn’t.

The fix is a habit, not more theory. Take ten sentences from any newspaper, circle every preposition and write its head word beside it. Ten sentences a day for two weeks trains your eyes to travel from the preposition back to its head word, and that’s the whole skill. Expert tips for exam day

Learn pairs in sentences. A pair inside a sentence is easier to recall than a pair on a list.

Trust the pair over your ear. “It sounds fine” is why most preposition errors survive.

Look at the word before the preposition. The error is usually a bad partner, not a bad preposition.

Keep an error log. Write down every pair you miss and revise that list for a week.

A 5-day practice plan

You don’t need a big block of time. Fifteen minutes a day is enough if you do the same thing each time: read the tricks, say the memory hooks aloud, then solve ten mixed sentences and note every mistake.

Day 1: Tricks 1 and 2

Day 2: Tricks 3 and 4

Day 3: Tricks 5 and 6

Day 4: Tricks 7 and 8

Day 5: Tricks 9 and 10, then retake the practice set below

Three mistakes to avoid

Learning a long list in one sitting. Groups stick. Lists fade by morning.

Using the wrong trick. Each trick has a trigger. Check that the trigger is really in the sentence.

Skipping the practice. Reading a trick isn’t learning it. Solve ten sentences the same day.

Practice set: 9 error spotting questions

Find the part with the error, or choose (D) if there’s none. These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. He is afraid (A) / from snakes (B) / since childhood. (C) / No error (D)
  2. She is good (A) / in singing (B) / and dancing. (C) / No error (D)
  3. This pen is (A) / different than (B) / that one. (C) / No error (D)
  4. He depends (A) / of his parents (B) / for money. (C) / No error (D)
  5. We discussed (A) / about the plan (B) / yesterday. (C) / No error (D)
  6. Submit the form (A) / until Friday (B) / without fail. (C) / No error (D)
  7. I stayed in Pune (A) / during two weeks (B) / last year. (C) / No error (D)
  8. He found (A) / a solution to (B) / the problem. (C) / No error (D)
  9. I look forward (A) / to meeting (B) / you soon. (C) / No error (D)

Answers

1. (B): fear takes of, so use “of snakes.” (Trick 1)

2. (B): a skill takes at, so use “at singing.” (Trick 2)

3. (B): different takes from, so use “different from.” (Trick 3)

4. (B): depend is a lean-on verb, so use “on his parents.” (Trick 4)

5. (B): discuss needs no preposition, so use “the plan.” (Trick 5)

6. (B): a deadline takes by, so use “by Friday.” (Trick 6)

7. (B): a length of time takes for, so use “for two weeks.” (Trick 7)

8. (D): no error. Solution takes to. (Trick 9)

9. (D): no error. Look forward to takes -ing. (Trick 10)

If you scored 8 or 9, you’re ready for harder questions. If you scored 5 to 7, revise the tricks you missed. If you scored below 5, go back to WORD, PAIR, CHECK and the cheat sheet, and retake the set after two days. For a bigger bank of questions, use the mixed preposition MCQs with answers.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to learn fixed prepositions?

Learn them in groups, not lists. Groups like fear takes of, support verbs take on and ARKS take to cover dozens of pairs at once, and a short daily practice makes them stick.

Is it different from or different than?

In exam answers, use different from. Different than appears in casual American English, but exams treat different from as the standard form.

Which verbs take no preposition?

Common ones are discuss, enter, reach, marry, attend, resemble and approach. Remember DERMA for the first five.

What is the difference between by and until?

By marks a deadline, meaning not later than that time. Until marks how long something continues. So you submit a form by Friday and wait until Friday.

How many preposition questions come in SSC CGL and bank exams?

There’s no fixed number, because prepositions appear in error spotting, fill in the blanks and cloze questions. Check the latest official notification for your exam pattern.
