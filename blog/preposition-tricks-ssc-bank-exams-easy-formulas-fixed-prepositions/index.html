Preposition Tricks for SSC and Bank Exams: Easy Formulas to Remember Fixed Prepositions

Which of these is correct? (A) She is good in English. (B) She is good at English. (C) She is good on English.

If you picked (B), you already know the secret: many prepositions are not free. They are fixed to particular words. The two building blocks: free vs fixed

Every preposition question is built from one of two types, so start there.

Free preposition. It can change according to meaning. “The book is on the table / in the bag / under the chair.” The choice depends on location or direction.

Fixed preposition. It is locked to a particular verb, adjective or noun. You cannot replace it without creating an error. “She depends on her brother” is correct; “depends in” or “depends at” is wrong.

SSC and Bank papers test fixed prepositions far more than free ones. If you want the full list of high-frequency verb + preposition combinations, see the 25 high-frequency verbs guide.

The Fix-and-Group formula

You do not need to memorise hundreds of random combinations. You need one routine.

“Fix and Group.”

FIX identify the verb, adjective or noun that usually takes a fixed preposition.
GROUP put it into its family: the ON-group, the OF-group, the IN-group, the FOR-group, the TO-group or the WITH-group.
Name it: once the group is clear, the preposition is automatic.

A memory hook for the six biggest families: ON – OF – IN – FOR – TO – WITH. Learn one family a day and the confusion disappears.

The four main groups with examples

1. Verb + Fixed Preposition (the biggest exam group)

These are the combinations that appear most often in error spotting.

✓ Depend on / Rely on / Insist on / Concentrate on
✓ Accuse of / Approve of / Consist of / Die of
✓ Believe in / Succeed in / Specialize in / Result in
✓ Prefer to / Object to / Belong to / Refer to

Memory trick: “ON for pressure and focus, OF for content and blame, IN for field and belief, TO for direction and preference.”

2. Adjective + Fixed Preposition

These are short but deadly in fill-in-the-blanks.

✓ Good at / Bad at / Expert at
✓ Interested in / Involved in / Engaged in
✓ Afraid of / Proud of / Fond of / Tired of
✓ Similar to / Equal to / Married to

Memory trick: “AT for skill, IN for activity, OF for feeling, TO for comparison or relation.”

3. Noun + Fixed Preposition

Less frequent but still tested every year.

✓ Solution to / Answer to / Key to / Reply to
✓ Reason for / Demand for / Need for / Respect for
✓ Increase in / Decrease in / Rise in / Fall in

Memory trick: “TO for solution-type nouns, FOR for requirement-type nouns, IN for change-type nouns.”

4. Phrasal combinations that look free but are fixed

These trip even strong students.

✓ Look after (care for) vs Look for (search)
✓ Put up with (tolerate) vs Put on (wear)
✓ Take after (resemble) vs Take over (assume control)

Memory trick: “Learn them as complete units, not as separate words.”

When each group appears in papers

Knowing the lists is only half the skill. The other half is recognising the pattern the examiner loves.

Error spotting almost always uses Verb + Fixed Preposition. The wrong option replaces “on” with “in” or “of” with “for”.

Fill-in-the-blanks prefers Adjective + Fixed Preposition because the blank is short and the options look similar.

Sentence improvement mixes both and sometimes adds a phrasal verb that looks free but is fixed.

Cloze tests in Bank exams love the OF-group and the TO-group because they fit naturally into formal passages.

A good aspirant does not wait for the question. He already knows which group is being tested the moment he sees the key word.

How to solve any fixed-preposition question in 4 steps

Step 1: Spot the key word. Find the verb, adjective or noun that usually takes a fixed preposition.

Step 2: Recall its group. Is it an ON-word, OF-word, IN-word, TO-word, FOR-word or WITH-word?

Step 3: Test the options. Only one option will match the group.

Step 4: Check meaning. Make sure the sentence still makes sense. If two options look possible, the group decides.

Try it on “She is interested ___ classical music.” Key word = interested → IN-group → answer is “in”.

Three traps that fool aspirants

Trap 1: Treating a fixed preposition as free. “She is good in English” looks natural to many Hindi or Marathi speakers, but the fixed combination is “good at”.

Trap 2: Confusing similar pairs. “Insist on” vs “Consist of”, “Look after” vs “Look for”, “Prefer to” vs “Prefer over” (the last is wrong).

Trap 3: Adding an unnecessary preposition. “Discuss about the matter” is wrong; “discuss” already contains the idea. “Enter into the room” is wrong in most contexts; “enter the room” is enough.

Commas and articles never decide the preposition. Always go back to the Fix-and-Group formula. For more error-spotting patterns, see preposition error spotting for SSC and Bank.

A short paragraph makeover

Why does any of this matter? Because the wrong preposition makes even a correct idea look weak. Here’s a paragraph full of common fixed-preposition mistakes.

Before: She is good in mathematics. She depends in her teacher. She is interested on science. She prefers coffee than tea.

Every sentence contains a fixed-preposition error. Now the corrected version:

After: She is good at mathematics. She depends on her teacher. She is interested in science. She prefers coffee to tea.

Four small changes, four correct groups. The paragraph now sounds confident and exam-ready.

Practice set: choose the correct fixed preposition

Write the correct preposition for each sentence. These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. She is very good ___ dancing.
  2. He was accused ___ stealing the money.
  3. I prefer tea ___ coffee.
  4. The success of the project depends ___ hard work.
  5. She is interested ___ learning French.
  6. There is no easy solution ___ this problem.
  7. He died ___ a heart attack.
  8. We must concentrate ___ our studies.
  9. She is married ___ a doctor.
  10. The committee consists ___ ten members.

Answers

1. at (good at = skill)

2. of (accuse of)

3. to (prefer to)

4. on (depend on)

5. in (interested in)

6. to (solution to)

7. of (die of)

8. on (concentrate on)

9. to (married to)

10. of (consist of)

If you scored 9 or 10, you have internalised the groups. If you scored 6 to 8, revise the traps. If you scored below 6, go back to Fix-and-Group and retake the set after two days.

Writing practice: change the preposition

Reading about groups helps, but writing them cements the skill. Start from a wrong sentence and correct it.

Start: She is good in English. She depends in her brother.

Task A: Correct both sentences.

Task B: Make two new sentences using “interested in” and “prefer to”.

Task C: Write one sentence each for the OF-group and the ON-group.

Answers

A. She is good at English. She depends on her brother.

B. I am interested in classical music. I prefer tea to coffee.

C. He was accused of cheating. We must insist on punctuality.

Your answers may differ, and that is fine if the fixed preposition is correct.

Error-spotting transformations

Examiners love to change one correct fixed preposition into a wrong one. The idea is simple: replace the fixed preposition with a free-looking one that sounds natural to Indian learners.

Correct → Wrong: “She is good at mathematics” becomes “She is good in mathematics.”

Correct → Wrong: “He depends on his parents” becomes “He depends upon his parents” (upon is sometimes accepted, but “on” is safer) or “depends in”.

Correct → Wrong: “I prefer tea to coffee” becomes “I prefer tea than coffee.”

Memory trick: “If it sounds too much like Hindi or Marathi word-for-word translation, check the fixed group again.”

A 5-day practice plan

Fifteen minutes a day is enough if you keep the routine the same.

Day 1: Learn the ON-group and OF-group. Write five sentences for each.

Day 2: Learn the IN-group and TO-group. Write five sentences for each.

Day 3: Learn the FOR-group and WITH-group. Write five sentences for each.

Day 4: Solve 20 previous-year error-spotting questions focusing only on prepositions.

Day 5: Retake the practice set above without looking at the answers and write five original sentences using mixed groups.

A pattern I see in almost every batch

Aspirants can usually list twenty fixed combinations. The slip happens when two similar words appear together. Show them “insist” and “consist”, and half will put the same preposition for both. The fix is a contrast question, not a longer list: “What does the word actually mean?” Insist = put pressure → on. Consist = made up of content → of. Ask that question every time, and the confusion disappears.

Mistakes to avoid

Translating from mother tongue. “Good in English” is a classic translation error.

Treating every preposition as free. Fixed combinations do not change with meaning.

Ignoring the noun group. “Solution of” is wrong; it is “solution to”.

Over-learning rare combinations. Master the high-frequency table first; rare ones can wait.

Frequently asked questions

What are fixed prepositions?

Fixed prepositions are prepositions that are permanently linked to certain verbs, adjectives or nouns. You cannot change them without changing the meaning or making the sentence wrong. Examples: depend on, interested in, accused of.

How can I remember fixed prepositions easily?

Use the Fix-and-Group formula. Group words that take the same preposition (the ON-group, the OF-group, the IN-group) and learn one group at a time with short memory sentences.

Are fixed prepositions asked in SSC and Bank exams?

Yes. Error spotting, fill-in-the-blanks and sentence improvement questions regularly test fixed prepositions. High-frequency combinations appear every year.

What is the difference between a fixed preposition and a free preposition?

A free preposition can change according to meaning (in the room / on the table). A fixed preposition is locked to the word (rely on, not rely in or rely at).

How do I stop confusing similar prepositions?

Learn them in contrast pairs: insist on vs consist of, good at vs good for, look after vs look for. Write two example sentences for each pair every day for five days.

Is there a short formula for exam day?

Yes. Ask three questions: 1. Is this verb/adjective/noun known for a fixed preposition? 2. Which group does it belong to? 3. Does the sentence still make sense with that preposition? If yes, lock it.
