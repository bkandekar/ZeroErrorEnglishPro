How to Join Sentences Using -ing and -ed Participles

By Balu Kandekar · Published 2026-10-26 · 14 min read

Read this sentence: “Walking to school, the rain started.” It sounds natural, and you’ve probably seen sentences like it. But think about it for a moment. Who was walking to school? According to the grammar, the rain was. That’s a dangling participle, and it’s one of the most common slips in English writing.

Participles are also one of the best tools you have for joining sentences smoothly. “She opened the box. She smiled.” becomes “Opening the box, she smiled.” Two short sentences turn into one clean one, and the writing instantly sounds more mature.

This guide teaches you how to join sentences with -ing and -ed participles, how to avoid the dangling trap, and how to choose the right form. You’ll get five patterns, a three-word routine, a paragraph makeover and a practice set with answers. What is a participle phrase?

A participle is a verb form that works like an adjective. A participle phrase is a participle plus the words that go with it. It describes a noun, usually the subject of the sentence, and it saves you a whole clause.

FormPatternExample
Present participleverb + -ingSmiling, she walked in.
Past participleverb + -ed, or the third formSurprised by the noise, she turned.
Perfect participlehaving + past participleHaving finished, she left.

You’ll find participle phrases listed with other phrase types in my guide to types of phrases, and the difference between a phrase and a clause is in phrase vs clause.

The DOER, TIME, FORM routine

Joining sentences with a participle isn’t hard if you ask three questions in the right order.

“DOER, TIME, FORM.”

DOER: do both sentences have the same doer? If not, stop. Use a clause instead.
TIME: did the actions happen at the same time, or did one happen earlier?
FORM: choose -ing for the same time, having plus past participle for earlier, and the past participle for passive meaning

The golden rule is “Same doer, or don’t use it.” The participle phrase must describe the subject of the main sentence, and the first question protects you from the dangling trap.

5 patterns for joining sentences

Pattern 1: -ing for actions at the same time

Before: She opened the box. She smiled.

✓ Opening the box, she smiled.

Use the -ing form when both actions happen together or one flows straight into the other. Memory trick: “Same time, -ing.”

Pattern 2: Having plus a past participle for an earlier action

Before: He finished his homework. He went out to play.

✓ Having finished his homework, he went out to play.

Use having plus a past participle when the first action is completed before the second begins. Memory trick: “Earlier action, having.”

Pattern 3: The past participle for a passive meaning

Before: The girl was surprised by the noise. She turned around.

✓ Surprised by the noise, the girl turned around.

When the doer receives the action (was surprised by), drop was and keep the past participle. You can also begin with a short description: “Tired, he fell asleep.” Memory trick: “Received the action? Drop the was.”

Pattern 4: Not plus a participle for a negative

Before: He didn’t know the answer. He guessed.

✓ Not knowing the answer, he guessed.

Put not before the participle to make it negative. Don’t put it after. Memory trick: “Not goes first.”

Pattern 5: A participle after the noun

Before: The boy is standing near the gate. He is my cousin.

✓ The boy standing near the gate is my cousin.

A participle phrase can also sit right after a noun to describe it. It replaces who is or which was. Memory trick: “Who is, which was: drop them.” The same idea is behind relative clauses.

Where does the comma go?

Phrase first, comma after. “Opening the box, she smiled.” Always put a comma after an opening participle phrase.

Phrase after a noun, usually no comma. “The boy standing near the gate is my cousin.” The phrase tells you which boy.

Extra information, commas around it. “My cousin, standing near the gate, waved at me.” If you could remove the phrase and keep the meaning, use commas.

For the broader comma rules, see comma rules in English grammar.

The dangling participle trap

A dangling participle happens when the phrase doesn’t describe the subject that follows it. The sentence says something silly, or at least unclear.

✗ Walking to school, the rain started.
✓ Walking to school, I got caught in the rain.

Here’s how to fix any dangling participle, using three methods.

Fix 1: Change the subject. Make the doer the subject of the main clause: “Walking to school, I got caught in the rain.”

Fix 2: Turn the phrase into a clause. “While I was walking to school, the rain started.”

Fix 3: Rewrite the sentence. “The rain started as I walked to school.”

Memory trick: “Who is doing it? Put that person right after the comma.” Always ask who is walking, reading or looking, and make sure the answer is the next noun. It’s a close cousin of the joining mistakes in run-on sentences and comma splices.

Participle or gerund?

Both end in -ing, which causes confusion. The difference is the job. A participle describes a noun, like an adjective. A gerund is a noun itself.

Gerund (a noun): “Swimming is good exercise.” Swimming is the subject.

Participle (a describer): “Swimming across the lake, he lost his hat.” Swimming describes he.

Memory trick: “If it names the activity, it’s a gerund. If it describes who is doing it, it’s a participle.” Only participle phrases can dangle.

When not to use a participle

Participles are neat, but they aren’t always the best choice. Step back to a clause in these cases.

When the doers differ. “The rain started, and I got wet” can’t become a participle sentence without a new doer.

When the reason matters. “Because he was tired, he fell asleep” states the cause clearly. “Tired, he fell asleep” only hints at it.

When the sentence gets heavy. If you have three participle phrases in one sentence, split it.

A short paragraph makeover

Here’s a paragraph made of short sentences. Read it aloud and notice how choppy it feels.

Before: Maya left the office. She was exhausted. She took the bus home. She read a book on the way. She felt calmer.

Maya is the doer in every sentence, so participles are safe. The second sentence is a state, so a short description works. The third and fourth flow together.

After: Exhausted, Maya left the office and took the bus home. Reading a book on the way, she felt calmer.

Practice set: join the sentences

Join each pair using a participle phrase. These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. She opened the box. She smiled.
  2. He finished his homework. He went out to play.
  3. The man was tired. He fell asleep at once.
  4. The girl was surprised by the noise. She turned around.
  5. He didn’t know the answer. He guessed.
  6. The boy is standing near the gate. He is my cousin.
  7. The letter was written yesterday. It arrived this morning.
  8. She saw the accident. She called the police.
  9. The train was delayed by fog. It reached the station late.
  10. He was walking down the road. He found a wallet.

Answers

1. Opening the box, she smiled. (same time, -ing)

2. Having finished his homework, he went out to play. (earlier action, having)

3. Tired, the man fell asleep at once. (or Being tired, the man fell asleep at once.)

4. Surprised by the noise, the girl turned around. (passive meaning, drop was)

5. Not knowing the answer, he guessed. (not goes first)

6. The boy standing near the gate is my cousin. (participle after the noun, no comma)

7. The letter written yesterday arrived this morning. (which was dropped)

8. Seeing the accident, she called the police. (same doer, -ing)

9. Delayed by fog, the train reached the station late. (passive meaning)

10. Walking down the road, he found a wallet. (same time, -ing)

Practice set: fix the dangling participle

Each sentence below has a dangling participle. Fix it using any of the three methods.

  1. Walking to school, the rain started.
  2. Having finished the exam, the bell rang.
  3. Looking out of the window, a bird was seen.
  4. Tired after the trip, the bed looked inviting.
  5. Reading the newspaper, my tea went cold.

Answers

1. Walking to school, I got caught in the rain. (or While I was walking to school, the rain started.)

2. Having finished the exam, we heard the bell ring. (or When we had finished the exam, the bell rang.)

3. Looking out of the window, I saw a bird.

4. Tired after the trip, she found the bed inviting.

5. While I was reading the newspaper, my tea went cold.

Your fixes may differ from mine, and that’s fine as long as the phrase now describes the subject that follows it. If you got 12 or more of the 15 right, you’re ready to use participles in your own writing.

Pick the best version

Sentence correction questions often give you four versions and ask for the best one. Try these three, applying DOER first.

1. A: Walking along the beach, a shell caught my eye. B: Walking along the beach, I noticed a shell. C: A shell was noticed walking along the beach.

2. A: Having finished the report, the manager signed it. B: Finishing the report, the manager signed it. C: Having finished the report, signed it the manager.

3. A: Not knowing the way, a map was bought. B: Not knowing the way, we bought a map. C: We bought a map, not knowing the way it was.

Answers

1. B: “I” is the doer who was walking.

2. A: the manager finished the report first, so having plus a past participle is correct.

3. B: “we” didn’t know the way, so it comes straight after the comma.

Participles in real writing

Participles aren’t only for grammar exercises. They give your everyday writing a calmer, more professional rhythm.

Emails. “Having reviewed your proposal, I’d like to suggest two changes” sounds more polished than “I reviewed your proposal. I’d like to suggest two changes.”

Essays. “Faced with rising costs, many families cut spending” puts the cause and the result in one smooth sentence.

Stories. “Hearing footsteps, she froze” moves the action along faster than two separate sentences.

Use them in moderation, because one good participle sentence per paragraph is plenty. Overuse makes writing feel mechanical.

Mistakes to avoid

Ignoring the doer. If the doers differ, the participle dangles.

Using -ing for an earlier action. If the first action finished earlier, use having plus a past participle.

Placing not after the participle. Say not knowing, not knowing not.

Overusing participles. Three in a row make the paragraph heavy. Mix them with other sentence types.

A pattern I see in almost every batch

Learners can usually form the phrase correctly. Where they slip is the doer. They write “Reading the report, the numbers looked wrong,” and the sentence says the numbers were reading. The fix is a single question: who is doing that action? Say the answer aloud, and then put that person straight after the comma.

Expert tips for practice

Check the doer first. It takes two seconds and saves you from the biggest mistake.

Read it aloud. If the first noun after the comma can’t do the action, it dangles.

Learn the three forms together. Smiling, smiled and having smiled solve three situations.

Keep a dangling log. Save two of your own each week and fix them in three ways.

Frequently asked questions

What is a participle phrase?

It’s a verb form ending in -ing, -ed or the third form, together with the words that go with it, and it describes a noun. For example, “Opening the box, she smiled.”

What is a dangling participle?

It’s a participle phrase that doesn’t describe the subject that follows it, as in “Walking to school, the rain started.” The rain wasn’t walking, so the sentence is unclear.

When do I use having plus a past participle?

Use it when the action in the phrase finished before the action in the main sentence, as in “Having finished his homework, he went out to play.”

Do I always need a comma after a participle phrase?

Put a comma after a participle phrase that opens the sentence. A phrase that sits right after a noun and tells you which one usually needs no comma.

Are dangling participles tested in exams?
