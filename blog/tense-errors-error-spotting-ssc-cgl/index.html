Why Tense Errors Hide So Well in Error Spotting

Most grammar errors announce themselves. A wrong preposition feels odd; a missing article leaves a gap. A tense error feels natural because the verb is a real, correctly spelt English word. It's only wrong in relation to something else in the sentence.

That "something else" is always one of three things: a time word (yesterday, since, by the time), a partner clause (if, when, that), or a second verb in the same sentence. The examiner puts the clue in one part of the sentence and the faulty verb in another part, with a few words between them. Your eyes read the sentence like a story, left to right, and your ear accepts the verb because it sounds like spoken English.

Spoken English is forgiving. In daily conversation we say "I have seen him yesterday" and nobody blinks. Exams aren't conversations. They test the written, rule-bound form of the language, and the rules are strict about which tense can live next to which clue.

So the skill you're building isn't "knowing tenses". You already know the twelve tenses. The skill is pairing: spotting the clue and checking whether the verb obeys it. Once you train your eyes to hunt for the clue first and the verb second, most tense questions turn into a matching exercise.

The Clue Word Map: Which Time Word Controls Which Tense

Here's the map I ask every student to copy into a notebook. It isn't complete, but it covers the clues that appear again and again in error-spotting questions.

Clue wordsTense they demandExample
yesterday, ago, last week, in 2019, just nowSimple PastShe left two hours ago.
since, for, so far, till now, yet, already, just (alone), ever, neverPresent Perfect or Present Perfect ContinuousHe has lived here since 2015.
by the time, by next month, by 5 pm tomorrowFuture Perfect (Past Perfect in past stories)By next June, she will have finished the course.
when, before, after, as soon as, until, if (about the future)Simple Present inside that clauseI will call you when I reach.
always, usually, every day, generally, oftenSimple PresentHe walks to school every day.
at this time tomorrow, at 8 pm tonightFuture ContinuousAt this time tomorrow, I will be flying to Pune.

Stick this table above your study desk. When you practise, don't just read the sentence; physically underline the clue word and write the tense beside it. After two weeks of this, your brain starts doing it automatically, even under exam pressure.

One honest warning: already, just and yet are sometimes used with the simple past in American English, and nobody calls that wrong in daily life. But error-spotting papers follow the classic rule, so in the exam, pair them with the present perfect and you'll stay safe.

Three Questions Solved the Way You Should Solve Them in the Exam

Let me walk you through three questions in the usual SSC pattern, thinking aloud the way I'd want you to think in the hall.

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) The manager told us / (b) that he has completed the report / (c) before we arrived. / (d) No error.

The reporting verb "told" is past, so the verb in the "that" clause must step back. On top of that, "before we arrived" tells me the report was finished earlier than the arrival. Two clues, one verdict: "has completed" should be "had completed". Answer: (b).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) My brother is working / (b) in this company / (c) since 2018. / (d) No error.

The clue word is "since". Since needs a perfect tense, and "is working" is simple present continuous. Correct form: "has been working". Answer: (a).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) If the weather will be fine / (b) we shall go / (c) for a picnic tomorrow. / (d) No error.

"If" opens a condition about the future, and a condition clause takes the simple present. "Will be" is the trap. It should be "If the weather is fine". Answer: (a).

Notice what I did each time. I didn't hunt for a mistake randomly; I found the clue, named the tense it demands, and compared. That's the entire game.

The Y.A.L. - S.F. - W.I.B.A. Memory Shortcut

  • Y.A.L. = Yesterday, Ago, Last: these demand the Simple Past.
  • S.F. = Since, For: these demand a Perfect tense.
  • W.I.B.A. = When, If, Before, After: in future talk, the clause after them stays in the Simple Present.

Say it aloud three times: YAL, SF, WIBA. Screenshot it and keep it on your phone.

The 10 Tense Mistakes That Cost Marks (With Corrections)

Here are the ten errors I see most in practice papers, roughly in the order they trap students. Read each wrong sentence aloud first and notice how normal it sounds. That's exactly why it works as a trap.

10 Mistakes to Avoid
Mistake 1
✗ Wrong: I have seen him yesterday.
✓ Right: I saw him yesterday.
Yesterday is a finished-time word. The present perfect can never sit next to it.
Mistake 2
✗ Wrong: She is working here since 2019.
✓ Right: She has been working here since 2019.
Since describes a period that began in the past and reaches the present. Simple present continuous cannot carry that meaning.
Mistake 3
✗ Wrong: Did you finished the work?
✓ Right: Did you finish the work?
Did already carries the past. The main verb goes back to its base form.
Mistake 4
✗ Wrong: I will call you when I will reach home.
✓ Right: I will call you when I reach home.
After when, before, after and if, the future idea is expressed with the simple present.
Mistake 5
✗ Wrong: I am knowing the answer.
✓ Right: I know the answer.
Know is a state verb. State verbs rarely take the -ing form.
Mistake 6
✗ Wrong: He said that he will come tomorrow.
✓ Right: He said that he would come the next day.
A past reporting verb pulls the next verb back, so will becomes would.
Mistake 7
✗ Wrong: The teacher told us that the earth revolved around the sun.
✓ Right: The teacher told us that the earth revolves around the sun.
Universal truths stay in the simple present whatever the main verb is.
Mistake 8
✗ Wrong: The train already left when we reached the station.
✓ Right: The train had already left when we reached the station.
Of two past actions, the earlier one takes the past perfect.
Mistake 9
✗ Wrong: No sooner did the teacher enter than the students stood up.
✓ Right: No sooner had the teacher entered than the students stood up.
No sooner pairs with the past perfect, and the second half is joined by than.
Mistake 10
✗ Wrong: It is high time we start preparing.
✓ Right: It is high time we started preparing.
After it is (high) time, the verb takes a past form even though the meaning is about now.

Look at the ten errors again and you'll see they fall into small families. Mistakes 1, 2 and 8 are time-word clashes: a clue word is ignored. Mistakes 4, 6 and 7 are partner-clause clashes: the verb in one clause doesn't respect the other clause. Mistake 5 is a verb-type error, and mistakes 3, 9 and 10 are fixed-structure errors that you simply have to memorise once.

Instead of cramming ten separate rules, learn the three families. When a new sentence appears in the exam, ask yourself which family it might belong to. If you'd like every family explained with graded practice, my tense ebook is built around exactly this kind of pattern-based learning.

The 6-Step Method to Check Any Sentence in 15 Seconds

Speed comes from a routine, not from talent. Practise this six-step check on twenty sentences and it will become automatic.

Your 6-Step Method
1
Read for meaning
Read the whole sentence once without hunting. Know what is being said.
2
Underline time words
Mark yesterday, since, ago, by the time, when, if and every similar clue.
3
Circle every verb
Find the verb in each part. Note its tense and form.
4
Match verb to clue
Use the clue map. Does the verb obey the clue word?
5
Check the partner clause
Look at if, when, that and before/after. Do both clauses agree?
6
Check the verb form
Confirm V1, V2, V3 or -ing is correct before you pick No error.
ZeroErrorEnglishPro

The routine looks long on paper, but each step takes a second or two. The point is order: clue first, verb second, form last.

Practice Set with Answers

Try each question on your own first. Use the clue method before you read the answer, and note which clue you used. Then check your reasoning against the explanation.

Q1. (a) The students have submitted / (b) their projects yesterday / (c) to the principal. / (d) No error.

Answer: (a). Yesterday is a finished-time word, so the verb must be the simple past: The students submitted their projects yesterday. The present perfect cannot sit next to it.

Q2. (a) I am living in / (b) this city / (c) for ten years. / (d) No error.

Answer: (a). For ten years shows a period that reaches the present. The verb needs the present perfect continuous: I have been living in this city for ten years.

Q3. (a) When the police arrived, / (b) the thief already ran / (c) away from the spot. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). The thief escaped before the police came, so the earlier action takes the past perfect: the thief had already run away.

Q4. (a) He said that / (b) he will meet me / (c) the next day. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). The reporting verb said is past, so will steps back to would: he would meet me the next day.

Q5. (a) Our teacher told us / (b) that the Sun was / (c) a star. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). That the Sun is a star is a universal truth. It stays in the simple present even after a past reporting verb.

Q6. (a) I will inform you / (b) as soon as / (c) the result will come. / (d) No error.

Answer: (c). After as soon as, a future idea takes the simple present: the result comes.

Q7. (a) She is knowing / (b) the answer / (c) to every question. / (d) No error.

Answer: (a). Know is a state verb and does not take the continuous form. The correct phrase is she knows.

Q8. (a) No sooner did he reach / (b) the station / (c) than the train left. / (d) No error.

Answer: (a). No sooner pairs with the past perfect: No sooner had he reached the station than the train left.

A Pattern I See Again and Again in Coaching

Imagine a student, let's call him Amit, who knows all twelve tenses by heart. Ask him to write the structure of the present perfect continuous and he does it perfectly. Yet in a 25-question error-spotting set he misses most of the tense questions.

Why? Because Amit reads every sentence for meaning and only then looks for "something wrong". By the time he finishes reading, the clue word is already forgotten. His knowledge is fine; his order of attack is wrong.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple. For ten days, Amit underlines every time word before reading the sentence a second time, and writes the demanded tense above it in tiny letters. In the first few days he slows down. By day six or seven, he doesn't need the pencil any more; his eyes jump to the clue automatically. That's the change I want for you too. It's not a new rule; it's a new habit.

Expert Tips to Remember

  • Practise with a pencil. Underline clue words physically for the first two weeks; it trains the eye faster than thinking alone.
  • Keep an error log. Write the wrong sentence, the clue word and the correct version. Revise this single notebook every Sunday.
  • Prefer 15 to 20 sentences every day over 100 on one weekend. Habits form through repetition, not volume.
  • When both parts of a sentence look fine alone, check how they relate to each other. Many tense errors live in the relationship, not in either part.
  • Don't rush to No error. If your six-step check passes honestly, choose it. If you skipped step 6, go back.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Clue first, verb second: underline every time word before you judge the verb.
  • Yesterday, ago and last year never sit with the present perfect. Since and for never sit with the simple present.
  • After when, if, before, after and until, future ideas use the simple present.
  • A past reporting verb pulls the next verb back, except for universal truths.
  • State verbs such as know, own and belong do not take the continuous form.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which tense errors appear most often in SSC CGL error spotting?

I cannot give you an official count, but the same patterns keep returning: present perfect used with finished-time words, wrong sequence of tenses after a past reporting verb, and a future verb inside a when or if clause. Master these three and you have covered a large share of tense questions.

What is the fastest way to find a tense error?

Find the clue first. Underline every time word and partner clause, then check whether the verb obeys it. Reading for meaning first and hunting afterwards wastes time.

Can I use the present perfect with just now?

In exam grammar, just now means a moment ago and takes the simple past: I saw him just now. The present perfect goes with just alone: I have just seen him.

Is No error a safe answer when I am unsure?

Do not treat it as a last resort. Run the six-step check; if every clue is satisfied and the verb forms are correct, No error is the right answer. Guessing it blindly loses marks.

How many sentences should I practise daily for tenses?

Fifteen to twenty focused sentences with an error log is a practical target. Quality matters more than quantity, because analysing why you missed a question is where the learning happens.

Final Thoughts

Tense errors feel sneaky only until you know where they hide. They hide between a clue word and a verb, and now you know how to check that gap quickly. Start tomorrow: take ten sentences from any practice paper, underline the clues, and run the six steps.
