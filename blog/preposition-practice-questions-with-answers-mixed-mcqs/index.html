Preposition Practice Questions with Answers: Mixed MCQs for Class 12 and Competitive Exams

Practise 30 mixed preposition MCQs with answers and reasons for Class 12 and competitive exams, plus memory shortcuts, a cloze passage and a 7-day plan.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Point, Surface, Space | Fixed pairs beat logic | Check every preposition against its partner word

Reading about prepositions is easy. Using them under exam pressure is a different game. That's why the fastest way to improve is to practise a lot of questions, check every answer and learn from every mistake.

This post gives you exactly that: 30 mixed multiple-choice questions on prepositions, built for Class 12 students and for SSC, bank, railway and state exam aspirants. They cover time and place, appropriate prepositions, confusing pairs and error spotting. Every question has an answer and a reason, and there's a memory-shortcut cheat sheet in the middle so you can revise before the harder sets.

A note on the questions: they are original sentences written on common exam patterns. They are not copied from any particular year's paper, and I haven't attached any exam year to them. The patterns, however, are the ones that appear again and again.

How to use this practice set:
  1. Keep a notebook and a pen. Don't write in the post.
  2. Attempt each set without looking at the answers. As a rule of thumb, give each question 30 to 40 seconds.
  3. Check the answer key at the end and mark your score for each set.
  4. For every wrong answer, write the sentence and the correct pair in your notebook.
  5. Retake the whole set after three days. You'll be surprised how much you improve.

Five rules to read before you start

If you have a minute before the first question, read these five lines. They solve a surprising number of the questions below.

  1. Clock time takes at, a day or date takes on, and a month, year or season takes in.
  2. Fixed pairs beat logic. Learn the pair, not the sentence.
  3. Rank words (senior, junior, superior, inferior) and prefer take to.
  4. After a preposition, a verb takes -ing, so "looking forward to meeting".
  5. In error spotting, check every preposition against its partner word before choosing "No error".

Memory shortcuts cheat sheet (revise before Set B)

Before the harder sets, take two minutes to read these shortcuts. Screenshot the table if you like. Every shortcut here comes from the earlier posts in this series, gathered in one place.

TopicMemory shortcut
Time: at / on / inAT the clock, ON the calendar day, IN the long stretch (month, year, season).
Place: at / on / inP-S-S: Point = AT, Surface = ON, Space = IN.
Rank words and "prefer"Senior, junior, superior, inferior and prefer all take TO, never "than".
congratulate / thankOn the podium we congratulate: ON. For a favour we thank: FOR.
sufferPain comes FROM somewhere: suffer from.
insist / depend / relyStand firm ON your feet: insist on, depend on, rely on.
Verbs that need no helperD-C-R-E: Discuss, Comprise, Reach, Enter go straight to their object.
between / amongBeTWeen = TWo. AMONG = a MOB (many).
since / forNumber in front? FOR. Date or event? SINCE.
beside / besidesBeSIDE = by the side. BeSIDES = extra S for extra things.
by / untilBY = deadline. UNTIL = UNbroken stretch.
look forward to ___If TO is a preposition, -ING is the condition.

Set D: Error spotting (6 questions)

Each sentence is divided into parts (A), (B) and (C). Choose the part with the error. If there's no error, choose (D). For more on this type of question, see my guide on preposition error spotting.

  1. The principal discussed about (A) / the new rules (B) / with the teachers. (C) / No error (D)
  2. She is senior than (A) / me by (B) / two years. (C) / No error (D)
  3. He congratulated me (A) / for my (B) / excellent result. (C) / No error (D)
  4. I have been (A) / living here (B) / since ten years. (C) / No error (D)
  5. The teacher was angry (A) / with the students (B) / for being late. (C) / No error (D)
  6. We are looking forward (A) / to meet (B) / you next week. (C) / No error (D)

Bonus Set E: Mini cloze passage (8 blanks)

In a passage, the surrounding sentences give you extra clues, so read the whole paragraph before you start. Fill each blank with one preposition. This set is a bonus and isn't counted in the 30-question score.

Last Sunday, our school organised a science exhibition (1) ___ the main hall. The programme began (2) ___ 10 am, and students from many schools participated (3) ___ it. I was responsible (4) ___ one project, and my teacher congratulated me (5) ___ my effort. I had been preparing (6) ___ two weeks, and I was proud (7) ___ my team. We are looking forward (8) ___ the next exhibition.

Don't judge yourself by the total alone. Look at each set. If Set A is strong and Set B is weak, you know your next job: fixed pairs. If Set D is weak, the issue is usually the "No error" habit or missing the extra preposition. That's the kind of insight a score alone won't give you.

Keep an error log (it takes five minutes)

Every wrong answer is a free lesson. Make a three-column log in your notebook: the sentence, the correct answer and the reason in a few words. For example: "She is senior than me | senior to me | rank words take TO".

Read the log once a week. You'll notice that your mistakes repeat. Three or four pairs will appear again and again, and those are the ones to learn first. This works far better than doing a hundred new questions without looking back.

How Class 12 students and competitive aspirants should use this set

If you're a Class 12 student, use this set to build accuracy in writing. After each set, write five original sentences using the pairs you got wrong. Prepositions that you can use in your own sentences are the ones you'll use correctly in letters, essays and reports.

If you're preparing for SSC, banking, railway or state exams, focus on speed along with accuracy. Take Sets B, C and D together as a timed block of 22 questions. Set a timer for 12 to 15 minutes and aim to finish with a few minutes left for review. Then use the answer key to see which question types cost you time, not just marks.

Frequently asked questions

How many prepositions questions should I practise every day?

Ten is a good number. Solve them, check the answers and write down mistakes. Ten careful questions a day teach more than fifty rushed ones.

Should I read the options before I read the sentence?

Read the sentence first and think of your own answer. Then look at the options. This stops the options from steering you in the wrong direction.

What should I do if two options look correct?

Check the meaning. Ask whether it's a point or a length, two or many, movement or position. If it's a fixed pair, trust the pair, not your ear.

Is "No error" the right answer in error-spotting questions?

Sometimes. But treat it as a conclusion, not a default. Check every preposition with its partner word before you choose it.

How long should this practice set take?

As a rule of thumb, 15 to 20 minutes for all 30 questions. If you take longer at first, that's fine. Speed comes after accuracy.

Practice makes the pattern permanent

Thirty questions are only the start. Retake this set in three days, then again in a week. By the third attempt, most of these pairs will feel as natural as your own phone number. And if you keep your error log, you'll know exactly which pairs still need work.
