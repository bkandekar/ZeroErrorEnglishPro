Punctuation Error Spotting for SSC CGL and Bank Exams: Tricks, Examples and Practice

Learn punctuation error spotting with comma, apostrophe, quotation mark, colon and semicolon rules, exam-style examples, and practice questions.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
COMMA structure | APOSTROPHE possession/contraction | QUOTES exact words | COLON introduce | SEMICOLON connect clauses | END MARK sentence purpose

A punctuation mark can change the meaning or grammatical structure of a sentence. Learn to identify errors by checking the rule—not merely where you pause while reading.

1. Commas: identify the structure

Subject and verb: Do not separate an ordinary subject from its verb: “The candidates who qualified were selected.” Not “The candidates who qualified, were selected.”

Defining clauses: Essential identifying information normally takes no commas: “Students who registered early received confirmation.”

Non-defining clauses: Extra information is set off with commas: “My only brother, who lives in Pune, is an engineer.” Without the context “only brother,” the punctuation may change the meaning rather than simply be wrong.

Introductory elements: Use a comma when it improves clarity, especially after longer introductory clauses: “After finishing the examination, she left.” A comma after a short introduction is sometimes optional.

Lists: Both “pens, books and files” and “pens, books, and files” are valid in common styles. Use the serial (Oxford) comma when clarity requires it.

2. Apostrophes: possession and contractions

Singular: “the girl's bag.” Regular plural ending in s: “the girls' bags.” Irregular plural: “the children's books.”

“It's” means “it is” or “it has”; “its” is possessive. “Who's” means “who is” or “who has”; “whose” shows possession.

Write “the 1990s” for the decade, not “the 1990's.”

3. Quotation marks

Use quotation marks for direct speech: “She said, ‘I am ready.’” Use no quotation marks for ordinary reported speech: “She said that she was ready.”

Question marks belonging to quoted questions stay with the question: “Are you ready?” she asked. The placement of periods and commas with quotations varies by British and American style; follow the style required by your exam or publication.

4. Colons: introduce an explanation or list

“She packed three things: a pen, a notebook and a calculator.” The words before this colon form a complete independent clause.

Avoid a colon directly between a verb and its object in a normal sentence: “She wants a pen and a notebook,” not “She wants: a pen and a notebook.”

5. Semicolons: connect related independent clauses

Correct: “The paper was difficult; many students ran out of time.” Incorrect comma splice: “The paper was difficult, many students ran out of time.”

With a conjunctive adverb: “She revised carefully; therefore, she felt prepared.” A semicolon can also separate complex list items that already contain commas.

6. Full stops, question marks and exclamation marks

Direct question: “Where are you going?” Reported question: “He asked me where I was going.” A reported question normally ends with a full stop when the whole sentence is a statement.

Use exclamation marks for strong emotion or emphasis, not as a substitute for every full stop.

Quick memory formula

COMMA = sentence structure and lists; APOSTROPHE = possession or contraction; QUOTES = exact words; COLON = introduction; SEMICOLON = related independent clauses; END MARK = sentence purpose.

Five-step checking method

  1. Locate the punctuation or suspected missing mark.
  2. Identify the sentence structure.
  3. Check whether the rule is mandatory or style-dependent.
  4. Test common pairs: its/it's, whose/who's, defining/non-defining.
  5. Read the corrected sentence and confirm its intended meaning.

Practice: spot and correct the error

These are original exam-style exercises, not verified past-paper questions. Each item has an intended correction; context-dependent alternatives are noted.

  1. The candidates who qualified, were called for verification.
  2. Its important to check the instructions.
  3. She said, “I have finished my revision.
  4. The test was lengthy, many students left questions unanswered.
  5. My only brother who lives in Delhi is a doctor.
  6. The teachers room is on the second floor. (The room belongs to multiple teachers.)
  7. He asked me where was I going?
  8. She has three goals a stable job, financial security and further study.
  9. The dog wagged it's tail.
  10. The 1990's were a decade of change.

Answer key

  1. Remove the comma: “The candidates who qualified were called for verification.”
  2. “It's important to check the instructions.”
  3. Close the quotation: “She said, ‘I have finished my revision.’” (Matching quotation marks are essential.)
  4. Use a semicolon or full stop between independent clauses.
  5. “My only brother, who lives in Delhi, is a doctor.” The “only” makes the clause non-defining.
  6. “The teachers' room is on the second floor.”
  7. “He asked me where I was going.” Change word order and end punctuation.
  8. “She has three goals: a stable job, financial security and further study.”
  9. “The dog wagged its tail.”
  10. “The 1990s were a decade of change.”

Day 1: commas and clause types. Day 2: apostrophes. Day 3: quotations and colons. Day 4: semicolons and end marks. Day 5: timed mixed practice and review. Adjust the schedule to your progress.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Oxford comma compulsory?

Not universally. Usage depends on the required style, and the comma may be needed to prevent ambiguity.

How many punctuation questions appear in SSC CGL?

There is no fixed number to assume for every paper. Check the current official syllabus and recent papers for the format and coverage.

Is “it's” possessive?

No. “It's” is a contraction; “its” is possessive.

Can a semicolon replace a full stop?

Yes, between closely related independent clauses, when the connection is appropriate.

What is the best way to improve?

Practise identifying the sentence structure and review the rule behind each correction.
