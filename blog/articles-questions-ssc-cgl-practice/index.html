How Articles Are Tested in SSC CGL

The exact pattern and weightage can change from year to year, so treat this as a guide to formats, not a guarantee of what will appear. Articles usually show up inside one of three question types.

Fill in the blanks

A sentence has one or more blanks, and you choose the correct article from four options, which may include "no article" as a choice.

SSC-style practice question: He is ___ honest officer and ___ excellent leader.
(A) a / an   (B) an / a   (C) an / an   (D) a / a
Answer: (C). "Honest" has a silent h, and "excellent" starts with a vowel sound.

Error spotting

A sentence is divided into four parts, and you identify the part that contains a grammatical error, which is often an article.

SSC-style practice question: (A) She works as / (B) a university lecturer / (C) and gave us an / (D) useful advice.
Answer: (D). "An useful advice" should be "useful advice"; advice is uncountable and "useful" starts with a "y" sound anyway.

Sentence improvement

Part of a sentence is underlined, and you choose the best replacement from four options, including "No improvement".

SSC-style practice question: He secured a first rank in the entire district.
Answer: "the first rank". Ordinal numbers point to one specific position, so they take the.

For the underlying rules behind each of these formats, our complete guide to articles for SSC CGL and our page on article error spotting both cover the traps that show up most often across these three formats.

Cloze passages

A short passage has several blanks, each testing a different grammar point. One or two blanks are usually articles, decided by the surrounding sentence rather than the blank alone. This format rewards reading the sentence before and after the blank, not just the blank itself.

SSC-style practice question: Ramesh joined ___(1)___ new company last month. He is ___(2)___ hardworking employee who never misses ___(3)___ deadline.
Answers: (1) a, (2) an, (3) a. "New" and "deadline" start with consonant sounds; "hardworking" starts with a vowel sound.

Notice how blank 3 depends on "deadline" being a specific, singular countable idea used in a general sense, not a known one. Reading the full sentence, not just the word next to the blank, prevents that kind of miss.

Using Previous-Year Questions the Right Way

Previous-year questions are valuable because they show the exam's actual style, not because memorising them predicts future papers. Used the wrong way, they create a false sense of security; used the right way, they sharpen your instinct for how examiners phrase traps.

  • Do use them to notice patterns, such as how often error spotting hides the article mistake in the third part of a sentence rather than the first.
  • Do use them under timed conditions, not as untimed reading material.
  • Don't assume a topic won't repeat just because it appeared last year. Article rules are foundational, so they return often.
  • Don't rely on unverified sources. Many "previous year question" lists circulating online are actually rewritten or misattributed. If you can't confirm the year and paper, treat the question as practice material and label it that way yourself.

Because we haven't independently verified specific SSC CGL papers for this guide, every question below is labelled as an SSC-style practice question. If you have a set of confirmed previous-year questions you'd like added, they can be incorporated with the correct year and source.

A Simple Way to Build Your Own Practice Bank

Beyond ready-made questions, you can generate near-unlimited practice from anything you read: a newspaper editorial, a bank exam passage, even this guide. Pick five sentences containing articles, delete them, and try to fill them back in from memory before checking the original. This forces active recall instead of passive recognition, which is closer to what the actual exam demands.

20 Practice MCQs in 4 Sets of 5

Each set focuses on one article-related skill. Attempt a set, check it against the answers in the next section, then move to the next set only once you've reviewed your mistakes.

Set A: Fill in the Blanks (Basic)

  1. She bought ___ new laptop for her studies.
    (A) a   (B) an   (C) the   (D) no article
  2. ___ Taj Mahal is one of the seven wonders of the world.
    (A) A   (B) An   (C) The   (D) No article
  3. He is ___ honourable man in his village.
    (A) a   (B) an   (C) the   (D) no article
  4. Children need ___ proper guidance at this age.
    (A) a   (B) an   (C) the   (D) no article
  5. He plays ___ violin every evening after dinner.
    (A) a   (B) an   (C) the   (D) no article

Set B: Error Spotting

  1. (A) He is a / (B) honest boy and / (C) never tells / (D) a lie.
  2. (A) The Ganga and / (B) the Yamuna are / (C) longest rivers / (D) in northern India.
  3. (A) She travels to / (B) office by the / (C) metro every / (D) single day.
  4. (A) He lost / (B) his the temper / (C) during the heated / (D) discussion.
  5. (A) Ravi is / (B) an best student / (C) in his entire / (D) batch this year.

Set C: Sentence Improvement

  1. He is an university graduate with a first-class degree.
  2. She gave the committee a useful advice on the new policy.
  3. This is most unique opportunity offered to fresh graduates.
  4. He goes to the school by bus every morning as a student.
  5. The more you revise, better you become at spotting errors.

Set D: Mixed Practice

  1. ___ Himalayas are the youngest mountain range in the world.
    (A) A   (B) An   (C) The   (D) No article
  2. (A) He completed / (B) his MBA and / (C) joined a / (D) reputed firm. (Identify the error, if any, or mark "No error".)
  3. She has a little hope of clearing the exam this year. (Choose: No improvement / a few / little / the little)
  4. He was praised for showing ___ honesty rarely seen at his age.
    (A) a   (B) an   (C) the   (D) no article
  5. It was ___ one-sided contest from the very first over.
    (A) a   (B) an   (C) the   (D) no article

Answers and Explanations

Show all 20 answers
  1. (A) a. "New" begins with a consonant sound.
  2. (C) The. Names of specific monuments take the.
  3. (B) an. "Honourable" has a silent h.
  4. (D) no article. "Guidance" is uncountable and used in a general sense.
  5. (C) the. Musical instruments played in general take the ("play the violin").
  6. (A). Should be "an honest boy"; "honest" has a silent h.
  7. (C). Should be "the longest rivers"; a superlative needs the.
  8. (B). Should be "office by metro"; "by" plus a mode of transport takes no article.
  9. (B). Should be "his temper"; "his" already functions as a determiner, so it cannot combine with "the".
  10. (B). Should be "the best student"; a superlative needs the.
  11. Improved: "a university graduate". "University" begins with a "y" sound.
  12. Improved: "useful advice". Advice is uncountable and takes no article.
  13. Improved: "a most unique opportunity" or simply "a unique opportunity". "Unique" begins with a "y" sound and needs a, not "an".
  14. Improved: "goes to school". "Go to school" as a student takes no article.
  15. Improved: "the better you become". The pattern "the more..., the better..." needs the in both halves.
  16. (C) The. Mountain ranges take the.
  17. (D) No error. "His MBA" is correct; MBA is spoken with a leading vowel sound, but here it's preceded by the possessive "his", not an article, so there's no article to check.
  18. No improvement. "A little hope" correctly means "some hope"; "little hope" without a would mean almost no hope, changing the sentence's meaning.
  19. (B) an. "Honesty" starts with a vowel sound.
  20. (A) a. "One-sided" begins with a "w" sound.

If any set gave you more than one wrong answer, revisit the matching rule before your next attempt: Set A and D lean on the sound rule from a and an rules with examples, while Set B and C lean on the and zero article, covered in rules of the definite article and zero article rules.

Common Reasons Marks Are Lost Even With Correct Knowledge

Before moving to timing, it helps to name the non-rule reasons students lose marks on questions they could actually answer correctly.

  • Rushing the first read. Skimming a sentence once and answering from a half-formed impression, instead of reading it fully.
  • Anchoring on the first option that looks plausible, without checking the remaining three.
  • Second-guessing a correct instinct under time pressure, and changing a right answer to a wrong one.
  • Skipping the "No error" or "No improvement" option as a matter of habit, assuming every sentence must contain a mistake.

None of these are grammar problems. They're exam-temperament problems, and timed practice is what fixes them, not further rule revision.

Time Strategy per Question

Speed comes from a fixed routine, not from rushing. Use these rough targets while practising, then tighten them as you improve.

Question type Target time What to check first
Fill in the blanks10-15 secondsNoun type, then specific or general, then sound
Error spotting20-25 secondsScan each part for a missing, extra or wrong article before checking verbs and prepositions
Sentence improvement20-25 secondsRead the whole sentence once, not just the underlined part

If a question takes noticeably longer than its target, mark it and move on. Coming back with fresh eyes after finishing the rest of the section is usually faster than staring at one question.

Keep an Error Log

A simple three-column log turns random mistakes into a focused revision list. After every practice set, note only the questions you got wrong.

Question Rule broken One-line fix
Set A, Q3Silent hSay the word aloud before choosing a or an
Set C, Q13"Y" soundUnique, university, useful all take "a"

Within a week, this log usually reveals one or two rules causing most of your mistakes. That's your real revision priority, not the entire rule set again from scratch.

A 14-Day Practice Plan

  • Days 1-2: Re-read the core rules for a/an, the and zero article. Attempt Set A and B untimed, then review every mistake.
  • Days 3-4: Attempt Set C and D with a timer. Start your error log.
  • Days 5-6: Revisit only the rules tied to your logged mistakes. Redo the questions you got wrong.
  • Days 7-8: Fresh timed set, mixing all three question formats. Log new mistakes.
  • Days 9-10: Focus practice entirely on your two weakest rules from the log.
  • Days 11-12: Timed mixed practice again, aiming to beat your Day 7 accuracy.
  • Day 13: Full revision of all four rule groups in one sitting.
  • Day 14: One final timed set, treated like exam conditions, no pausing mid-question.

Quick Revision

Before your next practice set:

  • Fill in the blanks: check noun type, then specific or general, then sound.
  • Error spotting: scan every part for a missing, extra or wrong article first.
  • Sentence improvement: read the full sentence, not only the underlined portion.
  • Log every mistake with its rule, not just its correct answer.
  • Revise the two or three rules causing most of your errors, not the whole topic equally.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How can I solve article questions in SSC CGL quickly?

A: Follow a fixed routine for each question type: for fill in the blanks, check noun type, specificity and sound in order; for error spotting, scan each part for an article issue before anything else; for sentence improvement, read the entire sentence, not only the underlined section.

Q: Are these previous-year questions?

A: No. Every question in this guide is an SSC-style practice question written to match the exam's usual pattern. None is presented as a verified previous-year question.

Q: How many practice questions should I attempt daily?

A: A focused set of 10 to 15 timed questions, followed by a careful review of mistakes, is usually more useful than 50 questions attempted without review.

Q: What is an error log, and why does it help?

A: An error log records the rule behind each mistake, not just the correct answer. Over a week or two, it shows exactly which one or two rules are costing you the most marks, so your revision time goes where it matters.

Q: Should I focus on speed or accuracy first?

A: Accuracy first. Practise untimed until your answers are consistently correct, then add a timer. Speed built on top of frequent mistakes only produces fast wrong answers.

Q: Do cloze passages test articles differently from standalone fill in the blanks?

A: The underlying rules are the same, but cloze passages require reading the surrounding sentences, not just the blank, because context there decides whether a noun is specific or general.

Q: Can I create my own article practice questions?

A: Yes. Take any short passage, remove the articles, and try to restore them from memory before checking against the original text. This kind of active recall builds the same skill the exam tests.
