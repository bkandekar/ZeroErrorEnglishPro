Why punctuation deserves a complete book

Punctuation looks like one topic. It is really eight topics wearing one coat: the period, the comma, the semicolon, the colon, the dash, the hyphen, quotation marks and the apostrophe. Each has its own rules and its own exceptions. A mistake in any one of them costs the whole answer in error-spotting or descriptive writing.

And exams do not test the topic in just one form. You may be asked to correct a sentence, choose the correctly punctuated option, spot the error in a long passage or write a precise paragraph. To be ready for all of these, you need three things that a single blog post cannot give you: a clear order of learning, a large amount of practice and one place to revise from.

A good book gives you all three. It teaches the rules in sequence, so you never meet a hard idea before you are ready for it. It gives you drills right after each idea, so you test yourself while the idea is fresh. And it gives you one thing to open the week before an exam, instead of twenty browser tabs.

Memory tricks from the week (screenshot this)

Here's every shortcut from this week's posts in one table. Save it, and read it aloud once a day. These tricks are the heart of how I teach.

What to rememberMemory shortcut
Period / Full stopSTOP the thought. Incomplete thoughts never get a period.
CommaPAUSE = COMMA. Lists, openers, interrupters, non-restrictive clauses.
Oxford commaLIST = OXFORD. Always put the final comma before "and" or "or".
SemicolonJOIN related ideas. Two independent clauses that belong together.
ColonINTRODUCE. Lists, explanations, quotations that follow a complete clause.
Em dashEMPHASIZE. Sudden break, strong interruption or dramatic addition.
HyphenCONNECT. Compound adjectives before a noun and certain prefixes.
ApostropheOWN or CONTRACT. Possession or missing letters. Never for plurals.
Quotation marksQUOTE the exact words. Comma and period go inside in American style.
ParenthesesASIDE. Extra information that can be removed without breaking the sentence.

Quick readiness check: 5 questions

Correct the punctuation in these sentences without looking at anything. This is a quick check, not a test.

  1. The candidates who scored above ninety percent were given certificates however the others were asked to reappear.
  2. She said I will submit the form tomorrow.
  3. Its important to revise the rules before the exam.
  4. He bought pens pencils notebooks and an eraser.
  5. The report which was submitted late was rejected.
Answers:
1) The candidates who scored above ninety percent were given certificates; however, the others were asked to reappear.
2) She said, "I will submit the form tomorrow."
3) It's important to revise the rules before the exam.
4) He bought pens, pencils, notebooks and an eraser.
5) The report, which was submitted late, was rejected. (or without commas if the clause is restrictive)

If you got all five: excellent. Keep revising with the shortcuts, and use the book for extra practice and exam-style applications.
If you got three or four: you have a good base, with a few gaps. Focused practice is exactly what fills them.
If you got two or fewer: reread this week's free posts, then move to the book for the full system.

How to judge any grammar book (including mine)

Before you spend money on any study material, ask these four questions. I'd want you to ask them about my book too.

  1. Does it explain in simple language? If you need a dictionary to understand the rule, it's the wrong book.
  2. Does it give examples for every idea? A rule without a sentence is just a slogan.
  3. Does it let you practise, with answers? Reading builds understanding. Practice with solutions builds accuracy.
  4. Does it fit your exam? A general English book is different from one built around the patterns your exam uses.
