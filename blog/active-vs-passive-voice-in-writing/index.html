Active vs Passive Voice in Writing: When Each One Works Best

Active vs passive voice in writing for UPSC and GRE: when each works best, the accountability trap, and how to fix overused passive sentences.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Active = direct, accountable, confident | Passive = unknown/unimportant doer, process-focused

Grammatically Correct Isn't the Same as Well Written

Every sentence in this series so far has asked one question: is this passive sentence grammatically correct? This guide asks a different one: should this sentence be passive at all? Both voices are correct English. The real skill, especially for UPSC essays, GRE analytical writing, and any descriptive or subjective exam paper, is choosing the one that actually serves your sentence.

This guide covers what changes when you pick one voice over the other, when each one genuinely works best, the accountability trap that passive voice can fall into, how academic and formal writing uses passive differently from everyday writing, and a practical test for catching overuse in your own drafts.

Quick answer: Use active voice for clear, direct, confident writing, especially in essays and argument-based answers. Use passive voice when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or when the process or result matters more than who performed it. Neither voice is "better"; the test is whether it serves the sentence's purpose.

What Changes When You Choose a Voice

This is a genuinely different question from everything covered so far in this series. The earlier guides asked whether a passive sentence was built correctly. This one asks whether building it as passive was the right call in the first place, a judgment call that depends on purpose and audience, not on a fixed rule.

The grammatical meaning of "The committee rejected the proposal" and "The proposal was rejected by the committee" is identical. What changes is emphasis, length and tone.

  • Emphasis: active voice puts the doer first, so attention lands on who acted. Passive voice puts the receiver first, so attention lands on what happened to it.
  • Length: active sentences are almost always shorter, since passive adds a be verb and often a "by" phrase.
  • Tone: active voice tends to read as direct and confident; passive voice tends to read as formal, distant, or neutral.

Neither effect is automatically good or bad. The question is always whether that effect suits what you're trying to say in that specific sentence.

When Active Voice Works Best

Active voice is the default choice for most writing, and especially for argument-driven, persuasive or narrative writing, because it's shorter, clearer and assigns responsibility directly.

  • Essays and argument answers: "The government introduced this policy to curb inflation" reads more confidently than "This policy was introduced by the government to curb inflation."
  • Narrative and descriptive writing: "The farmers protested against the new law" is more vivid than "The new law was protested against by the farmers."
  • Instructions and recommendations: "The committee should implement this reform" is clearer and more direct than "This reform should be implemented by the committee."

In GRE Analytical Writing specifically, graders consistently reward clear, direct sentences. A paragraph built almost entirely from active sentences reads as confident and easy to follow, which supports a stronger overall impression even when the ideas themselves are identical to a passive-heavy version.

When Passive Voice Works Best

Passive voice isn't a weaker version of active voice; it has genuine, specific jobs that active voice can't do as well.

  • The doer is unknown: "The shop was robbed last night." You don't know who did it, so there's no active subject to put first.
  • The doer is obvious or unimportant: "The results were announced yesterday." Everyone understands an exam board or official body announced them; naming them adds nothing.
  • The process or result matters more than the performer: "The samples were heated to 100 degrees for ten minutes." In a lab report, the procedure is the point, not who personally heated the sample.
  • You want to avoid repeating the same subject: "The bill was passed by the assembly and was signed into law the same week." Keeping "the bill" as the subject throughout keeps the paragraph's focus steady.

The Accountability Trap

Because passive voice can drop the doer entirely, it's sometimes used, deliberately or not, to avoid naming who's responsible for something. "Mistakes were made" is the textbook example: it describes an error without saying who made it.

Compare:

"Mistakes were made during the audit." (vague, no one is responsible)

"The finance team made errors during the audit." (clear, direct, accountable)

In exam writing, this matters most in essays that discuss policy failures, institutional responsibility, or cause-and-effect arguments. If your argument depends on identifying who is responsible for an outcome, passive voice can accidentally blur exactly the point you're trying to make. A simple check: if a passive sentence hides who's responsible for something your argument needs to pin down, rewrite it in active voice.

Passive Voice in Formal and Academic Writing

Scientific, technical and some formal administrative writing traditionally favours passive voice more than everyday prose does, for a specific reason: the convention emphasises the method or finding over the individual researcher.

  • "The solution was heated and then filtered." (standard lab report style, process-focused)
  • "It was observed that the reaction rate increased with temperature." (findings-focused, common in research writing)
  • "The application must be submitted within thirty days." (formal and procedural, doer is "anyone who applies", which doesn't need naming)

UPSC answer writing often benefits from a mix: active voice for your own arguments and recommendations, with passive voice reserved for describing established facts, processes or widely known events where naming the doer adds no value. GRE AWA tasks, on the other hand, usually reward a stronger lean toward active voice, since the task is explicitly to argue a position, not describe a neutral process.

A Quick Test for Overuse

Passive voice becomes a problem only when it's used by default rather than by choice. Run this two-part test on any paragraph you've written.

  1. Count your passive sentences. If more than a third of your sentences are passive, that's often a sign of habit rather than deliberate choice.
  2. For each passive sentence, ask: could I name the doer, and would naming it add anything? If you can easily name the doer and doing so would strengthen the sentence, rewrite it in active voice.

This test isn't a strict rule; some topics (scientific processes, historical events with no single clear actor) naturally produce more passive sentences. The goal is noticing the pattern, not eliminating passive voice entirely.

Rewriting Weak Passive Sentences

Here are three real patterns of overused passive voice, each rewritten for more direct, confident prose.

Weak: It is believed by many economists that the reform will reduce unemployment.
Stronger: Many economists believe the reform will reduce unemployment.

Weak: The need for better infrastructure is felt by rural communities across the state.
Stronger: Rural communities across the state need better infrastructure.

Weak: A decision was taken by the panel after the proposal was reviewed by them.
Stronger: The panel reviewed the proposal and took a decision.

Notice that each "stronger" version is also shorter. That's not a coincidence: removing an unnecessary passive construction usually removes words along with it, which is part of why active voice tends to read as more confident.

A Full Paragraph, Before and After

Style choices add up across a paragraph, not just within a single sentence. Here's a short UPSC-style paragraph with heavy, habitual passive voice, followed by a revised version.

Before: It is believed that the scheme was poorly implemented. Funds were allocated late, and delays were caused as a result. It was also noted that monitoring was not conducted regularly by the concerned department.

After: Critics believe the government implemented the scheme poorly. Officials allocated funds late, causing delays. The concerned department also failed to monitor progress regularly.

The revised paragraph is shorter, names who did what, and reads with more confidence, exactly the qualities that strengthen an argument-based answer. Notice that passive voice wasn't removed entirely; it simply gave way to active voice wherever naming the doer added clarity or accountability.

6 Style Mistakes to Avoid

6 Style Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Defaulting to passive out of habit

Writing "The report was completed by me" instead of "I completed the report" adds words without adding meaning. Default to active unless you have a specific reason to choose passive.

Mistake 2: Hiding a weak argument behind passive voice

"It is widely agreed that..." sounds authoritative but names no source. If you can cite who agrees, naming them in active voice makes the claim more credible, not less.

Mistake 3: Avoiding passive even when it's the natural choice

Forcing "Someone built the Taj Mahal in the seventeenth century" instead of "The Taj Mahal was built in the seventeenth century" is awkward. When the doer is genuinely unknown or irrelevant, passive is the natural, correct choice.

Mistake 4: Mixing voices awkwardly within one sentence

"The team analysed the data and the report was then written" switches voice mid-sentence for no reason. Keep related actions in the same voice: "The team analysed the data and then wrote the report."

Mistake 5: Losing the subject across a long paragraph

Chaining several passive sentences with different implied doers makes a paragraph hard to follow. If every sentence needs a different "by" phrase to make sense, the paragraph likely needs more active sentences to stay clear.

Mistake 6: Using passive to dodge responsibility in an argument

In an essay evaluating policy failure, "Targets were not met" is weaker than "The ministry failed to meet its targets" if your argument is specifically about who should be held accountable.

10 Practice Sentences

Unlike earlier practice sets in this series, there's no single "correct" grammar answer here; several sentences genuinely work either way. What matters is the reasoning behind your choice.

For each sentence, decide which voice works better for the stated context, and briefly say why. These are practice sentences for style analysis, not grammar correction exercises.

  1. (Essay argument) "It is thought by some that the policy has failed." vs "Some critics believe the policy has failed."
  2. (Lab report) "We heated the mixture for ten minutes." vs "The mixture was heated for ten minutes."
  3. (News report, doer unknown) "Someone vandalised the monument last night." vs "The monument was vandalised last night."
  4. (Recommendation) "It is suggested that the budget be increased." vs "We suggest increasing the budget."
  5. (Historical fact) "Ashoka built this edict in the third century BCE." vs "This edict was built in the third century BCE."
  6. (Accountability) "Errors were found in the report." vs "The auditors found errors in the report."
  7. (Formal notice) "Applicants must submit the form by Friday." vs "The form must be submitted by Friday."
  8. (Narrative) "The soldiers defended the fort for three days." vs "The fort was defended by the soldiers for three days."
  9. (Process description) "Technicians calibrate the instrument every morning." vs "The instrument is calibrated every morning."
  10. (Opinion with source) "Experts say the economy will recover by next year." vs "It is said that the economy will recover by next year."
Show suggested answers
  1. Active ("Some critics believe..."). Names the source, which strengthens the claim in an argument essay.
  2. Either works; "We heated" is common in instructional writing, "The mixture was heated" is standard in formal lab reports. Passive fits a formal report better.
  3. Passive. The doer is genuinely unknown, so passive is the natural, correct choice.
  4. Active ("We suggest..."). Shorter and more direct; useful in a recommendation you're personally making.
  5. Active. The doer (Ashoka) is specific, known and historically significant; naming him adds real information.
  6. Active ("The auditors found..."). Useful when the argument depends on who identified the problem.
  7. Either works; both are common in formal notices. Active names who must act; passive emphasises the requirement itself.
  8. Active. More vivid for narrative writing; keeps the soldiers as the focus of the sentence.
  9. Either works; passive is common when the routine matters more than who performs it.
  10. Active ("Experts say..."). Naming the source makes the claim more credible than the vague "it is said that".

Quick Revision

Choosing a voice on purpose:

  • Default to active voice for essays, arguments and recommendations.
  • Use passive when the doer is unknown, obvious or unimportant.
  • Use passive for process-focused or formal/scientific description.
  • Check whether a passive sentence is hiding responsibility your argument needs to name.
  • If more than a third of your paragraph is passive, check each sentence for a reason, not just a habit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is passive voice bad for UPSC essay writing?

A: Not inherently. Active voice generally reads as more direct and confident, which usually serves argument-driven essay writing well. Passive voice still has a place for describing established facts or processes where naming the doer adds nothing.

Q: Does GRE Analytical Writing penalise passive voice?

A: There's no fixed penalty for using passive voice correctly. However, since the task asks you to build and support an argument, a strong lean toward active voice generally produces clearer, more direct prose, which tends to read better overall.

Q: How much passive voice is too much?

A: There's no fixed number, but if more than roughly a third of your sentences in a paragraph are passive, it's worth checking whether each one is a deliberate choice or just a habit.

Q: Why is "mistakes were made" considered a weak sentence?

A: Because it describes an error without naming who is responsible for it. In writing where accountability matters, such as policy analysis, this vagueness can work against the clarity of your argument.

Q: Why does scientific writing use more passive voice than other writing?

A: The convention emphasises the method, process or finding over the individual researcher, since the result is meant to be reproducible and evaluated on its own merit rather than on who personally performed the procedure.

Q: Should every passive sentence in my essay be rewritten as active?

A: No. The goal isn't eliminating passive voice; it's using it on purpose. Keep passive sentences where the doer is genuinely unknown, unimportant, or where the process matters more than the performer, and rewrite the ones that are passive only out of habit.

Q: How do I check my own writing for overused passive voice?

A: Read each sentence and ask whether you could easily name the doer and whether doing so would add anything. If both answers are yes, and the sentence is still passive, that's a candidate for rewriting into active voice.
