Transition Words and Phrases: 60 Linking Words With Examples

Learn 60 transition words and phrases in nine groups, with example sentences, punctuation rules, memory tricks and a practice set with answers.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Ask how the ideas relate -> Pick a word from that group -> Punctuate it correctly

Read these two versions of the same idea. First: “It rained all day. We stayed home. We played cards.” Now: “It rained all day, so we stayed home and played cards.” Same facts, but the second one has a small word, “so,” that tells the reader how the ideas connect.

Words like that are called transition words, and they’re the glue of good writing. Without them, your sentences sit side by side like strangers. With them, your reader moves smoothly from one idea to the next, and your essays, emails and exam answers sound clear and confident.

Transition Words With Example Sentences

Quick answer: Transition words (and transitional phrases) connect one idea to the next. Pick the word that matches the relationship between the two ideas: addition (moreover), contrast (however), cause and result (therefore), time or order (then), example (for instance) or conclusion (in short).

Here is a quick reference you can scan in a minute. Every row has an example sentence, so you can see the word working.

Purpose and words Example sentence
Addition
also, moreover, in addition, furthermore		 She is intelligent. Moreover, she works hard.
Contrast
however, on the other hand, nevertheless, yet		 The test was hard. However, most students passed.
Cause and result
therefore, as a result, consequently, thus		 It rained all day. Therefore, the match was cancelled.
Time and order
first, then, later, meanwhile, finally		 First, read the question. Then, check the options.
Example
for example, for instance, such as		 Some words confuse students. For instance, affect and effect.
Conclusion
in conclusion, to sum up, in short		 In short, regular practice beats last-minute study.

How to choose the best transition in 3 steps

1. Read both ideas, one after the other.
2. Ask: how is the second idea related to the first? Is it an addition, a contrast, a result, a time order or an example?
3. Pick the word for that relationship, and put a comma after it.

Punctuation: where does the comma go?

At the start of a sentence, use a capital letter and then a comma:
✓ Rahul was tired. However, he kept studying.

Between two sentences joined by a semicolon, put the semicolon before the transition and a comma after it:
✓ Rahul was tired; however, he kept studying.

Try it: choose the best transition

  1. Rohan studied hard. ______, he failed the test.
    (A) Therefore  (B) However  (C) Moreover
  2. The road was blocked. ______, we took a longer route.
    (A) However  (B) For instance  (C) As a result
  3. She loves painting. ______, she plays the violin.
    (A) However  (B) In addition  (C) Finally
👉 Tap to see the answers
  1. (B) However. Studying hard and failing are opposite results, so you need a contrast word.
  2. (C) As a result. The blocked road caused the longer route.
  3. (B) In addition. The second sentence adds another fact about her.

Why transition words matter

They show relationships. A transition tells the reader whether the next idea adds, contrasts, explains or concludes.

They improve flow. Readers feel the writing is organised even when the ideas are complex.

They help in exams. Essay, letter and paragraph-writing tasks reward clear links between ideas.

There’s one catch. A transition word only works if it matches the real relationship. “However” in front of an idea that adds to the previous one confuses readers more than no transition at all. So your first job is to name the relationship, then pick the word.

The ASK, PICK, PUNCTUATE routine

“ASK. PICK. PUNCTUATE.”

ASK how the new idea relates to the last one: add, contrast, cause, result, time, example, emphasis, conclusion or condition
PICK a word from that group
PUNCTUATE correctly: comma after an opening transition, semicolon before however-type words that join two sentences

Say the three words whenever you write a sentence that follows another. It takes a few seconds, and it keeps your writing honest.

Group 1: Addition (8 words)

Use these when the new idea adds to the last one.

Word or phraseExample
andShe sings, and she plays the guitar.
alsoHe is also a skilled painter.
in additionIn addition, the hotel offers free breakfast.
moreoverThe plan is cheap; moreover, it is quick.
furthermoreThe book is clear. Furthermore, it is short.
besidesI don’t want to go. Besides, it’s late.
as well asShe teaches maths as well as science.
what is moreIt’s cheap, and what is more, it’s durable.

Memory trick: “Add means plus.”

Group 2: Contrast (8 words)

Use these when the new idea goes against the last one.

Word or phraseExample
butI tried, but I failed.
howeverIt was late; however, we kept working.
althoughAlthough it rained, we played.
thoughHe is rich, though he lives simply.
yetShe was tired, yet she smiled.
neverthelessThe road was closed; nevertheless, we arrived on time.
on the other handTea is cheap. On the other hand, coffee is richer.
in contrastRahul loves cricket. In contrast, his brother prefers chess.

Memory trick: “Contrast means but.” Never use although and but together in one sentence.

Group 3: Cause and reason (6 words)

Use these to explain why something happened.

Word or phraseExample
becauseWe stayed in because it was raining.
sinceSince you’re here, let’s begin.
asAs it was late, we left.
because ofThe match was delayed because of fog.
due toThe delay was due to fog.
owing toOwing to heavy traffic, he was late.

Memory trick: “Because of, due to and owing to are followed by a noun. Because, since and as are followed by a clause.”

Group 4: Result (7 words)

Use these to show what happened because of something.

Word or phraseExample
soIt was cold, so I wore a jacket.
thereforeHe worked hard; therefore, he passed.
thusThe data was incomplete; thus, the result was unclear.
as a resultIt rained all day. As a result, the match was cancelled.
consequentlyShe missed the bus; consequently, she was late.
henceThe tickets are cheap; hence, they sell fast.
accordinglyWe received the new rules and acted accordingly.

Memory trick: “Result means so.” Use so, therefore or as a result when one thing causes another.

Group 5: Time and sequence (9 words)

Use these to put events in order.

Word or phraseExample
firstFirst, wash the rice.
nextNext, add the water.
thenThen, cover the pot.
after thatAfter that, wait ten minutes.
meanwhileMeanwhile, prepare the sauce.
finallyFinally, serve it hot.
beforeCheck your answers before you submit.
afterShe called after she reached home.
eventuallyEventually, the rain stopped.

Memory trick: “First, next, then, finally: count on your fingers.”

Group 6: Example (5 words)

Use these to give a case that supports your point.

Word or phraseExample
for exampleMany fruits, for example, mangoes, are seasonal.
for instanceSome habits, for instance, reading, take time.
such asI like fruits such as mangoes and guavas.
namelyTwo cities were hit, namely Pune and Nashik.
in particularI enjoy sports, in particular, cricket.

Memory trick: “Such as sits inside the sentence. For example needs commas.”

Group 7: Emphasis (5 words)

Use these to stress an important point.

Word or phraseExample
indeedIt was indeed a difficult exam.
in factHe isn’t lazy. In fact, he works harder than anyone.
above allAbove all, stay calm.
especiallyI love fruits, especially mangoes.
clearlyClearly, the plan needs changes.

Memory trick: “Emphasis turns up the volume.” Use it once or twice per page.

Group 8: Conclusion (6 words)

Use these to close an argument or a paragraph.

Word or phraseExample
in conclusionIn conclusion, practice is the key.
to sum upTo sum up, plan, practise and revise.
overallOverall, the course was useful.
in shortIn short, we need more time.
all in allAll in all, it was a good trip.
in summaryIn summary, the results were positive.

Memory trick: “Conclusion comes last. Don’t introduce new ideas after it.”

Group 9: Condition and comparison (6 words)

Use these to set a condition or show similarity.

Word or phraseExample
ifIf you study, you will pass.
unlessUnless you hurry, you will miss the train.
similarlyRahul works hard. Similarly, his sister studies late.
likewiseShe helped me; likewise, I helped her.
in the same wayPlants need water. In the same way, people need rest.
otherwiseLeave now; otherwise, you will be late.

Memory trick: “Unless already means if not, so don’t add another not.”

Three punctuation rules

Rule 1: Comma after an opening transition. “However, we kept working.” “For example, mangoes are seasonal.”

Rule 2: Semicolon before a however-type word that joins two sentences. “It was late; however, we kept working.” A comma alone would create a splice.

Rule 3: Comma before and, but, so and yet when each side is a full sentence. “I tried, but I failed.”

Memory trick: “However needs a semicolon in front and a comma behind.” For more, read my guides on run-on sentences and comma splices, semicolon vs colon and comma rules.

Mistakes to avoid

Using two connectors for one relationship. “Although it rained, but we played” is wrong. Keep one: “Although it rained, we played.”

Joining sentences with however and a comma. “He was tired, however he kept working” is a comma splice. Use a semicolon before however.

Picking the wrong relationship. Moreover adds, and however contrasts. Mixing them confuses the reader.

Overusing transitions. One or two per paragraph is plenty. Too many make writing feel mechanical.

A short paragraph makeover

Here’s a paragraph with no transitions. Read it aloud and notice how it jerks along.

Before: Online classes are convenient. Students attend from home. Some students find it hard to focus. Teachers record the lessons. Students can watch again. Online classes are useful.

Now ask, pick, punctuate. The first two sentences add, the third contrasts, the fourth and fifth add and give a result, and the last concludes.

After: Online classes are convenient because students attend from home. However, some students find it hard to focus. Fortunately, teachers record the lessons, so students can watch them again. Overall, online classes are useful.

Four transitions did the work, and nothing was lost. Notice that I also joined two sentences into one with because and so, which ties back to combining sentences using clauses.

Practice set: choose the best transition

Choose the best word in each sentence. These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. It was raining. ____, we played football. (However / Therefore)
  2. She studied hard; ____, she passed. (therefore / however)
  3. I like fruits ____ mangoes. (such as / for instance,)
  4. ____ you hurry, you will miss the train. (Unless / Because)
  5. He was tired. ____, he kept working. (Nevertheless / Moreover)
  6. First, mix the flour. ____, add the eggs. (Next / Although)
  7. The match was cancelled ____ rain. (because of / because)
  8. The room is cheap. ____, it is clean. (Moreover / However)
  9. ____ the bus was late, we walked. (Since / Although)
  10. Study daily; ____, you will forget. (otherwise / therefore)
  11. He is a good player. ____, he is a great captain. (In addition / In contrast)
  12. ____, practice is the key. (In conclusion / Meanwhile)

Answers

1. However (contrast: rain, but they played)

2. therefore (result of studying hard)

3. such as (inside the sentence, no comma)

4. Unless (condition: if you don’t hurry)

5. Nevertheless (contrast: tired, but kept working)

6. Next (sequence)

7. because of (followed by a noun: rain)

8. Moreover (adds a second good point)

9. Since (reason: the bus was late)

10. otherwise (condition: if you don’t study daily)

11. In addition (adds to the previous point)

12. In conclusion (closing the idea)

If you got 11 or 12 right, you’re ready to use transitions in your own writing. If you got 7 to 10, revisit the groups you missed. If you got fewer than 7, go back to the routine and retake the set after two days.

Frequently asked questions

What are transition words?

Transition words are words and phrases that connect ideas, such as however, therefore and for example. They show the reader how one sentence or paragraph relates to the next.

What is the difference between however and but?

But is a conjunction that joins two clauses in one sentence, as in “I tried, but I failed.” However is an adverb. It can start a sentence, or follow a semicolon, as in “I tried; however, I failed.”

Do I need a comma after a transition word?

Yes, when the transition opens a sentence or joins two sentences after a semicolon, as in “However, we kept working.” Short words like so and then often need no comma.

How many transition words should I use?

One or two per paragraph is usually plenty. Too many make writing feel mechanical, so use them only when the relationship needs to be made clear.

Are transition words useful in exams?

Yes. Essay, letter and paragraph-writing tasks reward clear links between ideas, and sentence-joining questions test them directly. Check the latest official notification for your exam pattern.

What are transition words?

Transition words are words that connect one idea or sentence to the next and show how the two are related. Examples are however (contrast), therefore (result), moreover (addition) and finally (order).

What is the difference between transition words and transitional phrases?

Transition words are single words, such as however or therefore. Transitional phrases are groups of words doing the same job, such as on the other hand, as a result or for example. Both are used in the same way and take a comma after them at the start of a sentence.

How do you use transition words in a sentence?

First decide how the second idea relates to the first: addition, contrast, result, time order or example. Then choose the matching word and place it at the start of the second sentence, followed by a comma: "The test was hard. However, most students passed."
