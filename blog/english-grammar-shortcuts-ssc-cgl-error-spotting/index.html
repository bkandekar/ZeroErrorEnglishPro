25 English Grammar Shortcuts for SSC CGL Error Spotting (With Memory Tricks)

By Balu Kandekar · Published 2026-10-26 · 13 min read

Read this error spotting question and decide in ten seconds: (A) He is living / (B) here since 2015 / (C) with his family. / (D) No error.

Most students pick (D). The sentence sounds natural, and nothing looks wrong. But the error is in part (A), and it has a name: the wrong tense with since. The sentence needs “has been living,” not “is living.”

That’s how error spotting works in SSC CGL. You rarely fail because you never learned the rule. You fail because you couldn’t recall it in the eight seconds you had. A rhyme or a two-word reminder fires fast, and a paragraph of theory doesn’t.

This guide gives you 25 grammar shortcuts, each with a memory trick, a wrong sentence and its correction, plus a scan method called TRAPS and a practice set. After 22 years of teaching, I’ve found that students who carry short triggers beat students who carry long rulebooks. How grammar shortcuts are tested in exams

Grammar hides inside three familiar formats, and knowing them tells you where to look.

1. Error spotting. A sentence is split into parts (A), (B), (C), and (D) is “No error.” The error sits in one small word.

2. Sentence improvement. One part is underlined, and the options differ by a tense, an article or a preposition.

3. Cloze and fill in the blanks. Single blanks test articles, prepositions and verb forms.

A note on the examples in this post: every sentence is an original practice sentence on a common exam pattern. None of them is copied from a particular year’s paper, and I haven’t attached any year to them. The patterns are what repeat, and the patterns are what you should learn.

The TRAPS scan: one memory trick for every question

You can’t recall twenty-five rules one by one under the clock. You need one routine that tells your eyes where to go.

“Spot the TRAPS.”

T = Tense signals (since, ago, when, if, had)
R = Right verb for the subject (agreement)
A = Articles (a, an, the, or none)
P = Prepositions (at, on, in, to, with)
S = Special patterns (comparisons, pronouns, parallelism, voice, speech, tags, conditionals)

Run the five letters in order on every sentence, and say them silently. The routine takes about eight seconds and stops you from reading only for meaning. Subject-verb agreement shortcuts (1 to 5)

Agreement is the most reliable scorer in error spotting, so we start here.

Shortcut 1 (R): The Near-Neighbour Rule

✗ Neither the students nor the teacher were present.
✓ Neither the students nor the teacher was present.

With either…or, neither…nor and not only…but also, the verb agrees with the subject closer to it. Memory trick: “The nearest neighbour hears the verb first.”

Shortcut 2 (R): Along With Is a Passenger

✗ The captain, along with his players, were honoured.
✓ The captain, along with his players, was honoured.

Along with, together with, as well as and in addition to don’t change the subject. The verb follows the first subject. Memory trick: “Along with is a passenger. Only the driver steers the verb.”

Shortcut 3 (R): Each and Every Are Loners

✗ One of the boys are absent.
✓ One of the boys is absent.

Each, every, either, neither and one of the take a singular verb. Memory trick: “Each and Every walk alone, so they take a single verb.”

Shortcut 4 (R): The Five S-Wearing Singles

✗ Mathematics are my weakest subject.
✓ Mathematics is my weakest subject.

News, mathematics, physics, economics and politics end in s but take singular verbs in exam questions. Memory trick: “Five S-wearing singles: news, maths, physics, economics, politics.”

Shortcut 5 (R): The Number vs A Number

✗ The number of applicants are rising.
✓ The number of applicants is rising.

The number of takes a singular verb. A number of takes a plural verb, as in “A number of applicants are waiting outside.” Memory trick: “The number is alone. A number is a crowd.”

Tense shortcuts (6 to 9)

Tense errors hide behind small signal words. Spot the signal and the answer follows. For the most confusing pair, see present perfect vs past simple exam traps.

Shortcut 6 (T): Since Marks the Start, For Marks the Stretch

✗ He is living here since 2015.
✓ He has been living here since 2015.

Since goes with a point in time and for goes with a period. Both need a perfect tense, not the simple present. Memory trick: “Since is a Starting point. For is a Frame of time.”

Shortcut 7 (T): Ago and Yesterday Kick Out Has and Have

✗ I have seen him yesterday.
✓ I saw him yesterday.

A finished-time marker such as yesterday, last year, ago or in 2010 needs the simple past, never the present perfect. Memory trick: “Ago and yesterday push has and have out of the room.”

Shortcut 8 (T): Will Stays Home in the When-Clause

✗ I will call you when he will arrive.
✓ I will call you when he arrives.

After if, when, as soon as, before, after and until, a future meaning uses the simple present in that clause. Memory trick: “Will stays home in the when-clause.”

Shortcut 9 (T): First Action Gets Had

✗ When I reached the station, the train left.
✓ When I reached the station, the train had left.

When two past actions happen one after the other, the earlier one takes the past perfect. Memory trick: “First action, first Had.”

Article shortcuts (10 to 12)

Articles look easy and still cost marks every year. The full rule sets are in my posts on A, An, The rules and the zero article.

Shortcut 10 (A): Ears Decide, Not Eyes

✗ He is an university student.
✓ He is a university student.

Use an before a vowel sound, not a vowel letter. So it is an hour, an honest man, a university, a European. Memory trick: “Ears decide, eyes don’t.”

Shortcut 11 (A): Best, First, Only Need The

✗ She is most intelligent girl in class.
✓ She is the most intelligent girl in class.

Superlatives, ordinals and the word only take the. Unique things like the sun and the moon do too. Memory trick: “Best, First, Only: The has to be there.”

Shortcut 12 (A): Lunch, Tennis and Hindi Need No The

✗ He plays the cricket every evening.
✓ He plays cricket every evening.

Don’t use the before meals, games, languages and subjects. Memory trick: “Lunch, Tennis, Hindi: no The, no worry.”

Preposition shortcuts (13 to 16)

Prepositions can’t be reasoned out, so memory tricks matter most here. Keep my guide to fixed prepositions after verbs handy for longer lists.

Shortcut 13 (P): At Is a Dot, On Is a Table, In Is a Box

✗ She was born in 5 May at 2001.
✓ She was born on 5 May in 2001.

Use at for a point (6 am, the door), on for days, dates and surfaces, and in for months, years and enclosed spaces. Memory trick: “At is a dot. On is a table. In is a box.” More examples are in the in, on, at rules post.

Shortcut 14 (P): Twin Es Mean Two

✗ The prize was divided between five students.
✓ The prize was divided among five students.

Between is for two, among is for three or more. Memory trick: “Between has twin Es, and twins mean two.”

Shortcut 15 (P): Senior, Junior, Superior Say To

✗ This phone is superior than that one.
✓ This phone is superior to that one.

Senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior and prefer take to, never than. Memory trick: “Senior, Junior, Superior, Inferior, Prior: all say to.”

Shortcut 16 (P): Agree To a Thing, With a Who

✗ I agree with your proposal.
✓ I agree to your proposal.

Agree to goes with a proposal or plan. Agree with goes with a person. Memory trick: “To is the Thing. With is the Who.” For a longer list, see appropriate prepositions with verbs, adjectives and nouns.

Comparison, pronoun, voice, speech, tag and conditional shortcuts (17 to 25)

These nine shortcuts form the S in TRAPS. They’re separate topics, but each one has a clear trigger.

Shortcut 17 (S): Fewer You Can Count, Less You Can Pour

✗ There are less students in the hall.
✓ There are fewer students in the hall.

Use fewer with countable nouns and less with uncountable ones. Memory trick: “Fewer friends, less fuss.”

Shortcut 18 (S): One Comparison, One Marker

✗ He is more smarter than his brother.
✓ He is smarter than his brother.

Never use more with an -er word or most with an -est word. Memory trick: “One comparison, one marker.”

Shortcut 19 (S): Elder Has No Than

✗ My brother is elder than me.
✓ My brother is older than me.

Elder and eldest are only for family members and are never followed by than. Use older. Memory trick: “Elder never meets than.”

Shortcut 20 (S): Match the Coaches

✗ She likes dancing and to sing.
✓ She likes dancing and singing.

Items joined by and, or or but must be in the same grammatical form. Memory trick: “In a train, the coaches must match.”

Shortcut 21 (S): Prepositions Pull Me

✗ This is between you and I.
✓ This is between you and me.

After a preposition, use the object form of a pronoun: me, him, her, us, them. Memory trick: “Between you and me: a preposition always pulls me.” Pronoun traps with who, which and that are in relative clauses rules.

Shortcut 22 (S): Be Is Best Friends With V3

✗ The work was finish yesterday.
✓ The work was finished yesterday.

In every passive sentence, a form of be is followed by the past participle. Memory trick: “Be’s best friend is V3.” Tense-wise charts are in the active and passive voice guide.

Shortcut 23 (S): Past Said Pushes Tense Back

✗ He said that he is tired.
✓ He said that he was tired.

When the reporting verb is in the past, the tense in the reported speech moves one step back. Memory trick: “Said in the past? Tense goes to the past.” See the tense changes in direct and indirect speech chart.

Shortcut 24 (S): Opposites Attract in Question Tags

✗ She is a doctor, is she?
✓ She is a doctor, isn’t she?

A positive statement takes a negative tag and a negative statement takes a positive tag. Memory trick: “Opposites attract.” Special cases like I am and let’s are in question tag rules.

Shortcut 25 (S): Had in If, Would Have in Result

✗ If I would have known, I would have told you.
✓ If I had known, I would have told you.

For an unreal past, use if plus past perfect in one half and would have plus V3 in the other. Memory trick: “Had in the If. Would Have in the Result.” Mixed patterns are in third and mixed conditionals.

The 5-step error spotting method

TRAPS tells you what to check. This five-step routine tells you how to run the question from start to finish.

Step 1: Read the sentence once for meaning.

Step 2: Run TRAPS on each part. Tense, Right verb, Articles, Prepositions, then Special patterns.

Step 3: Name the shortcut. If something feels wrong, say which of the 25 shortcuts it matches.

Step 4: Test your fix. Rewrite the part in your head with the correction.

Step 5: Only then choose “No error.” Treat it as a conclusion, never a default. If all five TRAPS checks pass, “No error” is the answer.

A 20-second time plan

Here’s a plan that works for most students. Adjust the seconds to suit your pace.

0 to 5 seconds: read the sentence once.

5 to 13 seconds: run TRAPS on every part.

13 to 18 seconds: name the shortcut and test your fix.

18 to 20 seconds: mark the answer and move on. If you’re stuck, skip it and return.

Don’t spend a minute on one question. Also glance at commas and apostrophes, because small punctuation slips show up too. My post on punctuation error spotting covers that part.

A pattern I see in almost every batch

Here’s something that repeats year after year in my classes. A student can explain the since and for rule perfectly on the board, then picks “No error” on a sentence that says “is living here since 2015.” The knowledge is there. The scan isn’t.

The fix is a habit, not more theory. Take five shortcuts a day, say each memory trick aloud, then solve ten mixed questions. After five days, revise all 25. That routine trains your eyes to land on the trigger word first.

Expert tips for exam day

Learn the rule first, then the trick. If you only remember “nearest neighbour,” you won’t know it applies to either…or and not to and.

Look for the trigger. Every shortcut has a trigger word or pattern. If it isn’t there, the shortcut doesn’t apply.

Trust the rule over your ear. “It sounds fine” is why most grammar errors survive.

Keep an error log. Write down every shortcut you miss, and revise that list for a week.

Practice set: 6 error spotting questions

Find the part with the error, or choose (D) if there’s none. These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. Neither the manager nor (A) / his assistants has reached (B) / the office. (C) / No error (D)
  2. She has completed (A) / her course (B) / two years ago. (C) / No error (D)
  3. He is senior than me (A) / by three (B) / years. (C) / No error (D)
  4. Each of the players (A) / have received (B) / a medal. (C) / No error (D)
  5. I will inform you (A) / as soon as he (B) / will reach the office. (C) / No error (D)
  6. Between you and me, (A) / the plan (B) / seems risky. (C) / No error (D)

Answers

1. (A): with neither…nor, the verb follows the nearer subject, so use “have.” (Shortcut 1)

2. (A): “ago” needs the simple past, so use “completed.” (Shortcut 7)

3. (A): senior takes to, so use “senior to me.” (Shortcut 15)

4. (B): “each” takes a singular verb, so use “has.” (Shortcut 3)

5. (C): no will in an as soon as clause, so use “reaches.” (Shortcut 8)

6. (D): no error. After a preposition, “me” is correct. (Shortcut 21)

If you scored 5 or 6, you’re ready for harder questions. If you scored below 5, revise the shortcuts you missed and retake the set after two days.

Frequently asked questions

How many grammar shortcuts do I need for SSC CGL error spotting?

You don’t need hundreds on day one. These 25 cover patterns that repeat often. Master them, then add more rules as your practice papers show new gaps.

Are grammar shortcuts enough, or do I need the full rules?

Shortcuts are triggers, not replacements. Learn the rule once, attach a memory trick to it and use the trick under time pressure. If a trick fails on an odd sentence, go back to the rule.

How should I practise these shortcuts every day?

Pick five shortcuts a day. Read the rule, say the memory trick aloud, then solve ten mixed error-spotting questions. After five days, revise all 25.
