Notice how the table is organized in a specific order: basic polarity first, then the exceptions that override it (hidden negatives), then the irregular fixed forms that don't follow any flip-logic at all, then tricky subjects, then modal verbs, and finally sentence-structure rules for anything longer than a single clause. That order matters — it's the same order you should check a sentence in, top to bottom, whenever you're unsure of a tag.

There's a reason a single consolidated table works better for revision than re-reading three or four separate explainer posts one after another. Spaced-out learning across multiple sittings builds initial understanding well, but final-week revision needs something different: retrieval practice, where you actively pull the rule from memory rather than simply recognizing it on the page. A dense table forces exactly that — your eye jumps from row to row, and each row is a small retrieval test in itself, even before you get to the formal practice questions below.

Common Mistakes at a Glance

Missing hidden negatives is the single most repeated error. Words like "nobody," "never," and "hardly" quietly flip a sentence's polarity without a visible "not" appearing anywhere, and most aspirants only scan for "not" itself, missing the equivalent negative meaning carried by these other words entirely.

Dropping a modal for do-support is the second most common mistake. When a statement already contains "can," "will," "should," or "must," the tag has to reuse that exact modal — "you can swim, don't you?" should always be "can't you?" instead. This slip happens most often when students are moving quickly and default to the more familiar do/does/did pattern out of habit.

Tagging the wrong clause rounds out the top three, and it only shows up once sentences get longer than a single clause. The fix is always the same: find the one truly independent clause in the sentence — ignoring any subordinate, reason, or if-clause completely — and apply every other rule to that clause alone.

Together, these three categories account for the overwhelming majority of question-tag errors in competitive exams. If your practice results below cluster around one of these three, that's exactly where your revision time is best spent.

15 Free Practice Questions with Solutions

Fill in each blank yourself before reading the solution beneath it — resist the urge to check the answer first, since testing your own recall is what makes this diagnostic useful rather than just another reading exercise. The 15 questions below deliberately cover every category from the summary table, roughly in the same order, so a wrong answer tells you precisely which row to revisit.

1. She works hard, ____?
Answer: doesn't she? — Positive statement → negative tag, matching "works."

2. Nobody answered the phone, ____?
Answer: did they? — "Nobody" is a hidden negative → positive tag.

3. I am invited to the ceremony, ____?
Answer: aren't I? — Fixed irregular form for "I am."

4. Open the window, ____?
Answer: will you? — Imperative sentence takes "will you?"

5. Let's finish this today, ____?
Answer: shall we? — "Let's" always takes "shall we?"

6. Everyone submitted the form, ____?
Answer: didn't they? — "Everyone" takes "they" in the tag.

7. This is your final answer, ____?
Answer: isn't it? — "This" takes "it" in the tag.

8. You should apologize, ____?
Answer: shouldn't you? — Modal "should" repeats in the tag.

9. He hardly speaks in meetings, ____?
Answer: does he? — "Hardly" is a hidden negative → positive tag. This is the same category as "never" and "nobody" — none of them contain the word "not," which is exactly why they're so easy to miss under time pressure.

10. Although it was raining, they continued the match, ____?
Answer: didn't they? — Tag matches the main clause, not the "although" clause. "Although" signals a subordinate clause every time, regardless of where it sits in the sentence.

11. He left early, but she stayed, ____?
Answer: didn't she? — Tag matches the nearest clause and its subject "she," not "he" from the earlier clause — a compound sentence with two different subjects is exactly where this rule gets tested most.

12. If the train arrives late, we will miss the connection, ____?
Answer: won't we? — Tag matches the main clause, never the if-clause, even though the if-clause has its own perfectly valid subject and verb.

13. The committee has approved the plan, ____?
Answer: hasn't it? — Collective noun treated as a single unit.

14. You've never been late before, ____?
Answer: have you? — "Never" makes the statement negative → positive tag.

15. Unless you apologize, she won't forgive you, ____?
Answer: will she? — "Unless" clauses follow the same rule as "if" clauses; tag the main clause.

If you got through all 15 without looking back at the table, treat your first attempt as the honest baseline — the score box below only means something if the attempt was genuinely cold. If you found yourself peeking at the table partway through, that's fine too; just note which questions needed a peek, since those are effectively the same signal as a wrong answer for diagnostic purposes.

Expert Shortcuts Toppers Actually Use

1. Test before you study, not after. Taking a practice set cold reveals your actual weak categories far more accurately than re-reading rules you already feel confident about, since confidence and accuracy don't always line up.

2. Revise from a single summary table, not scattered notes. Having every rule in one place, side by side, makes patterns and exceptions easier to compare and remember together — you start noticing, for instance, that "everyone" and "this" both feel singular but take opposite tag pronouns, which is a far stickier memory than learning each rule in isolation.

3. Group your errors by category, not by individual question. Two wrong answers about hidden negatives point to one gap to fix, not two separate problems.

4. Re-test the same weak category daily until it stops producing errors. A category that repeats correctly across three different days is genuinely learned, not just recently reviewed.

5. Use this exact format — summary plus timed practice — for your other grammar topics too. The revision method matters more than the specific topic; it works for tenses, prepositions, and clauses just as well.

6. Keep your own running list of "personal traps." Beyond the 10 rule categories in this guide, everyone accumulates a few individual sentence patterns that trip them up specifically — writing those down separately, alongside the standard categories, closes gaps no generic guide can fully anticipate.
