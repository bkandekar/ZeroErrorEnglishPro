Tense Fill in the Blanks: 5 Clues to Pick the Right Tense in 10 Seconds

Learn 5 clues to solve tense fill in the blanks fast: time markers, second verbs, if patterns, verb type and option elimination, with solved examples.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Check in order: Time word, Second verb, If pattern, Verb type, Options. Predict the tense before reading the options.

Fill in the blanks looks like the friendliest question type in any English paper. Four options, one blank, no hunting for errors. So why do so many aspirants lose marks here, especially in tense questions?

Because they read the options first. Four tense forms sit in front of them, all of them look plausible, and the brain starts to guess by sound. A guess based on sound works about as well as a coin toss when the options are only one rule apart.

The fix is to flip the order. Look for the clue in the sentence, decide the tense in your head, and only then look at the options. In this post I'll give you five clues that decide almost every tense blank, a pattern table you can revise in minutes and solved questions in the exam style. Why Tense Blanks Cost Marks Even When You Know the Rules

Think about what a tense blank really tests. It doesn't test whether you know the twelve tenses. It tests whether you can choose among them under time pressure, and that choice always depends on a clue somewhere in the sentence.

When you read the options first, the clue becomes invisible. You see "has gone", "went", "had gone" and "was going", and your mind starts replaying how each one sounds. The clue, for example by the time, sits quietly at the start of the sentence, ignored.

The five-clue method works because it moves your attention away from the options and towards the sentence. Each clue takes only a couple of seconds, and the order is deliberate: the clues that settle the most questions come first.

Clue 1: Time Markers

This is the clue that settles the largest number of questions. Scan the sentence for words that fix a time: yesterday, ago, last year, in 2019, since, for, already, yet, by the time, by next month, at this time tomorrow.

Each of them points to one family of tenses. Yesterday, ago and last year point to the simple past. Since and for point to the present perfect. By the time and by next month point to the perfect tenses, and at this time tomorrow points to the future continuous. One quick scan, and two of the four options usually disappear.

If you've read Day 1 of this series on error spotting, you already know the clue map. The same map works here, only the job is to build the answer instead of finding the error.

Clue 2: The Second Verb or Clause

Many sentences have no time word, but they have a second verb that sets the tense of the blank. This is where relationships between clauses matter. The blank has to agree with its partner.

Here are the pairs that appear most often in practice papers.

PatternThe blank takesExample
Interrupted action: when plus a past simple verbPast continuous for the longer actionShe was cooking when the phone rang.
By the time plus a past simple verbPast perfect in the other clauseBy the time we reached, the film had started.
Hardly ... whenInverted past perfect after hardlyHardly had he sat down when the bell rang.
No sooner ... thanInverted past perfect after no soonerNo sooner had she left than it began to rain.
If plus past simplewould plus base verbIf I had more time, I would learn French.
If plus past perfectwould have plus V3If he had worked harder, he would have passed.
Since plus a past simple clausePresent perfect in the main clauseShe has lived here since she was ten.
It is the first timePresent perfectIt is the first time I have seen snow.

Learn these eight patterns and you will solve a surprising number of blanks without even reading the options. Pay special attention to patterns 3 and 4: the helping verb had comes before the subject, which is an inversion. Examiners love it because students forget the word order.

Clue 3: The If Pattern

If a sentence contains if, wish or unless, the tense of the blank follows a conditional pattern. The three patterns you must know are: If + present, will + base verb (real future), If + past, would + base verb (unreal present) and If + past perfect, would have + V3 (unreal past).

Notice the last two are about unreal situations: something that isn't true or didn't happen. In an unreal if clause with the verb be, exam English uses were for all subjects: "If I were you", "If she were here". Using was in an exam answer, even though it's common in speech, risks the mark.

Clue 4: Verb Type and Habit Words

If the blank has no time marker and no second verb, look at the verb itself. Is it a state verb (know, belong, like), or an action verb? Is there a habit word such as always, usually or every day?

Habit words and state verbs lead to the simple present. "She always ______ early" wants a simple present form. And a state verb refuses the continuous, so an option like "is knowing" can be eliminated immediately. If Day 4 of this series is still fresh, this clue will feel like an old friend.

Clue 5: Option Elimination

Only now do you look at the options. Because you've already predicted the tense, you're not choosing from four. You're confirming one and eliminating the rest.

Cross out options that break the basic form first: a verb that doesn't agree with its subject, a V2 form where V3 is needed after had, or an -ing form after a modal. Then compare what's left with the tense you predicted. If two options remain, go back to the clue and decide whether the action is completed or continuing, finished or still true.

This final clue also protects you against careless errors. Many students predict the right tense and then pick an option with the wrong form, like "had went" instead of "had gone". A five-second form check saves the mark.

Four Questions Solved the Five-Clue Way

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: By the time we reached the station, the train ______. (a) left (b) had left (c) has left (d) was leaving

Clue 1: by the time plus a past verb (reached). The train's departure happened earlier, so the blank needs the past perfect. Answer: (b) had left.

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: She ______ here since 2015. (a) works (b) is working (c) has been working (d) worked

Clue 1: since 2015. A period reaching the present needs a perfect tense. Only (c) fits. Answer: (c).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: If I ______ you, I would accept the offer. (a) am (b) was (c) were (d) will be

Clue 3: if plus would means an unreal present. For unreal if clauses with be, exam English takes were. Answer: (c).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: Hardly ______ the room when the lights went out. (a) he entered (b) did he enter (c) had he entered (d) he has entered

Clue 2: hardly ... when. This pattern takes the past perfect with inversion. Answer: (c) had he entered.

The T.S.I.V.O. Ladder

  • T = Time word. Scan for yesterday, since, by the time.
  • S = Second verb. Does another clause set the tense?
  • I = If pattern. Which of the three conditional types?
  • V = Verb type. State verb or habit word?
  • O = Options. Only now, eliminate and confirm.

Climb the ladder from T to O, one rung at a time, and look at the options last.

The 5 Fill in the Blanks Mistakes That Cost Marks

These five are the sentences students most often complete wrongly, even when they know the rule. Read each wrong version and spot which clue was ignored.

5 Mistakes to Avoid
Mistake 1
✗ Wrong: By the time he arrived, we left.
✓ Right: By the time he arrived, we had left.
By the time with a past verb needs the past perfect for the earlier action.
Mistake 2
✗ Wrong: She is living here since 2015.
✓ Right: She has been living here since 2015.
Since needs a perfect tense, not a simple present continuous.
Mistake 3
✗ Wrong: If I was you, I would apply for that job.
✓ Right: If I were you, I would apply for that job.
In an unreal if clause, exam English uses were for all subjects.
Mistake 4
✗ Wrong: Hardly he had entered the room when the lights went out.
✓ Right: Hardly had he entered the room when the lights went out.
Hardly takes the inverted past perfect, with had before the subject.
Mistake 5
✗ Wrong: It is the first time I saw snow.
✓ Right: It is the first time I have seen snow.
It is the first time takes the present perfect.

Each mistake above ignores one specific clue from the ladder. Mistake 1 ignores the second verb, mistake 2 ignores the time word, mistake 3 ignores the if pattern, and mistakes 4 and 5 ignore fixed patterns you have to learn once. That's the real value of the ladder: when you get a question wrong, you can name the clue you skipped.

Practice Set with Answers

Try each question on your own first. Use the clue method before you read the answer, and note which clue you used. Then check your reasoning against the explanation.

Q1. I ______ my homework when you called. (a) did (b) was doing (c) have done (d) do

Answer: (b). The called action interrupted a longer background action, which needs the past continuous: was doing.

Q2. He ______ the guitar since he was ten. (a) plays (b) played (c) has played (d) is playing

Answer: (c). Since plus a past clause needs the present perfect in the main clause: has played.

Q3. If I ______ rich, I would buy a big house. (a) am (b) was (c) were (d) will be

Answer: (c). An unreal if clause with be takes were in exam English.

Q4. No sooner ______ the bell rung than the students ran out. (a) has (b) had (c) did (d) was

Answer: (b). No sooner pairs with the past perfect: no sooner had the bell rung than.

Q5. I will call you as soon as I ______. (a) will arrive (b) arrive (c) would arrive (d) am arriving

Answer: (b). After as soon as, a future idea takes the simple present: arrive.

Q6. By next March, she ______ her course. (a) completes (b) completed (c) will have completed (d) has completed

Answer: (c). By next March points to a future deadline, so the future perfect will have completed is correct.

Q7. It is the first time she ______ a plane. (a) flies (b) flew (c) has flown (d) is flying

Answer: (c). It is the first time takes the present perfect: has flown.

Q8. At this time tomorrow, we ______ to Delhi. (a) travel (b) will be travelling (c) travelled (d) have travelled

Answer: (b). At this time tomorrow describes an action in progress at a future moment, so the future continuous fits.

The Habit That Costs Marks

Picture a student, let's call him Dinesh, who attempts fifty fill in the blanks in a timed practice session. He reads the sentence quickly, jumps to the options and picks whichever sounds best. By the end, he scores less than he expected, and he can't explain why.

His coach asks him to do something different for the next session. Before he looks at any option, he has to write one word next to the blank: the tense he expects. It slows him down at first. In the third session he notices that he no longer needs to write it; his mind lands on the tense almost automatically, and the options simply confirm it.

What changed wasn't Dinesh's knowledge. It was the order in which he used it. That's the whole message of this post: the clue first, the options last.

Expert Tips to Remember

  • Cover the options with your hand or a pencil while you read the sentence. Predict first, check later.
  • Time yourself. Aim for about ten to fifteen seconds per tense blank in practice, then adjust to your own pace.
  • Keep a pattern notebook with the eight clause pairs from this post. Add one new pattern each time you meet it.
  • When two options both look right, ask: finished or continuing, completed or still true? That usually separates them.
  • After every practice set, label each wrong answer with the clue you missed: T, S, I, V or O.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Predict the tense before you read the options.
  • Climb the ladder in order: Time word, Second verb, If pattern, Verb type, Options.
  • Learn the eight clause pairs, especially hardly and no sooner with the past perfect.
  • In unreal if clauses with be, exam English uses were for all subjects.
  • Label every wrong answer with the clue you missed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all tense blanks have a time clue?

No. When there is no time word, look for a second verb or a clause pattern such as if, hardly or no sooner. If neither is present, the verb type or meaning decides, and that is where the simple tenses usually come in.

Is If I was you accepted in exams?

In everyday speech people say it, but exam papers expect If I were you. For an unreal condition with the verb be, were is the safe choice for every subject.

How do I choose between the simple past and the present perfect?

Look for the time word. A finished time (yesterday, in 2019, ago) needs the simple past. A period that reaches now (since, for, so far) needs the present perfect.

How many seconds should one tense blank take?

Ten to fifteen seconds is a practical target in practice, but accuracy comes first. Speed arrives naturally once the clue order becomes a habit.

What if two options both seem correct?

Return to the clue and test the meaning. Decide whether the action is completed or continuing, and whether it connects to the present. Usually only one option satisfies the clue completely.

Final Thoughts

Tense blanks stop being risky once you stop trusting your ear and start trusting a routine. Time word, second verb, if pattern, verb type, options: five clues, in that order. Practise the ladder on twenty questions this week and you'll feel the difference.
