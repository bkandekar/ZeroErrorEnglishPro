Why 30 Days Is Enough (and the Math Behind It)

Thirty days sounds short for a topic with hundreds of word partners. Here's why it works.

The core list of fixed prepositions that exams repeat is around 400 pairs. Over roughly 24 learning days, that's about 13 to 15 new pairs a day. Learn 13 pairs by family, with a trick and a sentence for each, and it takes about 30 to 40 minutes. The remaining days handle revision, mock tests and exam practice. You aren't memorising a dictionary. You're building a habit, one small bite at a time.

Why not cram in one week? Because memory doesn't work that way. Pairs learned in one sitting fade within days. Pairs learned over a month, with spaced revision, stay. That's the whole secret behind this plan.

Before You Start: Your Notebook and Your Day 1 Check

Two things before Day 1.

First, buy a cheap notebook. Call it your Mistake Notebook. Every pair you get wrong goes in, with the correct partner and a sentence of your own. This notebook will be more valuable than any list, because it contains only your weak spots.

Second, read the basics once. If you're new to the topic, read my guide on what fixed prepositions are and how to stop forgetting them. It takes ten minutes and makes the rest of the plan easier.

Then take the 10-question self-check at the end of this post. Note your score. You'll repeat it on Day 14 and Day 30, and watching the number climb is the best motivation you'll get.

Your 60-Minute Daily Routine

On learning days (Days 3 to 21), spend about an hour. Here's how to split it.

MinutesActivityWhat to do
10RecallWrite five pairs from yesterday without looking. Check them.
20LearnStudy today's family. Read the pairs aloud twice and attach a trick or picture to the hard ones.
15WriteWrite one sentence of your own for each tough pair, about your own life.
15PractiseDo 10 to 15 questions on today's pairs. Put every miss in your notebook.

Notice that only a third of the hour is new learning. The rest is recall and practice. That's deliberate, because recall is what builds memory.

A Sample Day: Day 3 Step by Step

Abstract plans are easy to ignore, so here's what Day 3 (adjectives with OF) really looks like.

  1. Recall (10 minutes): write the five tricks from Day 2 and ten pairs you learned. Check them.
  2. Learn (20 minutes): read the OF family: afraid of, scared of, terrified of, fond of, proud of, jealous of, ashamed of, aware of, capable of, guilty of. Say each aloud twice. Tell yourself the family idea: feelings and states take OF.
  3. Write (15 minutes): write one sentence for each pair that felt hard, such as "I'm terrified of lizards, but I'm proud of my notes."
  4. Practise (15 minutes): do 10 fill in the blanks questions on OF pairs. Put every miss in your notebook with the right partner.

That's it. Nothing fancy, and nothing you can't repeat tomorrow. The plan works because every day looks like this one.

If You Only Have 30 Minutes a Day

Working a job or studying other subjects? Use the 30-minute version: 5 minutes of recall, 15 minutes of learning with sentences, and 10 minutes of practice. Stretch the plan to 45 days if you need to, but don't skip the recall step. Even on the lazy days, five minutes with your notebook keeps the habit alive.

The Revision Schedule That Stops Forgetting

Revision is where most plans fail, so schedule it. Every pair you learn gets revised on Day +1, Day +3, Day +7 and Day +21. That sounds complicated, but it's simple in practice:

  • Day +1: the five-pair recall at the start of each session.
  • Day +3 and +7: the weekly revision on Days 7, 14 and 21.
  • Day +21: the notebook review on Days 29 and 30.

If a pair keeps slipping, don't blame yourself. Give it a bigger picture or a funnier sentence, and drag it back into the next recall session.

5 Memory Tricks to Use Every Single Day

These five tricks run through the whole plan. Learn them on Day 2 and use them daily.

Trick 1: Learn the pair, never the preposition alone

Write "afraid of", not "of". Say it as one chunk. When the chunk sounds familiar, the answer jumps out.

Trick 2: Use word families

Fear words take OF. Skill words take AT. Answer words take TO. Learn the family and you learn five pairs at once.

Trick 3: Draw a silly picture

Depend on: a child standing on her father's shoulders. Free from: a bird flying out of a cage. Silly pictures stay longer than sensible ones.

Trick 4: Remember DREAM and SSJI

DREAM verbs need no preposition: Discuss, Reach, Enter, Attend, Marry. SSJI words take TO, never than: Superior, Senior, Junior, Inferior.

Trick 5: Write one sentence about your own life

"My brother is jealous of my phone." A sentence that carries your own memory sticks far better than a textbook example.

How Fixed Prepositions Appear in SSC, Bank and Railway Exams

Fixed preposition pairs appear in English sections across SSC CGL, SSC CHSL, bank exams such as IBPS PO and Clerk, and state PSC papers. Railway exams vary by post, so check your notification to see whether an English or language section is included for your post. Where English is tested, fixed prepositions typically show up in four places:

  • Error spotting: one wrong preposition hides in an otherwise correct sentence.
  • Fill in the blanks: four prepositions, all plausible, only one correct.
  • Sentence improvement: the underlined part has the wrong partner.
  • Cloze test: several pairs hide in one passage.
Common Exam Pattern: Error Spotting
The manager insisted (A) / for the immediate (B) / submission of the report. (C) / No error (D)
Answer: A. It should be "insisted on".

The fix is always the same: spot the key word, name its partner, and check the preposition after it. That five-second habit, built over 30 days, is what separates students who guess from students who know.

Day 1 Self-Check: 10 Questions

Take this test now, note your score, and repeat it on Day 14 and Day 30. Fill each blank. One of them needs no preposition.

  1. He is afraid ___ dogs.
  2. We are waiting ___ the bus.
  3. She is good ___ maths.
  4. The committee discussed ___ the plan.
  5. There is a sharp rise ___ prices.
  6. He insisted ___ paying the bill.
  7. The solution ___ this problem is easy.
  8. This phone is superior ___ that one.
  9. I prefer tea ___ coffee.
  10. She is married ___ a doctor.
Answers: 1. of 2. for 3. at 4. (no preposition) 5. in 6. on 7. to 8. to 9. to 10. to
Score 8 or above? You already have a good base. Below 6? Don't worry. That's exactly why you have 30 days.

Expert Tips

  • Study at the same time every day. A fixed slot beats willpower.
  • Tick off each day on a wall chart. Seeing a chain of ticks keeps you going.
  • Read an English newspaper for ten minutes daily and underline every pair you spot.
  • If you miss a day, don't restart. Pick up the next day and add the missed family to the weekend.
  • Take tests honestly. No peeking at your notes, or the score tells you nothing.

What to Do After Day 30

Day 30 isn't the finish line; it's the point where the pairs move from your notebook into your ear. Over the next month, keep three habits going. Do a ten-minute recall from your Mistake Notebook three times a week. Take one timed mixed set every weekend. And whenever you read an English article, underline any fixed preposition pair you notice.

If your Day 30 score is below your target, don't repeat the whole plan. Look at which families cost you marks, and give those families a second pass for a week. A targeted week fixes more than a full restart.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I learn fixed prepositions in 30 days?
Yes, if you study about an hour a day, revise on a schedule and test yourself regularly. Consistency matters far more than long sessions.

How many fixed prepositions should I learn for SSC CGL and bank exams?
There is no official number, but a few hundred common pairs cover most questions. Focus on pairs that repeat in practice papers and learn them by family.

What if I am a complete beginner?
Start with Day 1 and Day 2 exactly as written, and don't worry about your first score. Beginners often improve fastest because they build the habit from scratch.

What if I miss a day?
Don't restart. Continue from the next day, and add the missed family to your weekend revision.

Should I learn the list alphabetically?
No. Alphabetical lists are the hardest to remember. Learn by family and by preposition, with a trick or picture for each hard pair.

Is one hour a day necessary?
One hour is ideal, but the 30-minute version also works if you stay consistent. Stretch the plan to 45 days if you need more time.
