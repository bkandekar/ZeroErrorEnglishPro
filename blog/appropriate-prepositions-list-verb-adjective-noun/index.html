Appropriate Prepositions List: Verb, Adjective and Noun + Preposition with Examples

Learn the appropriate prepositions list with 80+ verb, adjective and noun pairs, examples, a 7-day plan and a 12-question quiz with answers for exams.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Verb / adjective / noun + fixed preposition: learn the pair, not the word

Ask a Class 12 student to complete "She is good ___ maths" and almost everyone writes at. Now ask for "She is jealous ___ her friend's success" and the room goes quiet. Same type of word, same type of sentence, but one pair feels familiar and the other doesn't.

That's the whole story of appropriate prepositions. There's no hidden logic. Some words simply belong together, the way tea belongs with a cup. Examiners know that, and they test it in error spotting, fill in the blanks and sentence improvement year after year.

The good news is that you don't need to learn 500 pairs in random order. In 22 years of teaching, I've found that students remember them best when they're grouped by word type (verbs, adjectives and nouns) and then by the preposition itself. That's exactly how this list is built. Each pair has an example sentence, because a pair on its own is easy to forget and a sentence is not.

Quick answer: An appropriate preposition is the fixed preposition that a particular verb, adjective or noun takes. Examples: depend on, fond of, reason for. They're learned as pairs, not worked out by rule. Start with the three lists below, learn five pairs a day, and test yourself with the 12-question quiz at the end.

What are appropriate prepositions?

An appropriate preposition (also called a dependent preposition) is the preposition that must follow a certain word to make the phrase correct. We say "depend on", never "depend from". We say "afraid of", never "afraid from". The word before the preposition decides which one is right.

The three most common patterns are:

  • Verb + preposition: listen to, believe in, wait for
  • Adjective + preposition: proud of, interested in, responsible for
  • Noun + preposition: reason for, solution to, increase in

Words that change meaning with the preposition

This is where examiners get creative. The same word takes different prepositions, and the meaning shifts.

WordWhat changes
look at / look for / look after / look intolook at = see; look for = search; look after = take care of; look into = investigate
agree with / agree to / agree onagree with a person or opinion; agree to a plan or request; agree on a matter that people decide together
angry with / angry atangry with a person; angry at a thing or situation
arrive at / arrive inarrive at a station, airport or small place; arrive in a city or country
result in / result fromThe rain resulted in a delay (effect). The delay resulted from the rain (cause).
compare with / compare tocompare with = examine differences in detail; compare to = point out a likeness

Don't try to memorise every row at once. Pick two rows a day, write one sentence for each version, and say them aloud.

Using these pairs in your own writing

Fixed prepositions don't only help in objective questions. They show up in letters, essays and emails, where a wrong pair quietly lowers the impression your writing makes. A formal letter that says "I am interested to this post" or "I am looking forward for your reply" looks careless, even if everything else is neat.

Here's a simple habit. After you finish writing, scan only for prepositions. Circle each one, look at the word before it, and ask: is this a pair I know? It takes less than a minute, and it catches the errors your eyes skip while reading for meaning. Correct versions of the two examples above are "I am interested in this post" and "I am looking forward to your reply".

5 ways to memorise appropriate prepositions

  1. Learn by family. One day for the "of" family, one day for "to", one for "with" and "from", and so on. Your brain remembers patterns better than loose items.
  2. Group by meaning. Communication verbs often take "to" (reply to, listen to, refer to). Verbs of blame and thanks take "for". Verbs of belief and success take "in".
  3. Always learn the pair with a sentence. "Fond of" is forgettable. "She is fond of classical music" sticks.
  4. Five pairs a day, not fifty. Revise yesterday's five before you add today's. In three weeks you'll have covered the whole list in this article.
  5. Read and mark. Take one newspaper editorial and underline every verb, adjective or noun followed by a preposition. You'll be surprised how often you've already met these pairs.
A small classroom tip: Write each pair on one side of a card and a gap sentence on the other: "She is jealous ___ her friend." Flip, answer, check. My ebook Preposition in English Grammar is built on the same learn-practise-check rhythm.

Three exam traps to watch for

  • "Than" instead of "to". Senior, junior, superior, inferior and prefer all take "to". Example: "I prefer tea to coffee."
  • An extra preposition. Discuss, comprise, mention and reach take no preposition at all. "We discussed the plan" is right, and "discussed about" is wrong.
  • The look-alike pair. Thank for, congratulate on. Blame for, accuse of. Suffer from, die of. Slow down and check the pair before you decide.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a rule to find the right preposition?

Not a single rule. Most pairs come from usage, so you learn them as pairs. But patterns help: communication verbs lean towards "to", and verbs of blame and thanks take "for".

Is it "depend on" or "depend upon"?

Both are correct. "Upon" is a little more formal. In an exam, if both appear in different options, check the rest of the sentence for the real error.

How many appropriate prepositions should I learn each day?

Five is a good number. Revise the previous day's five first, then add the new ones. This keeps your revision light and your memory strong.

Do appropriate prepositions change between British and American English?

A few do, like "different from" and "different than", but the standard pairs in exam syllabi follow the traditional forms. When in doubt, choose the classic pair.

Which list should I learn first?

Start with verbs, because they appear most often in error-spotting and fill-in-the-blank questions. Then move to adjectives, and finally nouns.
