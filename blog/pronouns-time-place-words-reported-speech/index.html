Changes in Pronouns, Time and Place Words in Reported Speech: Rules and Examples

Learn how pronouns, time words and place words change in reported speech with the P-T-P method, memory tricks, 12 examples and a practice set with answers.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
P-T-P: Person, Time, Place | S-O-N pronouns | N-F near to far | Y-B, T-A

Read this sentence: She said to me, "I will meet you here tomorrow." Now report it. A common attempt looks like this: "She told me that she would meet you here tomorrow."

Count the mistakes. The pronoun is wrong, because "you" now refers to me, the listener, so it must become "me". The place word is wrong, because "here" must become "there". And the time word is wrong, because "tomorrow" must become "the next day". Three small words, three changes, and most students catch only one of them.

The correct answer is: She told me that she would meet me there the next day.

This is the heart of reported speech. Tenses get all the attention, but pronouns, time words and place words decide whether your answer is right or only almost right. In this guide, I'll show you a simple three-check method, the complete lists of changes, the exceptions, the mistakes examiners love and a practice set with answers. If you haven't read the basics, start with my post on direct and indirect speech rules with examples, and my guide to the tense changes chart. The examples here are original sentences on common exam patterns, not questions from a particular year's paper.

Quick answer: the three checks (P-T-P)
  1. Person: change the pronouns (S-O-N rule).
  2. Time: change the time words (now to then, tomorrow to the next day).
  3. Place: change the place words (here to there, this to that).
Example: She said to me, "I will meet you here tomorrow." becomes She told me that she would meet me there the next day.

Part 1: Pronoun changes in reported speech

A pronoun in direct speech is chosen from the speaker's point of view. When you report it, you speak from your own point of view, so the pronoun must change to match who is who in the reporting sentence.

The three-part rule

  • First person (I, me, my, mine, we, us, our, ours) changes according to the subject of the reporting verb.
  • Second person (you, your, yours) changes according to the object of the reporting verb.
  • Third person (he, she, it, they, him, her, them, his, their) does not change.
Memory shortcut: S-O-N. First person follows the Subject. Second person follows the Object. Third person has No change.

Examples of each rule

TypeDirect speechIndirect speech
First person follows the subjectRavi said, "I am tired."Ravi said that he was tired.
Second person follows the objectRavi said to Sunil, "You are late."Ravi told Sunil that he was late.
Third person staysRavi said to Sunil, "She is late."Ravi told Sunil that she was late.
Both togetherRavi said to Sunil, "I will help you."Ravi told Sunil that he would help him.

Special cases that confuse students

When the speaker is also the reporter. If you're reporting your own words, "I" stays "I": I said to him, "I will help you." becomes I told him that I would help him. The first-person pronoun follows the subject, and the subject is "I".

When the listener is the reporter. You said to me, "You are late." becomes You told me that I was late. The second person "you" follows the object ("me"), so it becomes "I".

When there's no object. If the reporting verb has no listener (He said, "You are late."), the second person depends on the context. It becomes "I", "he", "she", "they" or a name, according to who the speaker meant. Exam questions usually give a clear context.

Words that point to a moment close to the speaker also change when the report is made later. Here's the standard list.

Memory shortcut: Y-B, T-A. Yesterday goes with Before (the day before, the previous day). Tomorrow goes with After (the day after, the next day, the following day).

Part 3: Place and demonstrative words

Direct speechIndirect speech
herethere
thisthat
thesethose
comego (when the movement is away from the reporter's place)
bringtake (when the movement is away from the reporter's place)
Memory shortcut: N-F (Near to Far). Near words (now, here, this, these) become far words (then, there, that, those).
Memory shortcut: COME GOES, BRING TAKES. When the action moves away from where you're standing, "come" becomes "go" and "bring" becomes "take".

When time and place words do NOT change

  • The reporting verb is in the present or future tense. He says, "I will come tomorrow." becomes He says that he will come tomorrow. Here, the report is happening now, so "tomorrow" is still tomorrow.
  • The report is made on the same day or in the same place. If you're reporting at the same moment and at the same location, the word may stay. For example, "He said that he would come tomorrow" is acceptable if you say it today. But in exams, unless a question says so, change the words by the standard rule.

In practice, treat these two as the only exceptions you need. In most exam questions, the reporting verb is in the past and there's no hint about when and where the report is made, so the standard changes apply.

A step-by-step walkthrough of a harder sentence

Let's apply P-T-P to a longer sentence that mixes everything: Ravi said to Seema, "I lost my pen yesterday, and you can use this one now."

CheckWhat changes
Reporting verbsaid to becomes told, and we add "that".
PersonI becomes he (subject, Ravi). my becomes his. you becomes she (object, Seema).
Timeyesterday becomes the previous day. now becomes then.
Place and demonstrativethis one becomes that one.
Tenselost becomes had lost. can becomes could.

Final answer: Ravi told Seema that he had lost his pen the previous day and that she could use that one then.

See how the sentence breaks into small, easy decisions? That's the real skill. A long sentence isn't harder than a short one. It just has more small decisions. Take them one by one in the P-T-P order, and the answer builds itself.

Frequently asked questions

Why do pronouns change in reported speech?

Because a pronoun is chosen from the speaker's point of view. When you report the sentence, you speak from your own point of view, so the pronouns must match the new situation.

How do I change "you" in indirect speech?

The second person follows the object of the reporting verb. If you say "Ravi said to Sunil, 'You are late'", then "you" refers to Sunil, so it becomes "he". If the listener is the reporter, "you" becomes "I".

Do I always change "now" to "then"?

In exam questions, yes, when the reporting verb is in the past. The exceptions are a present or future reporting verb, and a report made at the same moment and place.

Should I write "the next day" or "the following day"?

Both are correct for "tomorrow". Use whichever you prefer, and be consistent. The same is true for "the previous day" and "the day before" for "yesterday".

When does "come" become "go"?

When the movement is away from the place where the reporter is standing. If the movement is towards the reporter's place, "come" stays "come". Exam questions with "come" and "go" usually follow the first rule.

What is the easiest way to remember all these changes?

Use P-T-P to decide the order of checks (person, time, place), S-O-N for pronouns and N-F for near words. Practise ten sentences a day for a week and the changes become automatic.
