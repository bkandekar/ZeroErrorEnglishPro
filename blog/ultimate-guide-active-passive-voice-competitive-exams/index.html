Quick Facts Examiners Expect You to Know

  • Only transitive verbs (those that take a direct object) can form a true passive.
  • The passive always needs a form of BE + past participle; any other structure is either wrong or quasi-passive.
  • Sensory verbs (look, seem, smell, taste, feel, sound) followed by an adjective describe a state and almost never take a passive form.
  • When the agent is unknown, unimportant or obvious, the “by + agent” phrase is omitted—this is the default in formal and scientific English.
  • Modal + passive follows the pattern: modal + be + past participle (can be done, must be completed, should have been informed).
  • Causative passive (have/get + object + past participle) is tested separately from ordinary passive and carries its own set of traps.
  • In error-spotting, a passive form of an intransitive or stative verb is almost always the error.

The Complete Tense-Wise Transformation Table

Most students memorise only the present and past simple patterns. Examiners deliberately choose continuous, perfect and perfect-continuous forms because the auxiliary stack becomes longer and the chance of a wrong “be” form rises. The table below is the minimum set you must own cold.

Tense Active Pattern Passive Pattern
Present Simple V1 / V1+s am/is/are + V3
Present Continuous am/is/are + V1-ing am/is/are + being + V3
Present Perfect have/has + V3 have/has + been + V3
Past Simple V2 was/were + V3
Past Continuous was/were + V1-ing was/were + being + V3
Past Perfect had + V3 had + been + V3
Future Simple will + V1 will + be + V3
Future Perfect will + have + V3 will + have + been + V3
Modal modal + V1 modal + be + V3

Notice that continuous passives insert “being” and perfect passives insert “been.” Mixing these two is one of the highest-frequency errors in recent SSC and Banking papers. The Ultimate Guide expands each row with three exam-style sentences and the exact wrong option that appears in the choices.

Named Traps That Cost Marks Every Year

Trap 1 – The Sensory-Adjective Illusion.
Wrong: The soup was tasted delicious by the guests.
Right: The soup tasted delicious to the guests.
Why: “Taste” here is a linking verb describing a quality, not an action performed on the soup. No passive exists.

Trap 2 – Intransitive Verb Forced into Passive.
Wrong: The train was arrived at the station late.
Right: The train arrived at the station late.
Why: “Arrive” takes no object; passive is structurally impossible.

Trap 3 – Missing “Being” in Continuous Passive.
Wrong: The road is repaired at the moment.
Right: The road is being repaired at the moment.
Why: Present continuous passive requires “is/are being + V3.”

Trap 4 – Wrong Auxiliary with Modal.
Wrong: The form must been submitted by Friday.
Right: The form must be submitted by Friday.
Why: Modal passive is always modal + be + past participle (or modal + have been + past participle for perfect).

Trap 5 – Unnecessary Agent.
Wrong: English is spoken by people in many countries by them.
Right: English is spoken in many countries.
Why: When the agent is generic or obvious, “by + agent” is omitted; including it creates redundancy that examiners mark as error.

Trap 6 – Quasi-Passive with “Get.”
Wrong: He was got selected for the post.
Right: He got selected for the post. / He was selected for the post.
Why: “Get” itself functions as a passive marker in informal style; stacking “was got” is non-standard and incorrect in exam English.

These six traps appear, in slightly varied wording, across the last eight years of SSC CGL Tier-1 and IBPS PO prelims. The Ultimate Guide lists fifteen further edge cases with the exact distractors used in official papers.

The 5-Step Zero-Error Checking Method

Whenever you face a “change the voice” or error-spotting item involving voice, run this sequence in under thirty seconds.

  1. Identify the main verb and decide whether it is transitive. If no object exists, passive is almost certainly wrong.
  2. Check for sensory or stative meaning. If the verb is followed by an adjective describing a state, reject passive.
  3. Locate the object of the active sentence. That noun phrase becomes the new subject.
  4. Select the correct BE form according to the tense and number of the new subject; insert “being” or “been” only when the tense demands it.
  5. Decide whether the agent is needed. Keep “by + agent” only when the doer is specific and important; otherwise drop it.

Practise the five steps on twenty mixed sentences every third day. After two weeks the sequence becomes automatic and the error rate on voice questions drops close to zero.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can every active sentence be changed into passive?

A: No. Only sentences with a transitive verb that takes a direct object can form a true passive. Intransitive verbs, most stative verbs, and sensory verbs followed by an adjective cannot.

Q: Is “The book is belonging to me” ever correct?

A: Never in standard exam English. “Belong” is stative and does not form a progressive or passive in this sense. The correct form is “The book belongs to me.”

Q: When do I use “being” versus “been” in passive?

A: “Being” appears in continuous passives (is being repaired). “Been” appears in perfect passives (has been repaired). Mixing them is a frequent error.

Q: Do I always need “by + agent”?

A: No. Omit the agent when it is unknown, obvious or unimportant. Most formal and scientific passives drop the agent.

Q: How many voice questions can I expect in SSC CGL Tier-1?

A: Typically two to four direct or error-spotting items that turn on active–passive knowledge. In Banking prelims the number is similar; mains may add a few more in the form of sentence improvement.

Q: Is the guide useful for descriptive papers as well?

A: Yes. Impersonal passive and formal register are required in precis, essay and letter writing. Unit 6 of the full guide addresses exactly those structures.
