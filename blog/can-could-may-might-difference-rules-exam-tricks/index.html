Can vs Could vs May vs Might: Difference, Rules and Exam Tricks

Learn the difference between can, could, may and might with simple rules, examples, seven exam traps and four memory tricks for SSC and Bank exams.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
CAMP: Can I? Allowed? Maybe? Polite?

You've probably heard this classroom joke. A student asks, Can I go to the washroom? and the teacher replies, I'm sure you can, but you may not. Everyone laughs, and almost nobody remembers why the teacher was technically right.

Here's the thing: the joke works because can, could, may and might overlap. Each one can talk about ability, permission, possibility or politeness, and in some jobs they swap places. That overlap is exactly why examiners love them. They turn up in fill in the blanks, error spotting, sentence improvement and reported speech, and one wrong choice costs a mark.

In this guide I'll untangle the four, one job at a time. You'll get a quick comparison table, the rules for each modal, seven exam traps with corrections, and four memory tricks you'll still remember in the exam hall.

Quick comparison table

Start with the big picture. Read the table once, then come back to it after each section.

Modal What it does Example
can Ability; informal permission; general possibility She can solve this. Can I sit here? Heavy rain can cause floods.
could General ability in the past; polite request; weaker possibility; hypothetical I could swim at six. Could you help me? It could be true.
may Formal permission; real possibility; wishes May I come in? It may rain. May you succeed!
might Smaller possibility; past form of may in reported speech She might come. He said he might join us.

Can: ability and informal permission

Can is the most common of the four, and it does three jobs.

  • Ability: I can type 50 words a minute.
  • Informal permission: Can I use your phone?
  • General possibility: Smoking can cause cancer. Here can talks about what is possible in general, not what will happen today.

Can also makes everyday requests, like Can you pass the salt?, which is fine among friends. For the negative, write cannot as one word in formal writing. In speech we say can't.

Memory Trick 1: CAN = Capacity, MAY = Manners
CAN is about what you're capable of. MAY is about good manners: asking permission politely, the way you would with a teacher or a stranger.
If the question is 'Are you able?', think Capacity. If it's 'Is it allowed?' in a formal situation, think Manners.

Could: two hats

Could is where most students slip, because it wears two hats. Once you see which hat is on, the confusion disappears.

Memory Trick 2: COULD Wears Two Hats
Hat 1: the Past hat. General ability in the past. When I was ten, I could swim across the lake.
Hat 2: the Polite hat. Requests, suggestions and weak possibility with no past meaning at all. Could you open the window? We could try again. That could be the answer.

Could also appears in two other places you should know. In hypothetical sentences: If I could fly, I would visit Paris. And in reported speech, where it's the past form of can: He said, 'I can help.' becomes He said that he could help.

Now the rule that catches many students. Could talks about a general ability in the past, not a single achievement. For one specific success, use was able to or managed to.

The Single Win Rule
COULD = a general ability, a habit of being able.
WAS ABLE TO = one specific win.
Wrong: After three attempts, he could pass the exam. Right: After three attempts, he was able to pass the exam.
Negatives are the exception: He couldn't pass the exam is fine.

May: manners and maybe

May has three main jobs.

  • Formal permission: May I come in? You may leave now.
  • Real possibility: It may rain this evening.
  • Wishes and blessings: May you succeed! May God bless you. In wishes, the modal comes first and the subject follows.

Two things to remember about may. First, it has no past form for permission. To say permission existed in the past, use was allowed to: We were allowed to leave early. Second, for past possibility, use may have + V3: She may have missed the bus.

A quick warning: You may go can mean 'You're allowed to go' or 'Perhaps you will go', depending on the situation. In writing, context usually settles it. In exams, the question gives you a clue, such as a teacher addressing a student (permission) or a weather forecast (possibility). Read the whole sentence before you decide which job may is doing.

Might: the mist of doubt

Might is the cautious one.

Memory Trick 3: MIGHT = a Mist of Doubt
Picture a misty morning where you can barely see the road. MAY is a half-clear morning: a real possibility. MIGHT is the thick mist: a smaller chance, with more doubt.
It may rain (I can see clouds). It might rain (I doubt it, but who knows).

Might does four things:

  • Smaller possibility: She might come, but I doubt it.
  • Past form of may in reported speech: He said, 'I may come.' becomes He said that he might come.
  • Past possibility: He might have forgotten the address.
  • Very formal request: Might I ask a question? It sounds old-fashioned today, but you may meet it in formal writing.

Can vs may, and may vs might

Can vs may for permission

Both are accepted in modern English. In everyday speech, Can I is completely normal. But in a formal situation, such as asking your principal or writing to an officer, May I is the polite choice, and exam questions often follow that thinking. So the practical rule is: informal, use can; formal, use may; in an exam with both options, choose may.

Could vs might for possibility

Both show a weak chance, and in many sentences they're interchangeable: It could rain and It might rain mean almost the same thing. The difference is that might carries more doubt, while could simply says it's possible. When in doubt in an exam, check the surrounding tense.

May vs might for possibility

In present and future sentences, the gap is small, and many modern writers use them almost equally. Exams care about two places: after a past reporting verb, where may becomes might, and in unreal or doubtful situations, where might fits better.

Polite requests: the politeness ladder

Requests are the one place where all four modals meet, so it helps to see them on a ladder from casual to very formal.

  • Can you help me? (casual, fine with friends and family)
  • Could you help me? (polite, safe almost everywhere)
  • May I ask for your help? (formal, you're asking for permission)
  • Might I ask for your help? (very formal, rare in modern speech)

Notice the subjects. Can you and could you ask the other person to do something. May I and might I ask for permission to do something yourself. Students sometimes write May you help me? and that's wrong, because may with you doesn't make a request. It makes a wish: May you live long.

In an interview, an email to a manager or an application letter, could you and may I are your safest friends. Can you works in a chat with a classmate, and might I belongs mostly in old novels.

Reported speech: what happens to the four modals

Modals change in indirect speech only when the reporting verb is in the past. Here's the pattern in a single glance:

Direct speech Indirect speech
She said, 'I can swim.' She said that she could swim.
He said, 'I may come.' He said that he might come.
They said, 'We could help.' They said that they could help. (no change)
She said, 'It might rain.' She said that it might rain. (no change)

So can becomes could and may becomes might, while could and might stay exactly as they are. Remember this as 'the two that move and the two that stay', and you'll never lose a reported speech question on these modals.

One more point. If the reporting verb is in the present or future, such as She says or She will say, nothing changes: She says that she can swim. Examiners sometimes set exactly this trap to see whether you shift tenses blindly.

The CAMP method

Memorising all this is hard in the middle of an exam. So here's a four-question routine.

Memory Trick 4: CAMP
Can I do it? That's ability, so use can (or could for the past).
Allowed? That's permission. Use can informally or may formally.
Maybe? That's possibility. Use may, might or could, depending on how sure you are.
Polite? That's a request. Use could or may for the softest tone.

Let's test it. ___ you please send me the file? Is it ability? No. Is it permission? No. Is it a request that needs to be polite? Yes. So Could is the best fit. Next, It ___ rain by evening, but I doubt it. The speaker is unsure, so it's Maybe, and the phrase 'but I doubt it' points to the mist: might.

Seven exam traps with corrections

These seven patterns appear again and again in error spotting and sentence improvement. Each one is a Common exam pattern.

✘ Wrong: He said that he may come tomorrow.
✔ Right: He said that he might come tomorrow.
Reported speech. The reporting verb is in the past, so may becomes might.
✘ Wrong: After many attempts, he could solve the puzzle.
✔ Right: After many attempts, he was able to solve the puzzle.
The Single Win Rule. One specific success needs was able to, not could.
✘ Wrong: It may rained last night.
✔ Right: It may have rained last night.
Past possibility. Use modal + have + V3.
✘ Wrong: She asked if she can leave early.
✔ Right: She asked if she could leave early.
Reported speech again. Can becomes could after a past reporting verb.
✘ Wrong: If I can, I would help you.
✔ Right: If I could, I would help you.
An unreal condition needs could in the if clause. (A real condition, such as If I can, I will help, keeps can with will.)
✘ Wrong: May be he is late today.
✔ Right: Maybe he is late today.
Here we need the adverb maybe, meaning perhaps. Use may be only when it's a modal plus a verb: He may be late.
✘ Wrong: He can able to speak four languages.
✔ Right: He can speak four languages. / He is able to speak four languages.
Don't stack can and able to. Choose one.

Eight practice questions with answers

Give yourself three minutes. Cover the answers first.

  1. ___ I use your phone for a minute? (a) Must (b) Should (c) May (d) Ought
  2. When he was young, he ___ run ten kilometres without stopping. (a) can (b) could (c) may (d) might
  3. She said that she ___ join us later. (a) may (b) can (c) might (d) will
  4. Find the error: He can able to speak four languages.
  5. Find the error: May be she forgot the meeting.
  6. Improve the sentence: After many attempts, he could solve the puzzle.
  7. Find the error: She asked if she can leave early.
  8. Find the error: It may rained last night.
Answers:
1) (c) May. It's a polite, formal permission request.
2) (b) could. General ability in the past.
3) (c) might. Reported speech: may becomes might.
4) Remove able to or can: He can speak four languages.
5) May be should be Maybe.
6) He was able to solve the puzzle after many attempts.
7) can should be could.
8) rained should be have rained.

Frequently asked questions

Is it wrong to say 'Can I' when asking for permission?

Not in everyday speech. People say Can I borrow your pen? all the time, and it's accepted. But may is more formal and more polite, and many exam questions expect it when the situation is formal. If both can and may appear as options in a permission question, may is usually the safer answer.

Can could be used for the future?

Yes, in two ways. Could can show a possibility (It could rain tomorrow) or make a polite suggestion (We could meet on Friday). For a firm future ability, use will be able to instead of will can.

What is the difference between cannot and can not?

Cannot is the normal written form, and can't is the short form used in speech. Can not, written as two words, is used only when you want to stress the word not, as in 'You can not only read but also write.' In exams, treat cannot as the standard form.

Is there any real difference between may and might for possibility?

There is a small one. May suggests a real, open possibility. Might suggests a smaller chance or more doubt. In many everyday sentences they're interchangeable, but might is the correct choice after a past reporting verb (He said he might come) and in unreal situations.

What is the difference between maybe and may be?

Maybe is one word and means perhaps: Maybe he's late. May be is two words, a modal plus a verb, and it means 'could be': He may be late. A quick test: if you can replace it with perhaps, write maybe.

Is could the past tense of can?

Only partly. Could is the past form of can for general ability (I could swim as a child) and in reported speech. But could is also used for polite requests and present possibility, which have nothing to do with the past.
