For the negative, write <b>cannot</b> as one word in formal writing. In speech we say <i>can't</i>.</p> <div class="mv13"><div class="mv14">Memory Trick 1: CAN = Capacity, MAY = Manners</div><div class="mv15"><b>CAN</b> is about what you're capable of. <b>MAY</b> is about good manners: asking permission politely, the way you would with a teacher or a stranger.<br>If the question is 'Are you able?', think Capacity. If it's 'Is it allowed?' in a formal situation, think Manners.</div></div> <h2 id="could" class="mv4">Could: two hats</h2> <p class="mv1"><b>Could</b> is where most students slip, because it wears two hats. Once you see which hat is on, the confusion disappears.</p> <div class="mv13"><div class="mv14">Memory Trick 2: COULD Wears Two Hats</div><div class="mv15"><b>Hat 1: the Past hat.</b> General ability in the past. <i>When I was ten, I could swim across the lake.</i><br><b>Hat 2: the Polite hat.</b> Requests, suggestions and weak possibility with no past meaning at all. <i>Could you open the window? We could try again. That could be the answer.</i></div></div> <p class="mv1">Could also appears in two other places you should know. In <b>hypothetical sentences</b>: <i>If I could fly, I would visit Paris.</i> And in <b>reported speech</b>, where it's the past form of can: <i>He said, 'I can help.'</i> becomes <i>He said that he could help.</i></p> <p class="mv1">Now the rule that catches many students. <b>Could</b> talks about a general ability in the past, not a single achievement. For one specific success, use <b>was able to</b> or <b>managed to</b>.</p> <div class="mv13"><div class="mv14">The Single Win Rule</div><div class="mv15"><b>COULD</b> = a general ability, a habit of being able.<br><b>WAS ABLE TO</b> = one specific win.<br><i>Wrong: After three attempts, he could pass the exam. Right: After three attempts, he was able to pass the exam.</i><br>Negatives are the exception: <i>He couldn't pass the exam</i> is fine.</div></div> <h2 id="may" class="mv4">May: manners and maybe</h2> <p class="mv1"><b>May</b> has three main jobs.</p> <ul class="mv11"><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Formal permission:</b> <i>May I come in? You may leave now.</i></li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Real possibility:</b> <i>It may rain this evening.</i></li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Wishes and blessings:</b> <i>May you succeed! May God bless you.</i> In wishes, the modal comes first and the subject follows.</li></ul> <p class="mv1">Two things to remember about may. First, it has <b>no past form</b> for permission. To say permission existed in the past, use <i>was allowed to</i>: <i>We were allowed to leave early.</i> Second, for past possibility, use <b>may have + V3</b>: <i>She may have missed the bus.</i></p> <p class="mv1">A quick warning: <i>You may go</i> can mean 'You're allowed to go' or 'Perhaps you will go', depending on the situation. In writing, context usually settles it. In exams, the question gives you a clue, such as a teacher addressing a student (permission) or a weather forecast (possibility). Read the whole sentence before you decide which job may is doing.</p> <h2 id="might" class="mv4">Might: the mist of doubt</h2> <p class="mv1"><b>Might</b> is the cautious one.</p> <div class="mv13"><div class="mv14">Memory Trick 3: MIGHT = a Mist of Doubt</div><div class="mv15">Picture a misty morning where you can barely see the road. <b>MAY</b> is a half-clear morning: a real possibility. <b>MIGHT</b> is the thick mist: a smaller chance, with more doubt.<br><i>It may rain (I can see clouds). It might rain (I doubt it, but who knows).</i></div></div> <p class="mv1">Might does four things:</p> <ul class="mv11"><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Smaller possibility:</b> <i>She might come, but I doubt it.</i></li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Past form of may in reported speech:</b> <i>He said, 'I may come.'</i> becomes <i>He said that he might come.</i></li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Past possibility:</b> <i>He might have forgotten the address.</i></li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Very formal request:</b> <i>Might I ask a question?</i> It sounds old-fashioned today, but you may meet it in formal writing.</li></ul> <h2 id="compare" class="mv4">Can vs may, and may vs might</h2> <h3 class="mv19">Can vs may for permission</h3> <p class="mv1">Both are accepted in modern English. In everyday speech, <i>Can I</i> is completely normal. But in a formal situation, such as asking your principal or writing to an officer, <i>May I</i> is the polite choice, and exam questions often follow that thinking. So the practical rule is: <b>informal, use can; formal, use may; in an exam with both options, choose may.</b></p> <h3 class="mv19">Could vs might for possibility</h3> <p class="mv1">Both show a weak chance, and in many sentences they're interchangeable: <i>It could rain</i> and <i>It might rain</i> mean almost the same thing. The difference is that <b>might</b> carries more doubt, while <b>could</b> simply says it's possible. When in doubt in an exam, check the surrounding tense.</p> day2 script.js part 2 of 2

<h3 class="mv19">May vs might for possibility</h3>
<p class="mv1">In present and future sentences, the gap is small, and many modern writers use them almost equally. Exams care about two places: after a past reporting verb, where <b>may</b> becomes <b>might</b>, and in unreal or doubtful situations, where <b>might</b> fits better.</p>
<h2 id="requests" class="mv4">Polite requests: the politeness ladder</h2>
<p class="mv1">Requests are the one place where all four modals meet, so it helps to see them on a ladder from casual to very formal.</p>
<ul class="mv11"><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Can you</b> help me? (casual, fine with friends and family)</li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Could you</b> help me? (polite, safe almost everywhere)</li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>May I</b> ask for your help? (formal, you're asking for permission)</li><li style="margin-bottom:6px;"><b>Might I</b> ask for your help? (very formal, rare in modern speech)</li></ul>
<p class="mv1">Notice the subjects. Can you and could you ask the other person to <i>do</i> something. May I and might I ask for permission to do something yourself. Students sometimes write <i>May you help me?</i> and that's wrong, because may with <i>you</i> doesn't make a request. It makes a wish: <i>May you live long.</i></p>
<p class="mv1">In an interview, an email to a manager or an application letter, <b>could you</b> and <b>may I</b> are your safest friends. Can you works in a chat with a classmate, and might I belongs mostly in old novels.</p>
<h2 id="reported" class="mv4">Reported speech: what happens to the four modals</h2>
<p class="mv1">Modals change in indirect speech only when the reporting verb is in the past. Here's the pattern in a single glance:</p>
<div class="mv17"><table class="mv18"><tr><th class="mv6">Direct speech</th><th class="mv6">Indirect speech</th></tr><tr><td class="mv2">She said, 'I can swim.'</td><td class="mv2">She said that she could swim.</td></tr><tr><td class="mv3">He said, 'I may come.'</td><td class="mv3">He said that he might come.</td></tr><tr><td class="mv2">They said, 'We could help.'</td><td class="mv2">They said that they could help. (no change)</td></tr><tr><td class="mv3">She said, 'It might rain.'</td><td class="mv3">She said that it might rain. (no change)</td></tr></table></div>
<p class="mv1">So <b>can becomes could</b> and <b>may becomes might</b>, while could and might stay exactly as they are. Remember this as 'the two that move and the two that stay', and you'll never lose a reported speech question on these modals.</p>
<p class="mv1">One more point. If the reporting verb is in the present or future, such as <i>She says</i> or <i>She will say</i>, nothing changes: <i>She says that she can swim.</i> Examiners sometimes set exactly this trap to see whether you shift tenses blindly.</p>
<h2 id="camp" class="mv4">The CAMP method</h2>
<p class="mv1">Memorising all this is hard in the middle of an exam. So here's a four-question routine.</p>
<div class="mv13"><div class="mv14">Memory Trick 4: CAMP</div><div class="mv15"><b>C</b>an I do it? That's ability, so use <b>can</b> (or <b>could</b> for the past).<br><b>A</b>llowed? That's permission. Use <b>can</b> informally or <b>may</b> formally.<br><b>M</b>aybe? That's possibility. Use <b>may</b>, <b>might</b> or <b>could</b>, depending on how sure you are.<br><b>P</b>olite? That's a request. Use <b>could</b> or <b>may</b> for the softest tone.</div></div>
<p class="mv1">Let's test it. <i>___ you please send me the file?</i> Is it ability? No. Is it permission? No. Is it a request that needs to be polite? Yes. So <b>Could</b> is the best fit. Next, <i>It ___ rain by evening, but I doubt it.</i> The speaker is unsure, so it's Maybe, and the phrase 'but I doubt it' points to the mist: <b>might</b>.</p>
<h2 id="traps" class="mv4">Seven exam traps with corrections</h2>
<p class="mv1">These seven patterns appear again and again in error spotting and sentence improvement. Each one is a <b>Common exam pattern</b>.</p>
<div class="mv7"><div class="mv8"><b>&#10008; Wrong:</b> He said that he may come tomorrow.</div><div class="mv9"><b>&#10004; Right:</b> He said that he might come tomorrow.</div><div class="mv10">Reported speech. The reporting verb is in the past, so <i>may</i> becomes <i>might</i>.</div></div>
<div class="mv7"><div class="mv8"><b>&#10008; Wrong:</b> After many attempts, he could solve the puzzle.</div><div class="mv9"><b>&#10004; Right:</b> After many attempts, he was able to solve the puzzle.</div><div class="mv10">The Single Win Rule. One specific success needs <i>was able to</i>, not <i>could</i>.</div></div>
<div class="mv7"><div class="mv8"><b>&#10008; Wrong:</b> It may rained last night.</div><div class="mv9"><b>&#10004; Right:</b> It may have rained last night.</div><div class="mv10">Past possibility. Use modal + have + V3.</div></div>
<div class="mv7"><div class="mv8"><b>&#10008; Wrong:</b> She asked if she can leave early.</div><div class="mv9"><b>&#10004; Right:</b> She asked if she could leave early.</div><div class="mv10">Reported speech again. <i>Can</i> becomes <i>could</i> after a past reporting verb.</div></div>
<div class="mv7"><div class="mv8"><b>&#10008; Wrong:</b> If I can, I would help you.</div><div class="mv9"><b>&#10004; Right:</b> If I could, I would help you.</div><div class="mv10">An unreal condition needs <i>could</i> in the <i>if</i> clause. (A real condition, such as <i>If I can, I will help</i>, keeps <i>can</i> with <i>will</i>.)</div></div>
<div class="mv7"><div class="mv8"><b>&#10008; Wrong:</b> May be he is late today.</div><div class="mv9"><b>&#10004; Right:</b> Maybe he is late today.</div><div class="mv10">Here we need the adverb <i>maybe</i>, meaning perhaps. Use <i>may be</i> only when it's a modal plus a verb: <i>He may be late.</i></div></div>
<div class="mv7"><div class="mv8"><b>&#10008; Wrong:</b> He can able to speak four languages.</div><div class="mv9"><b>&#10004; Right:</b> He can speak four languages. / He is able to speak four languages.</div><div class="mv10">Don't stack <i>can</i> and <i>able to</i>. Choose one.</div></div>

<h2 id="faq" class="mv4">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3 class="mv12">Is it wrong to say 'Can I' when asking for permission?</h3><p class="mv1">Not in everyday speech. People say Can I borrow your pen? all the time, and it's accepted. But may is more formal and more polite, and many exam questions expect it when the situation is formal. If both can and may appear as options in a permission question, may is usually the safer answer.</p>
<h3 class="mv12">Can could be used for the future?</h3><p class="mv1">Yes, in two ways. Could can show a possibility (It could rain tomorrow) or make a polite suggestion (We could meet on Friday). For a firm future ability, use will be able to instead of will can.</p>
<h3 class="mv12">What is the difference between cannot and can not?</h3><p class="mv1">Cannot is the normal written form, and can't is the short form used in speech. Can not, written as two words, is used only when you want to stress the word not, as in 'You can not only read but also write.' In exams, treat cannot as the standard form.</p>
<h3 class="mv12">Is there any real difference between may and might for possibility?</h3><p class="mv1">There is a small one. May suggests a real, open possibility. Might suggests a smaller chance or more doubt. In many everyday sentences they're interchangeable, but might is the correct choice after a past reporting verb (He said he might come) and in unreal situations.</p>
<h3 class="mv12">What is the difference between maybe and may be?</h3><p class="mv1">Maybe is one word and means perhaps: Maybe he's late. May be is two words, a modal plus a verb, and it means 'could be': He may be late. A quick test: if you can replace it with perhaps, write maybe.</p>
<h3 class="mv12">Is could the past tense of can?</h3><p class="mv1">Only partly. Could is the past form of can for general ability (I could swim as a child) and in reported speech. But could is also used for polite requests and present possibility, which have nothing to do with the past.</p>
