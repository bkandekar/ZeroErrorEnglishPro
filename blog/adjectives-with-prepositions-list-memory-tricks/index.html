What Are Adjective and Preposition Combinations?

An adjective describes a noun, like proud or keen. Many adjectives need a particular preposition to complete their meaning. You can't say "proud" and stop; you must say proud of something. The adjective and its preposition work as one unit, which is why they're called word partners or dependent prepositions.

There's no rule that tells you which preposition goes with which adjective, so you can't reason your way to the answer. But the pairs aren't random either. Many cluster into families with a shared idea, and once you spot the family, you remember five pairs for the price of one. Think about a typical English section. In one paper you may see three or four error spotting sentences where the only fault is a wrong preposition after an adjective. The pairs are also reused. The same adjectives, such as fond, keen, superior, famous and satisfied, appear in paper after paper, dressed in new sentences. 5 Memory Tricks That Make the List Stick

A list this long can't be memorised line by line. These five tricks turn it into something your brain can hold.

Trick 1: Learn the family, not the pair

Don't memorise "scared of" and "terrified of" separately. Learn "fear feelings take OF" and you've got afraid, scared, terrified and frightened in one go. Use the family idea in the headings above as your cue card.

Trick 2: Remember the SSJI rule: no THAN after TO-words

Superior, senior, junior, inferior and prior already carry a built-in comparison, so they take to, never than. The mistake "superior than" appears again and again in error spotting. Remember: Superior, Senior, Junior, Inferior: SSJI, all TO.

Trick 3: Draw a tiny picture for the tough ones

Pictures beat rules. For rich in, imagine a mango with vitamins sitting in it. For addicted to, picture a phone glued to someone's hand. For free from, see a bird flying out from a cage. For keen on, picture a fan leaning on the boundary rope. Silly pictures stay longer than sensible ones.

Trick 4: Pair it with a feeling or a person

Link each tricky pair to someone you know. "My brother is jealous of my phone." "My teacher is satisfied with my notes." A sentence about your own life sticks in memory far better than a textbook example, because it carries emotion.

Trick 5: Do a daily five-pair recall

Each morning, write five pairs from yesterday on paper without looking at your notes. Check them. Pairs you miss go into a "retry" list. This takes five minutes and does more than an hour of passive reading.

Confusing Pairs: Same Adjective, Different Preposition

Some adjectives change their preposition when the meaning changes. These trip up even good students, and examiners know it.

AdjectivePair and meaningExample
angrywith a person, at a thing or event, about a matterMother was angry with me. He was angry at the delay.
sorryfor = feel pity for someone, about = regret somethingI feel sorry for him. I'm sorry about the delay.
goodat = skilled, for = beneficial, to = kindShe's good at drawing. Fruit is good for health. He was good to us.
tiredof = fed up with, from = exhausted after effortI'm tired of waiting. She was tired from the journey.
kind, rudeto a person, but of when you judge the behaviourHe is kind to children. It was kind of you to help.

The last row is a real exam favourite: "It was very kind of you" is correct, and "It was very kind to you" changes the meaning. Read the whole sentence before choosing.

What Comes After the Preposition: The -ing Rule

Here's a point that catches many students. When an adjective with a preposition is followed by a verb, the verb takes the -ing form. A preposition is never followed by "to + verb" in these patterns.

WrongRight
She is fond of to sing.She is fond of singing.
He is good at to solve puzzles.He is good at solving puzzles.
I am tired of to wait.I am tired of waiting.
They are keen on to join.They are keen on joining.

Keep one small exception in mind: "used to" and "accustomed to" also take -ing when they mean being familiar with something. "I'm used to waking up early" is correct. "I used to wake up early" is a different structure and means a past habit. This difference is a favourite exam trap, so read it twice.

5 Mistakes That Cost Marks

Here are five errors that show up again and again.

MistakeWrongRight
Wrong preposition after fondShe is fond at music.She is fond of music.
Using than after superiorThis phone is superior than that one.This phone is superior to that one.
Using to after interestedI am interested to join the club.I am interested in joining the club.
Using with after marriedHe is married with a doctor.He is married to a doctor.
Using in after good (skill)She is good in cooking.She is good at cooking.

Notice that every wrong sentence still makes perfect sense. That's why the error is so easy to miss. Examiners count on your ear to say "sounds fine".

The 6-Step Method to Learn the List

Ten minutes a day, done every day, is enough. Here's the routine.

  1. Pick one family a day. Start with OF, then AT, then IN, and so on.
  2. Read the pairs aloud twice, with the family idea in your head.
  3. Choose five pairs that you didn't know and attach a picture or a person to each.
  4. Write one sentence for each of the five, about your own life.
  5. Revise after 1, 3 and 7 days. Use the five-pair recall from Trick 5.
  6. Test with exam questions. A few error spotting or fill in the blanks questions finish the session.

How Exams Test Adjectives with Prepositions

Adjective and preposition pairs appear in error spotting, fill in the blanks, sentence improvement and cloze passages. Here are four questions in the usual style.

Common Exam Pattern: Error Spotting
Rahul is very fond at (A) / playing chess (B) / in his free time. (C) / No error (D)
Answer: A. It should be "fond of".
SSC CGL Exam Pattern: Error Spotting
This laptop is superior than (A) / the one I bought (B) / last year. (C) / No error (D)
Answer: A. It should be "superior to".
Common Exam Pattern: Fill in the blank
She was annoyed ___ his rude behaviour.
Answer: with.
Common Exam Pattern: Sentence improvement
I am not interested to join the club.
Answer: interested in joining.

A five-second way to attack these

Spot the adjective first. In the first question it's "fond", and its partner is "of", but the sentence says "at". Done. Don't try to understand the whole sentence; just check the adjective and the word right after it.

In a cloze passage, the same pairs hide inside longer sentences. The blank may come right after an adjective, such as "The villagers were grateful ___ the doctor for his help." Look at the word before the blank and name its family. Grateful takes TO before a person and FOR before the help received, so the answer here is TO. A cloze question is just a fill in the blank with extra noise around it, so the same five-second habit works.

Expert Tips

  • Read the news and underline pairs. Seeing adjectives with their partners in real sentences builds the habit faster than lists.
  • Don't trust "it sounds right". Your ear may have been trained on wrong usage. A good dictionary shows the partner in its examples.
  • Learn opposites together: different from, similar to; superior to, inferior to. Contrasts help memory.
  • Play safe in exams. Where two forms exist, such as different from and different to, choose the one most grammar books accept: different from.
  • Fix your weakest family first. If you keep missing TO-words, spend three days on them before moving on.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are adjectives with prepositions?
They are adjectives that always go with a particular preposition to complete their meaning, such as afraid of, good at and married to. They are also called dependent prepositions or word partners.

Is it afraid of or afraid from?
The correct form is afraid of. Afraid from is a common mistake. All fear words, such as scared, terrified and frightened, also take of.

Which prepositions can follow angry?
Angry with is used for a person, angry at for a thing or event, and angry about for a matter. For example: angry with her brother, angry at the delay.

Is it superior than or superior to?
Superior to is correct. Superior, inferior, senior, junior and prior already contain a comparison, so they are followed by to and never by than.

How many adjective and preposition pairs do I need for competitive exams?
There is no official number, but a few dozen common pairs cover most questions. Learn them by family and focus on the ones that repeat in exam practice.

Can one adjective take two different prepositions?
Yes, sometimes the meaning changes with the preposition, as in good at, good for and good to. Learn those contrast pairs together.
