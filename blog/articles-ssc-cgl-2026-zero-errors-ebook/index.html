Why Articles Cost Marks Even After Reading the Rules

Every rule in this series is genuinely simple on its own. The sound rule for a and an is one line. The, in most cases, is one idea: a known, specific noun. Zero article, at its core, is just "general idea, no article needed". So why do these small words still cost aspirants marks?

  • The rules are scattered. Learning them across separate sessions, weeks apart, means the connections between them never fully form.
  • Exceptions outnumber the main rule. Institutions, meals, transport phrases and fixed idioms each behave slightly differently, and there's no single memory trick that covers all of them.
  • Recognition under pressure is a different skill from understanding. Knowing a rule when it's explained is not the same as spotting its violation inside an unfamiliar four-part sentence, under a countdown timer.
  • Without repeated, spaced practice, the rules fade. A concept read once in a blog post, without follow-up practice a few days later, rarely survives to exam day.

None of this means the rules are hard. It means they need a structured system, not scattered reading, to actually stick.

Why the Wrong-Right-Why Format Works

Most grammar resources state a rule and move on. This series, and the book it's built from, do something slightly different for every trap: show the incorrect sentence first, then the corrected version, then explain why. This matters because recognising an error is a different skill from reciting a rule. Exam questions rarely ask "what is the rule for the", they present a sentence and ask you to judge it. Practising against wrong examples, not just correct ones, trains the exact skill the exam tests.
