Used To, Would, Was Going To: Past Habits and Future in the Past Made Easy

Used to vs would explained with simple rules, plus be used to, was going to, exam-style questions, and a memory trick to stop past habit errors.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Past state = used to + V1; repeated past action = used to / would + V1; plan in the past = was/were going to + V1

"When I was a child, I would live in a small village." Read that sentence twice. It feels natural, almost poetic. Yet it contains a classic error, and examiners know exactly which students will fall into it.

Used to, would and was going to are three small expressions that do a big job: they let us talk about the past, about old habits and about plans that didn't work out. Because they look similar and overlap in some situations, many aspirants mix them up or apply them in places they don't belong.

In this post I'll show you what each expression really does, where they overlap, where they must not, the spelling trap with be used to, and how to handle them in exam questions. Used To: Past Habits and Past States

Use used to + base verb for two things: habits that were repeated in the past, and states that were true in the past but are no longer true now.

Habit: "He used to jog every morning." State: "We used to live in Nashik." "She used to be very shy." In every example, the situation existed before and has now changed. That sense of "not any more" is the heart of used to.

The negative is didn't use to and the question is Did you use to...? Notice that there is no d at the end of use here. Why? Because did already carries the past tense, so the main verb goes back to the base form, exactly as in "Did you finish?" And used to has no present form: you can't say "He uses to jog" in this meaning.

One more limitation: used to describes a long-term or repeated situation, not a single event. "I used to visit Delhi once in 2019" is wrong because it's a single, specific occasion.

Would: Repeated Past Actions, Never States

Would can also describe past habits, but only repeated actions, and usually when the past time frame has already been set. "Every summer we would visit our grandparents." "On Sundays, my father would take us to the market."

It doesn't work for states. You can't say "I would live in Pune" or "She would be shy" in this meaning, because living and being aren't actions repeated again and again. For states, only used to works.

A good trick: if the sentence includes words like every summer, each morning or whenever, would is likely to fit. If it describes where someone lived, what someone was like or what someone had, use used to.

Used To vs Would: Quick Comparison

Here is a side-by-side table you can revise in two minutes before a practice session.

FeatureUsed toWould
Repeated past actionsYes: He used to jog every morning.Yes: He would jog every morning.
Past states (live, be, have, know)Yes: She used to be shy.No. She would be shy is wrong in this meaning.
Needs a time frame set earlierNoUsually yes: When I was young, we would...
Question formDid you use to play cricket?Would you play cricket? (rare for habits)

Whenever you're unsure in the exam, used to is the safer choice, because it works for both habits and states. Would is the specialist; use it only when you're certain the sentence is about repeated actions.

Be Used To vs Used To: The Spelling Trap

This is the pair that traps even good students. They look nearly identical, but they mean different things and are followed by different verb forms.

Used to + base verb describes a past habit: "I used to get up at five." Be used to + V-ing or noun means being accustomed to something: "I am used to getting up early." "She is used to the cold weather." You can also use get used to for the process of becoming accustomed: "He is getting used to the new city."

Notice that in the second pattern, to is a preposition, so a verb after it takes the -ing form. That's why "I am used to get up early" is wrong. The correct form is "I am used to getting up early".

Was Going To: Future in the Past

Sometimes we look back at the past and talk about what was planned or expected at that time. For this, English uses was/were going to + base verb.

"I was going to call you, but I forgot." "They were going to launch the app in May, but the date changed." The structure expresses a plan or intention that existed in the past. Very often, it also hints that the plan didn't happen.

Don't confuse this with would in reported speech, which gives the future from a past viewpoint: "He said that he would call." Was going to is more about a plan or a strong expectation. In many sentences both are possible, but in exam questions the past plan that failed is almost always was going to.

Four Questions Solved Step by Step

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) When I was a child, / (b) I would live / (c) in a small village. / (d) No error.

Living is a state, so would can't be used. The correct phrase is "I used to live". Answer: (b).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) He didn't used to / (b) smoke / (c) before his marriage. / (d) No error.

Did(n't) already carries the past tense, so the main verb takes the base form: "didn't use to". Answer: (a).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) I am used to / (b) get up / (c) early every day. / (d) No error.

Be used to means accustomed, and to is a preposition, so the verb takes -ing: "getting up". Answer: (b).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) She was going to / (b) call you / (c) but she forgot. / (d) No error.

This is a correct use of was going to for a past plan that wasn't carried out. No change is needed. Answer: (d).

The States-Need-Used-To Rule

  • States (live, be, have, know, like, want): only used to.
  • Repeated actions (visit, go, play, walk): used to or would.
  • Be used to = accustomed: always followed by -ing.

Remember it as: Live, be and have never take would. Be used to always takes -ing.

The 5 Past Habit Mistakes That Cost Marks

Notice how each of these sounds natural when read quickly. Slow down and the error appears.

5 Mistakes to Avoid
Mistake 1
✗ Wrong: When I was young, I would be very shy.
✓ Right: When I was young, I used to be very shy.
Be is a state, and would can not describe a state in the past.
Mistake 2
✗ Wrong: She didn't used to like coffee.
✓ Right: She didn't use to like coffee.
Didn't carries the past tense, so use goes back to the base form.
Mistake 3
✗ Wrong: Did you used to play cricket?
✓ Right: Did you use to play cricket?
In a question with did, the main verb is the base form.
Mistake 4
✗ Wrong: I am used to wake up early.
✓ Right: I am used to waking up early.
Be used to means accustomed. To is a preposition, so the verb takes -ing.
Mistake 5
✗ Wrong: He used to going to the gym every day.
✓ Right: He used to go to the gym every day.
Used to describing a past habit is followed by the base verb, not -ing.

Two of these five (mistakes 4 and 5) are the mirror images of each other, and they are exactly why examiners love this topic. One pattern takes -ing; the other takes the base verb. Learn them as a pair and you will never confuse them again.

The 6-Step Method for Used To, Would and Was Going To

Use this routine whenever you see used to, would or going to in a sentence about the past.

Your 6-Step Method
1
Confirm the time
Is the sentence about the past? If not, these patterns may not apply.
2
State or action?
Is it a state (live, be, have) or a repeated action (visit, play)?
3
Choose the form
State: used to. Repeated action: used to or would.
4
Check did and didn't
After did or didn't, the verb is use to, with no d.
5
Check be used to
If the meaning is accustomed, the next verb takes -ing.
6
Check plans in the past
A past plan that may not have happened takes was or were going to plus the base verb.

Step 2 does the heavy lifting. Once you decide whether the sentence is about a state or an action, most of the other choices are made for you.

Practice Set with Answers

Try each question on your own first. Use the clue method before you read the answer, and note which clue you used. Then check your reasoning against the explanation.

Q1. (a) We used to / (b) playing cricket / (c) every evening. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). Used to for a past habit is followed by the base verb: we used to play cricket.

Q2. (a) Did you use to / (b) live in / (c) Pune? / (d) No error.

Answer: (d). After did, the form is use to with no d. The sentence is correct.

Q3. (a) She is used to / (b) work late / (c) at night. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). Be used to means accustomed, and to is a preposition, so the verb takes -ing: working late.

Q4. (a) When he was young, / (b) he would be / (c) very naughty. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). Being naughty is a state, and would cannot describe a past state. The correct form is he used to be.

Q5. (a) I was going to / (b) tell you / (c) about it yesterday. / (d) No error.

Answer: (d). Was going to correctly expresses a plan in the past. There is no error.

Q6. I ______ a lot of tea when I was in college, but now I prefer coffee. (a) use to drink (b) used to drinking (c) used to drink (d) am used to drink

Answer: (c). A past habit that has stopped takes used to plus the base verb.

Q7. (a) My father didn't / (b) used to smoke / (c) before he retired. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). After didn't, the form is use to: my father didn't use to smoke.

Q8. He is getting used to ______ in a new city. (a) live (b) living (c) lived (d) lives

Answer: (b). Get used to means becoming accustomed, and to is a preposition, so the verb takes -ing.

The Student Who Used Would Everywhere

Imagine a student named Sneha. In school, her teacher once praised a sentence she wrote: "On Sundays, we would visit my grandmother." Since then she has believed that would sounds more elegant than used to, so she uses it everywhere.

In her practice test, she meets "When I was a child, I used to be afraid of dogs" and changes it to "I would be afraid of dogs". The answer key marks her wrong. The sentence is about a state, being afraid, and would can't describe states.

Her teacher doesn't tell her to stop using would. Instead, she gives her a small drill: write ten sentences about your childhood and mark each verb as state or action. By the end of the exercise, Sneha has noticed something useful. Most of her sentences about feelings and situations need used to, and only her sentences about routines can take would. The lesson she keeps is not a rule to memorise but a habit of checking the verb type before choosing the pattern.

Expert Tips to Remember

  • When in doubt, choose used to. It works for both habits and states, while would only works for repeated actions.
  • Write ten sentences about your own childhood. Label each as a state or an action. This is the best revision exercise for this topic.
  • Remember the spelling shift: used to in affirmative sentences, use to after did or didn't.
  • For be used to and get used to, remember that to is a preposition. Always use -ing after it.
  • Don't use used to for single events. If the sentence names a specific date or one-time occasion, use the simple past instead.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Used to plus base verb: past habits and past states that no longer continue.
  • Would: repeated past actions only, never states such as live, be or have.
  • After did or didn't, write use to with no d.
  • Be used to and get used to mean accustomed, and they take -ing.
  • Was or were going to: a plan in the past, often one that did not happen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can would always replace used to?

No. Would works only for repeated actions, usually when the time frame is clear. It can not replace used to for states such as live, be or have.

Why is it didn't use to and not didn't used to?

Because didn't already shows the past tense. The main verb goes back to its base form, as in didn't go or didn't eat. Exam papers expect didn't use to.

What is the difference between used to and be used to?

Used to plus a base verb describes a past habit that has stopped. Be used to plus -ing or a noun means you are accustomed to something now.

Is was going to the same as would?

Not exactly. Was going to describes a plan or intention in the past, often one that didn't happen. Would in reported speech gives a future as seen from the past, such as He said he would call.

Can I use used to for a single past event?

No. Used to describes repeated or long-term past situations. For a single event, use the simple past: I visited Delhi in 2019.

Final Thoughts

Used to, would and was going to look like small expressions, but they decide a surprising number of marks. Remember the single idea that organises everything: states need used to, repeated actions can take either, and plans in the past take was going to.
