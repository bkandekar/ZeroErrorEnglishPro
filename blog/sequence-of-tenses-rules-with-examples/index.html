Sequence of Tenses: Rules for Main Clause and Subordinate Clause with Examples

Understand sequence of tenses with simple rules, a clear exceptions list, solved exam-style questions and a memory trick for main and subordinate clauses.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
Past main clause = Past subordinate clause (will to would, can to could, may to might); exceptions: universal truth, habitual fact, comparison

Here is a sentence from a typical practice set: "He hoped that his son will pass the examination." Does it feel wrong to you? For many students it doesn't. And that is precisely why sequence of tenses is one of the most reliable scoring areas for examiners.

The idea is simple: when one verb sits in the past, the verb in its partner clause has to respect that. The trouble is the exceptions, because a few special cases refuse to follow the rule, and examiners love to test exactly those. In this post you'll get the main rule, the exceptions in one place, a memory test you can run in three seconds, and solved questions in the usual SSC style.

What Is Sequence of Tenses?

A sentence with two parts has a main clause, which can stand alone, and a subordinate clause, which depends on the main clause. In "She said that she was tired", the main clause is "She said" and the subordinate clause is "that she was tired".

Sequence of tenses is the rule that decides which tense the subordinate clause takes depending on the tense of the main clause. Think of the main verb as the leader. If the leader is walking in the past, the follower can't suddenly walk in the present, unless the follower is carrying something that never expires. We'll come to that shortly.

The rule matters most for three kinds of subordinate clauses: noun clauses beginning with that, adverb clauses of time, reason and purpose, and adjective clauses. In every one of them, the same logic applies.

Rule 1: Past Main Clause, Past Subordinate Clause

When the main clause verb is in any past tense, the verb in the subordinate clause also moves into a past form. The change follows a predictable pattern.

Idea in the subordinate clauseBecomesExample
Simple present ideaSimple pastI knew that he lived in Pune.
Present continuous ideaPast continuousShe saw that it was raining.
Present perfect ideaPast perfectHe said that he had finished.
Earlier past actionPast perfectI learned that the train had left.
will / shallwould / shouldHe promised that he would help.
can / maycould / mightShe said that she could swim.

Read the examples aloud a few times. Notice how natural they sound once the pattern settles in your ear. "I knew that he lived in Pune" feels more complete than "I knew that he lives in Pune", doesn't it?

Two small reminders. First, the earlier of two past actions takes the past perfect, which is why "I learned that the train had left" is correct. Second, modals shift in pairs: will to would, shall to should, can to could, may to might. Learn these four pairs by heart and a good share of the rule is already in your pocket.

Rule 2: Present or Future Main Clause, Any Tense Follows

If the main clause is in the present or future, the subordinate clause is free. It can take whichever tense the meaning needs.

For example: "He says that he is tired", "He says that he was tired yesterday", "He says that he will come tomorrow". All three are correct because the main verb says is present and doesn't force the follower anywhere.

The same freedom exists when the main clause is in the present perfect: "She has told me that she will join us." This is why you should never apply the backshift rule mechanically. Check the main verb first, and only then decide if the rule even applies.

The Exceptions Every Examiner Loves

Now the part where marks are won and lost. Even when the main clause is in the past, the subordinate clause can stay in the present or take any tense in the following cases.

1. Universal truths. A fact that is always true doesn't step back. "The teacher said that the earth revolves around the sun." "We learned that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius."

2. Habitual or permanent facts. "She said that she gets up at five every day." When the sentence describes a habit or a permanent fact, the present tense stays.

3. Historical facts. Here the opposite happens: the verb stays in the simple past even if the main clause is present. "The teacher tells us that India became independent in 1947." Past events don't shift forward.

4. Comparison with than or as. A clause of comparison can take any tense the meaning demands. "He was stronger than he is now." "She loved him more than she loves anyone else."

5. Purpose clauses. After a past main clause, a clause of purpose uses would, could or might. "He worked hard so that he might pass." Not "so that he will pass".

Five exceptions can feel like a lot, but they reduce to one idea, which brings us to the memory test below.

Three Questions Solved the Way You Should Solve Them in the Exam

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) He hoped that / (b) his son will pass / (c) the examination. / (d) No error.

The main verb "hoped" is past. The subordinate clause needs a past form, so "will pass" must become "would pass". Answer: (b).

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) I always knew that / (b) honesty was / (c) the best policy. / (d) No error.

Honesty being the best policy is a general truth. It never expires, so the verb should stay present: "honesty is the best policy". Answer: (b). Students often see "knew" and automatically push "is" back to "was". That is the trap.

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: (a) She worked hard / (b) so that she will / (c) get a first class. / (d) No error.

A purpose clause after a past main clause takes would, could or might. "So that she will" should be "so that she might" or "would". Answer: (b).

The Time-Expiry Test (3 Seconds)

  • Step 1: Is the main verb in the past? If no, the rule does not apply.
  • Step 2: Ask, does this fact ever expire? Earth revolves, water boils, honesty pays: they never expire, so keep the present.
  • Step 3: If it can expire, step the verb back: will to would, can to could, may to might, shall to should.

Ask only one question: Does this fact ever expire? Expires means step back. Never expires means stay present.

The 5 Sequence of Tenses Mistakes That Cost Marks

Students make two kinds of mistakes here: forgetting to step back when they should, and stepping back when they shouldn't. These five cover both directions.

5 Mistakes to Avoid
Mistake 1
✗ Wrong: I thought that he is honest.
✓ Right: I thought that he was honest.
A past main verb (thought) pulls the subordinate verb back into the past.
Mistake 2
✗ Wrong: He worked hard so that he will pass.
✓ Right: He worked hard so that he might pass.
A purpose clause after a past main verb uses would, could or might.
Mistake 3
✗ Wrong: The teacher said that the sun rose in the east.
✓ Right: The teacher said that the sun rises in the east.
A universal truth never steps back, even after a past main verb.
Mistake 4
✗ Wrong: I knew that he has already left.
✓ Right: I knew that he had already left.
Earlier action relative to a past main verb takes the past perfect.
Mistake 5
✗ Wrong: She said that she can solve the problem.
✓ Right: She said that she could solve the problem.
Can steps back to could after a past reporting verb.

Notice mistakes 1, 2, 4 and 5 forgot to step back, while mistake 3 stepped back when it shouldn't have. Two directions, one test: does the fact expire? You can see why the Time-Expiry Test above is worth learning today and not on the night before the exam.

The 6-Step Method for Any Sequence of Tenses Question

Use this routine on every question where two clauses are joined by that, so that, because, when or than.

Your 6-Step Method
1
Find the main verb
Locate the verb of the main clause and note its tense.
2
Is it past?
If the main verb is present or future, the subordinate clause is free. Stop.
3
Name the clause type
Noun clause, purpose clause, comparison, time clause. Each has its own behaviour.
4
Run the expiry test
Does the fact expire? Universal or habitual facts stay in the present.
5
Step back the verb
If it expires, move the verb back one step and change will, can, may, shall to would, could, might, should.
6
Check connectors
Confirm that, so that, than and as agree with the new verb form.

Once you've practised twenty questions, steps 1 and 2 happen in the blink of an eye, and most of your thinking time goes to the expiry test, which is where marks are actually decided.

Practice Set with Answers

Try each question on your own first. Use the clue method before you read the answer, and note which clue you used. Then check your reasoning against the explanation.

Q1. (a) The teacher said / (b) that the earth / (c) moved round the sun. / (d) No error.

Answer: (c). A universal truth stays in the simple present: the earth moves round the sun.

Q2. (a) I thought that / (b) he is / (c) a good singer. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). The main verb thought is past, so the subordinate verb steps back: he was a good singer.

Q3. (a) He ran fast / (b) so that he / (c) would catch the bus. / (d) No error.

Answer: (d). A purpose clause after a past main verb takes would, could or might, so this sentence is correct.

Q4. (a) She told me / (b) that she can / (c) finish the work. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). Can steps back to could after a past reporting verb: that she could finish the work.

Q5. He worked harder than he ______ last year. (a) works (b) worked (c) has worked (d) work

Answer: (b). A than clause can take any tense the meaning needs. Last year points to the past, so worked fits.

Q6. (a) I learnt that / (b) the train left / (c) before we reached. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). The train left earlier than we reached, so the earlier action takes the past perfect: the train had left.

Q7. (a) The doctor advised / (b) that exercise keeps / (c) the body healthy. / (d) No error.

Answer: (d). Exercise keeping the body healthy is a general truth. It stays in the present, so there is no error.

Q8. (a) My friend promised / (b) that he will / (c) help me. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). After a past main verb, will becomes would: that he would help me.

Why Students Overuse the Exceptions

Picture a student I'll call Neha. She works through the sequence of tenses rules on a Sunday and feels confident. On Monday, she meets the sentence "The teacher said that Gandhi was born in 1869." Remembering that "facts don't change tense", she marks it wrong and writes "is born".

The mistake isn't carelessness. Neha learned the universal truth exception as a slogan: facts stay present. But a birth date isn't a universal truth; it's a historical event. "Gandhi is born in 1869" doesn't make sense at all. The right test is not "is it a fact?" but "does it expire or stay forever true right now?".

That's why I insist on the expiry question. It forces you to think about the meaning, not the label. After Neha started asking "Is this still true today, in all times?", her accuracy on these questions improved noticeably within a couple of weeks of practice.

Expert Tips to Remember

  • Learn the modal pairs cold: will and would, shall and should, can and could, may and might. Write them on a card and carry it.
  • Never apply the rule when the main verb is in the present or future. Check the main verb before you touch the other clause.
  • Treat purpose clauses (so that, in order that) as a separate pattern. They love would, could and might after past verbs.
  • When you see a general truth like the earth, water, honesty or the sun, pause before you step back the verb.
  • Revise your error log weekly and group mistakes by direction: forgot to step back or stepped back wrongly.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Check the main verb first. If it is present or future, the subordinate clause is free.
  • Past main verb: step the subordinate verb back, and change will, can, may and shall to would, could, might and should.
  • Ask the Time-Expiry Test: does the fact ever expire? Universal and habitual facts stay in the present.
  • Purpose clauses after a past verb use would, could or might.
  • Than clauses take any tense the meaning needs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sequence of tenses the same as reported speech?

They are close cousins. Reported speech uses the same backshifting logic, but sequence of tenses is the wider rule that works in any sentence with a main and subordinate clause. If you want the reported speech side, read our post on backshifting secrets in direct and indirect speech.

What if the main clause is in the present perfect?

The present perfect behaves like a present tense for this purpose, so the subordinate clause can take any tense that suits the meaning: She has said that she will join us.

Is He said that he is ill always wrong?

In everyday speech it is acceptable when the illness continues. Exam papers, however, expect the backshifted form (was ill) unless the statement is a universal truth. So follow the exam rule in the exam.

Which tense follows than in comparison?

Any tense that the meaning needs. A than clause is an exception to the backshift rule, as in: He was richer than he is now.

Why does so that take would or might after a past verb?

Because the purpose is seen from a past point of view. A past main verb sets the time frame, and the purpose clause borrows the past-in-future form, which is would, could or might.

Final Thoughts

Sequence of tenses isn't a long list of rules. It's one rule (past leads, past follows) and one test (does the fact expire?). Practise that pairing for ten minutes a day and you'll see these questions change from risky to routine.
