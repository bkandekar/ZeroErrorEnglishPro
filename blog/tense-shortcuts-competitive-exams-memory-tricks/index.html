Tense Shortcuts for Competitive Exams: Easy Memory Tricks to Pick the Right Tense

By Balu Kandekar · Published 2026-10-26 · 14 min read

Try this in ten seconds: (A) When have you / (B) come to Pune / (C) for the first time? / (D) No error.

Most students read it twice, find it natural and pick (D). It sounds natural because we say “Have you come to Pune?” all the time. But the moment you add “when,” the tense has to change. The correct question is “When did you come to Pune?” and the error sits quietly in part (A).

That’s how tense questions work in competitive exams. The sentence rarely looks broken. One small time word decides which tense is allowed, and examiners hide that word where your eye won’t land. Students who memorise twelve tense formulas still miss it. Students who learn to hunt for the time word catch it.

How tenses are tested in exams

Tense questions don’t announce themselves. They hide inside three familiar formats, and knowing them tells you where to look.

1. Error spotting. A sentence is split into parts (A), (B), (C), and (D) is “No error.” The time word sits in one part and the faulty verb in another.

2. Fill in the blanks. You choose between forms such as did, has done and had done. The time word in the sentence decides.

3. Sentence improvement. A part is underlined, and the four options differ only in the tense of the verb.

A note on the examples in this post: every sentence is an original practice sentence on a common exam pattern. None of them is copied from a particular year’s paper, and I haven’t attached any year to them. The patterns are what repeat, and the patterns are what you should learn.

The LOOK, LABEL, LOCK routine

You can’t recall twelve tense formulas with the clock running. You need one routine that works on every sentence.

“LOOK, LABEL, LOCK.”

LOOK for the time word: yesterday, already, by the time, since, every day
LABEL the time: past, present, future, or up to now
LOCK the tense that fits that label, then check the verb against it

Say the three words silently on every sentence. It takes about five seconds, and it stops you from judging the verb by how it sounds.

10 tense shortcuts with examples

Trick 1: Facts Don’t Age

✗ The teacher said that water boiled at 100 degrees Celsius.
✓ The teacher said that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.

A universal truth or a habit stays in the simple present, even in reported speech. Memory trick: “Facts don’t age.” For the other backshift rules, see the tense changes in direct and indirect speech chart.

Trick 2: Every Day, No -ing

✗ She is going to school every day.
✓ She goes to school every day.

Words like always, usually, often and every day call the simple present, not the continuous. Memory trick: “Every day, no -ing.”

Trick 3: Mind Verbs Don’t March

✗ I am knowing the answer.
✓ I know the answer.

Verbs of thinking, feeling and owning (know, like, love, want, believe, understand, belong) are rarely used in the continuous form. Memory trick: “Mind verbs don’t march.”

Trick 4: Already Is a Yes, Yet Is a No

✗ She has finished her work yet.
✓ She has already finished her work.

Already goes with something that has happened. Yet goes with something that hasn’t happened, or with a question, as in “She hasn’t finished her work yet.” Memory trick: “Already is a yes. Yet is a no.”

Trick 5: When Wants Did

✗ When have you come to Pune?
✓ When did you come to Pune?

A question that begins with when or what time asks about a finished moment, so it takes the simple past. Memory trick: “When wants did.”

Trick 6: Long Was, Short Did

✗ She was reading when the phone was ringing.
✓ She was reading when the phone rang.

The long background action takes the past continuous. The short action that interrupts it takes the simple past. Memory trick: “Long was, short did.”

Trick 7: Hardly Had, No Sooner Had

✗ No sooner he reached home than it started raining.
✓ No sooner had he reached home than it started raining.

Hardly and no sooner open the sentence and always bring had and an inverted subject. Hardly pairs with when, and no sooner pairs with than. Memory trick: “Hardly and Sooner always bring Had.”

Trick 8: By the Time Brings Had

✗ By the time we reached, the film started.
✓ By the time we reached, the film had started.

When by the time points to a past moment, the earlier action takes the past perfect. Memory trick: “By the time = had done.”

Trick 9: Wish Walks Backwards

✗ I wish I have a bigger house.
✓ I wish I had a bigger house.

After wish, as if, would rather and it is time, an unreal present takes the past form. The same pattern appears in third and mixed conditionals. Memory trick: “Wish, as if, it’s time: step back.”

Trick 10: First Time, Have

✗ This is the first time I visited Pune.
✓ This is the first time I have visited Pune.

After “this is the first time,” use the present perfect. In the past, “it was the first time I had visited” takes the past perfect. Memory trick: “First time, have.”

Three trap sentences examiners reuse

Once you know the ten shortcuts, these three disguises are easy to spot.

Trap 1: The hidden finished time. “I have visited Agra last year.” Last year kills the present perfect, so use “I visited Agra last year.”

Trap 2: The unbacked reported verb. “She told me that she will join the next day.” The reporting verb is past, so use “would join.”

Trap 3: The ear test. “The sun rose in the east” may sound fine, but a fact needs the simple present, so use “rises.”

Notice the pattern. In each trap, the verb sounds fine on its own. It only fails when you check it against the time word, which is why LOOK always comes first.

The 5-step error spotting method

LOOK, LABEL, LOCK tells you what to do. This routine tells you how to run it from start to finish.

Step 1: Read the sentence once. Underline the verb.

Step 2: LOOK. Circle every time word, including small ones like when, by the time and yet.

Step 3: LABEL. Decide whether the time is past, present, future or up to now.

Step 4: LOCK. Choose the tense that fits the label and test your fix by reading the corrected sentence.

Step 5: Only then choose “No error.” It’s a conclusion, never a default.

A 12-second time plan

Here’s a plan that works for most students. Adjust the seconds to suit your pace.

0 to 3 seconds: read once and underline the verb.

3 to 7 seconds: LOOK for the time word and LABEL it.

7 to 11 seconds: LOCK the tense and compare it with the verb.

11 to 12 seconds: mark the answer and move on. If you’re stuck, skip it and return.

A pattern I see in almost every batch

Here’s something that repeats year after year in my classes. A student can list all twelve tenses with their formulas, then picks “No error” on “I have visited Agra last year.” The formulas are there. The signal isn’t.

The fix is a habit, not more theory. Take ten sentences from any newspaper, circle every time word and write its tense label beside it. Ten sentences a day for two weeks trains your eyes to land on the time word before the verb, and that’s the whole skill.

Expert tips for exam day

Read the time word before the verb. The time word is the question, and the verb is only the answer.

Trust the signal over your ear. “It sounds fine” is why most tense errors survive.

Check small words. When, by the time, yet and already are short, so eyes skip them.

Keep an error log. Write down every shortcut you miss and revise that list for a week.

Three mistakes to avoid

Learning the trick without the rule. A hook only reminds you. Know why the rule works.

Using the wrong trick. Each shortcut has a trigger. Check that the trigger is really in the sentence.

Learning all ten in one evening. It feels productive and fades by morning. Two a day holds far better.

Practice set: 9 error spotting questions

Find the part with the error, or choose (D) if there’s none. These are original practice sentences written for this guide. Try them before you read the answers.

  1. The teacher said that (A) / the sun rose (B) / in the east. (C) / No error (D)
  2. I am knowing (A) / the answer (B) / to this question. (C) / No error (D)
  3. Have you (A) / finished your work (B) / yet? (C) / No error (D)
  4. When have (A) / you come (B) / to Pune? (C) / No error (D)
  5. She was reading (A) / when the phone (B) / was ringing. (C) / No error (D)
  6. No sooner (A) / he reached home (B) / than it started raining. (C) / No error (D)
  7. I wish (A) / I have (B) / a bigger house. (C) / No error (D)
  8. This is the first time (A) / I visited (B) / Pune. (C) / No error (D)
  9. By the time we reached, (A) / the film (B) / had started. (C) / No error (D)

Answers

1. (B): a fact stays in the simple present, so use “rises.” (Trick 1)

2. (A): know is a mind verb, so use “I know.” (Trick 3)

3. (D): no error. Yet is correct in a question. (Trick 4)

4. (A): when wants did, so use “When did.” (Trick 5)

5. (C): the short interrupting action takes the simple past, so use “rang.” (Trick 6)

6. (B): no sooner brings had, so use “had he reached.” (Trick 7)

7. (B): wish walks backwards, so use “I had.” (Trick 9)

8. (B): first time brings have, so use “I have visited.” (Trick 10)

9. (D): no error. By the time brings had, and it’s already there. (Trick 8)

If you scored 8 or 9, you’re ready for harder questions. If you scored 5 to 7, revise the shortcuts you missed. If you scored below 5, go back to LOOK, LABEL, LOCK and the chart, and retake the set after two days.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose the right tense in error spotting questions?

Look for the time word, label the time as past, present, future or up to now, and lock the tense that fits. The time word decides the tense more often than the verb does.

What is the difference between already and yet?

Already goes with something that has happened. Yet goes with something that hasn’t happened or with a question. “She has already left” and “Has she left yet?” are both correct.

Can I use the present perfect with yesterday or last year?

No. Words that point to a finished time need the simple past. Say “I visited Agra last year,” not “I have visited Agra last year.”

Which tenses are tested most often?

The present perfect, the past perfect and the simple tenses appear often in practice papers. Check the latest official notification for your exam pattern.

Why does wish take the past form?

Because wish talks about an unreal present. “I wish I had a bigger house” means I don’t have one now, so the verb steps back to show it isn’t real.
