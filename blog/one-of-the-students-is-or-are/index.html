“One of the Students Is” or “Are”? Cracking the Most Confusing Agreement Traps in Error Spotting

Confused about 'one of the students is' or 'are'? Master one-of, number-of and more-than-one agreement traps with examples and practice.

Core Governing Rule Formula:
One of + plural = singular | one of those who = plural | a number of = plural | the number of = singular.

“One of the students are absent.” Your ear hears “students” and shouts “are!”. That's exactly what the examiner is counting on.

Of all the agreement traps in error spotting, this family is the most popular. It uses short, simple words, such as one, number, many and most, and it works because students recognise the sentence shape and answer without thinking. I've seen bright students lose marks on these patterns simply because they read too fast.

Here's the plan. You'll learn six traps, each with a clear rule, examples and a memory trick. Then we'll go through four worked sentences, the five mistakes students make, ten exam traps in one table, and a ten-sentence practice set with answers.

Why These Patterns Fool Even Good Students

There's a reason strong students lose marks here. These patterns use words we say every day, so the brain treats the sentence as familiar and speeds through it. Familiar sentences feel safe, and safe feelings switch off the checking habit.

Add one more factor. In normal speech, we match the verb to the word that comes just before it. That habit works in conversation, and it breaks when the noun before the verb is only a decoy. Examiners know this, so they place a plural noun, such as students or books, directly before the verb.

The cure is not more grammar knowledge. It's a short pause. Before you choose the verb, name the real subject out loud in your mind: one, number, pair, most. That two-second pause protects the mark.

Trap 1: One of + Plural Noun

The subject of this sentence is the word one. The phrase “of the students” only tells you which group the one belongs to. So the verb is singular.

  • One of the students is absent.
  • One of my friends has won the prize.
  • One of the reasons for the delay was poor planning.
Memory trick: Cover the Of-Phrase
Cover “of the students” with your finger. What's left is “One ___ absent.” The verb is obviously is.

Why do students get it wrong? Because the plural noun students sits right beside the verb. The brain says “students are” and the eye follows. Train yourself to find the first noun, not the nearest noun.

Trap 2: One of Those Who and The Only One Who

Now the rule flips. When the pattern is one of those who or one of the few who, the verb belongs to the word who. The word who points back to the plural noun, so the verb is plural.

  • He is one of those students who work hard.
  • She is one of the girls who sing well.
  • It is one of the best books that have ever been written.

Read the first sentence carefully. Many students share this quality of working hard, and he is one of them. That's why who refers to the group, students, and the verb is plural.

Now compare it with the version that includes the only:

  • He is the only one of the students who works hard.
  • She is the only one of the girls who sings well.

Here, only one person has the quality. Who now refers to one, so the verb is singular. The words “the only” change the meaning, and the verb follows the meaning.

Memory trick: Many or Only One
“One of those who” means many share the quality, so go plural. “The only one who” means only one has it, so go singular.

Trap 3: A Number of vs The Number of

These two phrases look almost identical, but one tiny word flips the verb.

PhraseMeaningVerbExample
A number ofManyPluralA number of books are missing.
The number ofOne totalSingularThe number of applicants is growing.
Memory trick: A = Many, THE = One
A number = many things (plural verb). The number = a single count (singular verb).

Why do examiners love this? Because the difference is one small word. Students who skim past small words, a and the, lose the mark. Slow down on this pattern. Read the first two words, then choose the verb.

Trap 4: More Than One, Many a and One or Two

Three more sneaky patterns, and each looks plural but isn't, or the reverse:

  • More than one candidate has failed. (Singular. The noun right after is singular, so the verb is singular.)
  • Many a student has regretted it. (Singular. It describes one student at a time, many times over.)
  • One or two mistakes are acceptable. (Plural. Two is plural, and the verb agrees with it.)

The pattern here is that the verb follows the noun form that comes after the counting words. Candidate and student are singular, so the verbs are singular. Mistakes is plural, so the verb is plural.

Trap 5: Most of, Half of, Two-Thirds of and The Rest of

Quantity words followed by of, such as all of, some of, most of, half of, two-thirds of, a lot of, plenty of and the rest of, follow a clean rule: the verb agrees with the noun after of.

Singular noun after ofPlural noun after of
Most of the water is clean.Most of the students are present.
Half of the cake was eaten.Half of the cakes were eaten.
Two-thirds of the work is complete.Two-thirds of the students are absent.
The rest of the money is safe.The rest of the books are torn.

This is the opposite of the one-of rule, so students often mix the two. Remember: with one of, the verb ignores the noun after of. With most of, half of, the rest of, the verb follows it.

Exam Note
Percentages follow the same rule. “Twenty per cent of the students are absent.” but “Twenty per cent of the work is complete.” The noun after of decides.

Trap 6: A Pair of, A Series of and A Set of

Some words, such as pair, series and set, name a single unit made of several parts. The subject is the unit word, and the verb is singular.

  • A pair of gloves is lying on the table.
  • A series of explosions was heard last night.
  • A set of rules has been issued.

Compare this with the plural nouns that always take plural verbs, such as scissors or trousers: “The scissors are sharp.” I've covered those in my cheat sheet on news, mathematics and physics.

A 4-Question Decision Path

When a sentence contains an of-phrase and a counting word, run through these four questions in order. Together they handle nearly every pattern in this post.

  1. Does the sentence start with one, a pair, a series or a set? Then the subject is that word, and the verb is singular.
  2. Does it say one of those who, one of the few who or one of the best that? Then the verb is plural, because who or that points to the group.
  3. Does it say a number of or the number of? A number of is plural, and the number of is singular.
  4. Does it start with most, half, some, the rest or a fraction? Then look at the noun after of, and the verb follows it.

If none of the four fits, go back to the Cross-Out Test from the beginning of this series and find the real subject the usual way. Between the decision path and the Cross-Out Test, you've got a method for almost every sentence you'll meet.

Four Sentences, Four Decisions

  1. One of the boys who were late was punished. Careful! The subject of “was punished” is one, so the verb is singular. The “who were late” part belongs to the boys.
  2. He is one of those who always arrive early. One of those who: plural verb (arrive).
  3. The number of cars on the road has increased. The number of: singular verb (has).
  4. Most of the audience were standing. Audience here means the members, so the verb follows the sense. In exams, treat unclear cases as singular unless a split clue appears.

The first sentence is the subtle one. It has two verbs, were and was, and each agrees with a different subject. That's how the examiner adds difficulty without adding a new rule.

5 Mistakes Students Make With These Patterns

  1. Matching the verb to the nearest noun. This is the root of almost every error in this post. Find the first noun, not the nearest.
  2. Mixing up one of the and one of those who. They look alike but behave oppositely. Check whether who follows.
  3. Skipping the small words. A number and the number differ by one word and one verb form.
  4. Treating more than one as plural. The noun after more than one is singular, so the verb is singular.
  5. Applying the one-of rule to most of. With one of, ignore the noun after of. With most of, follow it.

If you can avoid these five slips, you'll handle nearly every sentence in this family.

Case Study: Two Questions, Two Different Traps

Here are two SSC CGL exam pattern practice questions. Each hides a different trap.

Question 1
One of the reasons (A) / for the delay (B) / were poor planning. (C) / No error (D)

The subject is one, not reasons. So the verb should be was. The error is in C.

Question 2
He is one of those teachers who (A) / believes in (B) / hard work. (C) / No error (D)

One of those teachers who: plural. Who points to teachers, so the verb should be believe. The error is in B. Notice how the first question uses the singular rule and the second the plural rule, using almost the same words.

4 Tips From the Classroom

Tip 1: Find the first noun

Underline the first noun or pointer word in the subject, such as one, number or pair. That's usually the real subject.

Tip 2: Look for the who

If you see who after a plural noun, ask whether the sentence says only one. If yes, singular. If no, plural.

Tip 3: Respect small words

A, the, only, many and more change the whole rule. Circle them before you choose the verb.

Tip 4: Revise in pairs

Study one of the and one of those who together, and a number of and the number of together. Contrast makes memory stronger.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is it “One of the students is” and not “are”?
The subject is the word one. The phrase “of the students” only describes which one, so it doesn't decide the verb.

How do I tell “one of those who” from “the only one who”?
With “one of those who”, many people share the quality, so the verb is plural. With “the only one who”, a single person has it, so the verb is singular.

Why does “more than one candidate” take a singular verb?
Grammar follows the noun form that comes right after it. Candidate is singular, so the verb is singular.

Is “a pair of shoes” singular or plural?
The subject is pair, so it's singular: “A pair of shoes is on the shelf.”

What is the difference between “a number of” and “the number of”?
A number of means many and takes a plural verb. The number of refers to a total and takes a singular verb.

Do percentages and fractions take singular or plural verbs?
The verb agrees with the noun after of. “Half of the cake was eaten” and “Half of the cakes were eaten” are both correct.
