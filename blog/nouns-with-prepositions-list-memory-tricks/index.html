What Are Nouns with Prepositions?

A noun names a thing, an idea or a feeling, like reason, demand or respect. Many nouns need a particular preposition to link them to what comes next. You can't just say "a demand" and stop; you say a demand for something. The noun and its preposition work as a single unit, which is why they're called word partners or dependent prepositions.

There's no rule that tells you which preposition belongs to which noun. But the pairs aren't random either. Many follow a pattern, and many borrow the preposition from the verb or adjective they come from. If you haven't read the basics yet, start with my guide on what fixed prepositions are. You may also like the companion post on adjectives with prepositions.

Why These Pairs Decide Your Score

In a typical English paper, noun and preposition pairs sit quietly inside error spotting sentences, fill in the blanks and cloze passages. A wrong preposition after a noun rarely looks wrong, because the sentence still makes sense. That's why it works so well as an exam trap.

These pairs also repeat. Reason, cause, solution, demand, respect, increase and effect appear in paper after paper, dressed in new sentences. Learn the list once, and you can pick up marks in SSC, bank, railway and state exams for months. Few topics give you that return for ten minutes a day.

5 Memory Tricks for Noun and Preposition Pairs

A list of this size can't be memorised line by line. These five tricks turn it into something your brain can hold.

Trick 1: Borrow from the verb or adjective

Many nouns keep the preposition of the word they come from. Depend on becomes dependence on. Rely on becomes reliance on. Interested in becomes interest in. React to becomes reaction to. Believe in becomes belief in. Apologise for becomes apology for. If you know the verb pair, you often get the noun pair free.

Watch for the exceptions, which exams love. Prefer to but preference for. Differ from but difference between. These are worth a separate note in your notebook.

Trick 2: Learn the family, not the pair

Don't memorise solution, answer, key and reply separately. Learn that "answer words take TO" and you have four pairs. Do the same with the others: need, demand and desire (wants take FOR); increase, rise and fall (changes take IN).

Trick 3: Draw a tiny picture

Pictures beat rules. For cause of, picture a row of dominoes: the first one is the cause of the whole fall. For effect on, imagine a stone landing on water and making ripples. For demand for, see customers shouting for a product. For access to, picture a key that gets you to the room. Silly pictures stay longer than sensible ones.

Trick 4: Count the sides

If a noun needs two things to make sense, it takes between. A difference, a gap, a link, a choice and a conflict all need two sides. You can't have a difference of one thing. So "difference of the two" is always wrong, and "difference between the two" is right.

Trick 5: Write one sentence about your own life

Link each tricky pair to something real. "My love for cricket began in school." "My dad always asks for the reason for a late return." A sentence that carries your own memory sticks far better than a textbook example.

Confusing Pairs: Same Noun, Different Preposition

Some nouns take different prepositions depending on the meaning. These trip up even good students.

NounPair and meaningExample
cause / reasoncause of = what produces an effect; reason for = why someone does or says somethingRain was the cause of the delay. Give a reason for the delay.
advantageadvantage of = a benefit; advantage over = better than someoneExercise has many advantages. She has an advantage over her rivals.
differencebetween two things; in a qualityThe difference between the two is small. There's a difference in price.
influenceon or over a personHis teacher has a great influence on him.
attitudeto or towardsHer attitude to work is serious.

A quick tip: if you're not sure, cause of and reason for are your safest anchors. Heavy rain is the cause of floods, and the reason for staying home.

The 6-Step Method to Learn the List

Ten minutes a day, done every day, is enough. Here's the routine.

  1. Pick one family a day. Start with TO, then FOR, then OF, and so on.
  2. Read the pairs aloud twice, with the family idea in your head.
  3. Choose five pairs you didn't know and attach a picture or a person to each.
  4. Write one sentence for each of the five, about your own life.
  5. Revise after 1, 3 and 7 days. Cover the preposition and recall it from the noun.
  6. Test with exam questions. A few error spotting or fill in the blanks questions close the session.

How Exams Test Nouns with Prepositions

Noun and preposition pairs appear in error spotting, fill in the blanks, sentence improvement and cloze passages. Here are four questions in the usual style.

SSC CGL Exam Pattern: Error Spotting
There has been a sharp rise on (A) / the price of onions (B) / this month. (C) / No error (D)
Answer: A. It should be "rise in".
Common Exam Pattern: Error Spotting
She has great respect (A) / on her teachers (B) / and always obeys them. (C) / No error (D)
Answer: B. It should be "respect for".
Common Exam Pattern: Fill in the blank
The government has imposed a ban ___ plastic bags.
Answer: on.
Common Exam Pattern: Sentence improvement
There is no solution of this problem.
Answer: solution to this problem.

A five-second way to attack these

Find the noun first. In the first question it's "rise", and its partner for price changes is "in", but the sentence says "on". Done. Don't try to understand the whole sentence; check the noun and the word right after it.

Expert Tips

  • Read the news and underline the pairs. Headlines are full of them: rise in prices, ban on exports, demand for jobs.
  • Don't trust "it sounds right". Your ear may have been trained on wrong usage. A good dictionary shows the partner in its examples.
  • Learn the exceptions together: prefer to but preference for; differ from but difference between.
  • Play safe in exams. Where two forms exist, such as attitude to and attitude towards, both are accepted. Choose the one you remember best and stay consistent.
  • Fix your weakest family first. If you keep missing IN-words, spend three days on them before moving on.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are nouns with prepositions?
They are nouns that always go with a particular preposition to complete their meaning, such as reason for, cause of and solution to. They are also called dependent prepositions or word partners.

Is it solution of or solution to?
Solution to is correct, as in a solution to a problem. Answer, key, reply and response also take to.

Is it reason for or reason of?
Reason for is the correct form. For example, the reason for the delay. The noun cause takes of: the cause of the delay.

Which preposition follows difference?
Use between when you compare two things, as in the difference between tea and coffee. Use in when you talk about a quality, as in a difference in price.

How many noun and preposition pairs do I need for competitive exams?
There is no official number, but a few dozen common pairs cover most questions. Learn them by family and focus on pairs that repeat in exam practice.

Can the same noun take two prepositions?
Yes. Some nouns, like influence, take on or over, and attitude takes to or towards. Others change meaning, such as advantage of and advantage over. Learn those contrast pairs together.
