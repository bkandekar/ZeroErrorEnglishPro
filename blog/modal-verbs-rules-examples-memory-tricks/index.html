What are modal verbs?

A modal verb is a helping verb that adds a shade of meaning to the main verb. It doesn't tell you what action is happening. It tells you how the speaker feels about that action: is it possible, allowed, necessary, advisable or certain?

Look at these four sentences. She sings states a fact. She can sing talks about ability. She may sing talks about permission or possibility. She must sing talks about obligation. The main verb never changed. Only the modal did, and the whole message shifted. That's the entire job of a modal.

The nine core modal verbs are can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would and must. Then there's a smaller second group called semi-modals: ought to, need, dare and used to. They behave like modals in some sentences and like ordinary verbs in others, and that's where a good number of tricky exam questions come from.

Some textbooks count modals slightly differently, so don't panic if your coaching notes show another list. For exams, learn the nine core modals plus ought to, and treat need, dare and used to as special cases.

The five rules (and a trick to remember them)

Nearly every modal verb error in competitive exams breaks one of five rules. Learn these and you'll catch the mistake before you've finished reading the sentence.

Memory Trick 1: The Five No's
No S. No TO. No ING. No DO. No TWINS.
Say it out loud three times. It takes ten seconds, and it covers the majority of modal verb errors you'll ever see.

Rule 1: No S

A modal never changes with the subject, and the verb after it doesn't take an -s either. We say He can swim, never He cans swim and never He can swims.

Rule 2: No TO

After a modal comes the plain verb without to. So it's You must leave, not You must to leave. The one exception is ought to, which carries its to everywhere: You ought to leave.

Rule 3: No ING

The verb after a modal stays in its base form. It can't be an -ing form or a past form. We say They can play, not They can playing or They can played. Also remember that modals have no -ing form or infinitive of their own, so when you need one, borrow be able to: I'm happy to be able to help.

Rule 4: No DO

Modals make their own questions and negatives. The modal itself moves or takes not. It's Can you drive?, never Do you can drive? And it's He cannot go, never He doesn't can go.

Rule 5: No TWINS

Standard English doesn't put two modals together. He will can help you is wrong. Swap the second modal for a phrase that works: He will be able to help you.

What each modal actually does

Before you memorise rules for each modal, get clear on the jobs they do. Most modal sentences fall into six jobs, and there's a handy word to hold them all.

Memory Trick 2: PRAISE
Permission, Request, Ability, Instruction (obligation), Suggestion (advice), Expectation (possibility or probability).
Whenever you meet a modal, ask: which letter of PRAISE is this sentence using? Once you've named the job, choosing the right modal gets much easier.

Here's PRAISE in action: May I leave? (permission). Could you pass the salt? (request). She can solve it in two minutes. (ability). You must carry your ID. (instruction). You should rest. (suggestion). It might rain. (expectation).

Now the full chart. Revise this table once a day for a week and these meanings will stick.

How sure are you? The certainty ladder

Here's something many students miss. Modals don't only talk about duty and permission. They also show how sure the speaker is. Read these six sentences slowly:

  • He must be at home. (almost certain)
  • He will be at home. (confident)
  • He should be at home. (expected)
  • He may be at home. (possible)
  • He might be at home. (less likely)
  • He could be at home. (just a possibility)

Same sentence, six levels of confidence. In a fill in the blanks question, the context decides which rung of the ladder fits. If the sentence says the speaker saw the lights on, must works. If the speaker has no idea, might or could is safer.

Memory Trick 3: The Ladder Sentence
My Wife Sometimes Makes Mango Cake.
Must, Will, Should, May, Might, Could. Strongest to weakest. Treat this as a rough guide, because tone and context can shift the level a little, but it gets you the right answer in most exam questions.

The tricky four: ought to, need, dare and used to

These four cause more confusion than all nine core modals put together, so let's sort them out quickly.

Ought to means moral duty or good advice, and it always keeps its to. You ought to apologise. In questions and negatives it's usually written as ought not to.

Need is a modal only in negatives and questions, and it takes no -s and no to: You needn't worry. Need he come? In positive sentences, need is an ordinary verb: He needs to rest. She needs to submit the form. Exam setters love to test exactly this switch.

Dare follows the same split. As a modal it appears in negatives and questions: He daren't speak. How dare you! As an ordinary verb it takes to and the -s: She dares to dream big.

Used to talks about a past habit or a past state that's no longer true: I used to walk to school. After used to, the verb stays plain. Don't confuse it with be used to, which takes -ing: I'm used to walking.

Seven exam traps with corrections

These are the patterns examiners return to again and again. Each one is a Common exam pattern in error spotting and sentence improvement.

✘ Wrong: She can sings very well.
✔ Right: She can sing very well.
Rule 1 and 3. After a modal, the verb stays in its base form. No -s.
✘ Wrong: You must to submit the form today.
✔ Right: You must submit the form today.
Rule 2. Modals don't take to. Only ought to does.
✘ Wrong: Do you can swim?
✔ Right: Can you swim?
Rule 4. The modal builds its own question. No do, does or did.
✘ Wrong: He will can help you tomorrow.
✔ Right: He will be able to help you tomorrow.
Rule 5. Two modals can't stand together. Use be able to for the second idea.
✘ Wrong: He musted leave early yesterday.
✔ Right: He had to leave early yesterday.
Must has no past form. For past obligation, use had to.
✘ Wrong: If he would come, I would help him.
✔ Right: If he came, I would help him.
In a standard conditional sentence, the if clause doesn't take would. Use the simple past there. (Polite phrases like If you would wait here are a different case.)
✘ Wrong: She said that she can join us.
✔ Right: She said that she could join us.
The reporting verb is in the past, so can shifts to could in indirect speech. Modals are a favourite place to hide a backshift error.

How exams test modal verbs

Knowing where the questions come from makes your revision sharper. Modal verbs appear in four main places.

  • Error spotting: a modal followed by the wrong verb form, an extra to, or a double modal. This is the most common pattern in SSC and Bank papers.
  • Fill in the blanks: you choose between close options such as can and may, or should and must, based on meaning.
  • Sentence improvement: the underlined part usually hides a tense or form problem, such as musted or would in an if-clause.
  • Cloze tests: a modal chosen to match the tone of the passage, like advice, possibility or obligation.

Notice that three of the four are really about form, and only one is about meaning. That's good news. Form errors follow rules, and rules can be memorised. Spend your first week on the Five No's and the traps, and only then move on to the finer differences between modals.

The SCAN method for exam questions

Knowing the rules is half the battle. Under exam pressure, you also need a routine. Here's mine, and my students tend to like it because it's short.

Memory Trick 4: SCAN
Spot the modal.
Check the verb after it. Is it plain, with no s, no to, no ing?
Analyse the meaning. Which PRAISE job is it doing?
Note the time. Is the sentence about the past, or in indirect speech?

Let's run one through. He said that he can finish the work by evening. S: the modal is can. C: finish is a plain verb, so no problem there. A: it's ability. N: the reporting verb said is in the past, so can must become could. Four steps, about eight seconds, and you've found the error.

The mistake most students make is jumping straight to meaning. They read the sentence the way they'd read a story, and the grammar slips past. SCAN forces your eye to the verb form first, which is where most modal errors live.

Five practice questions with answers

Try these without looking at the answers. Give yourself two minutes.

  • She can ___ the piano very well. (a) plays (b) play (c) playing (d) to play
  • ___ you lend me your pen for a minute? (a) Do (b) Are (c) Could (d) Have
  • Find the error: You must to complete the form before Friday.
  • Find the error: Do you can drive a car?
  • Improve the sentence: He musted leave early yesterday.

Answers:

  • (b) play. After a modal, use the base form.
  • (c) Could. A polite request needs a modal, and could is softer than can.
  • The error is to. Correct: You must complete the form before Friday.
  • The error is Do. Correct: Can you drive a car?
  • Correct: He had to leave early yesterday.

Frequently asked questions

How many modal verbs are there in English?

There are nine core modal verbs: can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would and must. Ought to is usually added as a tenth. Need, dare and used to are called semi-modals because they act like modals only in some sentences. Different grammar books count slightly differently, so follow the list your exam syllabus uses.

Is 'have to' a modal verb?

Not exactly. Have to is a modal-like expression. Unlike true modals, it changes with the subject (he has to), it needs do or does in questions and negatives (Does he have to go?), and it has a past form (had to). That's why have to is used to fill the gap when must has no past tense.

Can we use two modals together?

In standard English, no. Sentences like 'He will can help' or 'You should must go' are marked wrong in exams. Replace the second modal with a suitable phrase, for example 'He will be able to help'.

What is the difference between a modal verb and an auxiliary verb?

An auxiliary verb is any helping verb, and that includes be, do, have and the modals. Modal verbs are a special group of auxiliaries that express ability, permission, possibility, obligation or advice. That's why you'll also see the term modal auxiliaries.

How do I stop making silly mistakes with modals in exams?

Don't read the sentence for meaning first. Run the same quick check every time: find the modal, look at the verb that follows it, then think about meaning and time. The SCAN method in this guide is built for exactly that, and five minutes of daily practice makes it automatic.
