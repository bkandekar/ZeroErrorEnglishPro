Why Tenses Decide So Many Marks

Count how many places tenses appear in a competitive English paper. Error spotting, of course. Fill in the blanks. Sentence improvement. Reported speech. Conditionals. Cloze passages. Even in comprehension, understanding when something happened depends on the tense.

That's what makes tenses different from, say, idioms. An idiom question appears in one place. A tense rule appears in half the paper wearing different costumes. Which means every hour spent understanding tenses properly returns marks from many question types.

It also explains why aspirants who "know" tenses still lose marks. They know the forms, but they haven't practised the uses and the clue patterns that exams actually test. A good tense study plan fixes exactly that gap.

How to Use This Series in the Last Week Before Your Exam

If your exam is close, you don't need a new plan. You need a revision plan. Here is how I would use the six earlier posts in the final seven days.

On days one and two, reread the clue map from Day 1 and the Time-Expiry Test from Day 2. These two tools solve most error-spotting questions. On day three, revise the W.I.B.A.U. rule from Day 3 and attempt the practice set again without looking at the answers. On day four, go through the stative verb categories from Day 4 and the used to and would rules from Day 5, because these topics produce quick marks when you remember the exceptions.

On day five, solve twenty fill in the blanks using the T.S.I.V.O. ladder from Day 6. On day six, take one full mixed test and mark every error with the clue you missed. On the last day, do nothing new. Read your error log from start to finish and sleep well. The main thing is to keep the whole routine light. Fifteen minutes of focused revision a day, with a pencil in your hand, will do more for your tense accuracy than a night of reading without a plan.

A 10-Question Tense Self-Test

Before you decide how to study, find out where you stand. Attempt these ten questions on your own, using the clue method from this series, then check the answers. Nine or ten correct means you mainly need speed practice. Six to eight means a few patterns need attention. Below six, start with the foundation week of the plan below.

Q1. (a) I have met / (b) him yesterday / (c) at the station. / (d) No error.

Answer: (a). Yesterday needs the simple past: I met him yesterday.

Q2. (a) He said that / (b) he will / (c) call me later. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). After a past reporting verb, will becomes would.

Q3. If it ______ tomorrow, we will cancel the trip. (a) will rain (b) rains (c) rained (d) would rain

Answer: (b). If plus simple present gives the first conditional: if it rains.

Q4. (a) I am knowing / (b) his father / (c) very well. / (d) No error.

Answer: (a). Know is a state verb. The correct form is I know his father very well.

Q5. (a) We used to / (b) played / (c) in this park. / (d) No error.

Answer: (b). Used to takes the base verb: we used to play.

Q6. She ______ in Pune since 2018. (a) lives (b) lived (c) has lived (d) is living

Answer: (c). Since 2018 needs the present perfect: has lived.

Q7. (a) Hardly had she / (b) reached the office / (c) when it started to rain. / (d) No error.

Answer: (d). Hardly takes the inverted past perfect followed by when. The sentence is correct.

Q8. I wish I ______ taller. (a) am (b) was (c) were (d) will be

Answer: (c). After wish about an unreal present, exam English uses were.

Q9. (a) The teacher said / (b) that the earth / (c) is round. / (d) No error.

Answer: (d). A universal truth stays in the simple present, so the sentence is correct.

Q10. By the time I reached, the show ______. (a) started (b) has started (c) had started (d) starts

Answer: (c). The show began before I reached, so the earlier action takes the past perfect.

Two Aspirants, Thirty Days

Imagine two aspirants, A and B, who both start the same tense book on the same day. Aspirant A reads the book from beginning to end, three times, and solves very few questions. Aspirant B reads it once, but follows the daily routine above: five original sentences, practice questions and an error log, every day.

After thirty days, A feels familiar with the book. B feels familiar with her own mistakes. When both take a mixed test, B has the advantage, because she knows which three or four traps she personally falls into, and she's trained herself to check for them.

That's the difference between reading and studying. Reading builds familiarity. Studying builds accuracy. In exams, only accuracy earns marks.

Expert Tips to Remember

  • Start with thirty minutes a day. A routine you can keep beats a heroic plan you drop in week two.
  • Use the Y.A.L., S.F. and W.I.B.A.U. shortcuts from this series as flash cards on your phone.
  • Read your error log aloud on weekends. Hearing your own mistakes makes them stick.
  • Take one timed mixed test every Sunday in week four. Treat it like the real paper.
  • Teach one tense rule to a friend each week. If you can explain it simply, you have really learned it.

Quick Revision Checklist
