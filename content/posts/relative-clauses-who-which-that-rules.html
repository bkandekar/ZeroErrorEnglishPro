"The person which called you" — did you catch the error? If it took you a second look, you're exactly who this post is for. Relative pronouns feel simple until an exam sentence quietly swaps one for the wrong one, and most aspirants read straight past it.

Who, whom, whose, which, and that each have one clear job. Once you know which pronoun belongs to which situation, these questions stop being a guessing game and become some of the fastest marks on the paper.

The Core Concept: A Relative Clause Always Points Back

A relative clause is a type of adjective clause that describes a noun using a relative pronoun — who, whom, whose, which, or that. Each pronoun points back to a specific kind of noun and plays a specific role in its own clause.

Who / Whom

Used only for people. "Who" = subject, "Whom" = object.

The teacher who taught us left.

Which

Used only for things, animals, and ideas — never for people.

The car which he bought is new.

That

Used for people or things — but only in defining (essential) clauses.

The book that I bought is excellent.

The Definitive Identification Matrix

Pronoun Refers To Role in Clause Exam Trap Level
Who People only Subject of the clause HIGH
Whom People only Object of the clause CRITICAL
Whose People, animals or things Shows possession HIGH
Which Things, animals, ideas Subject or object CRITICAL
That People or things (defining clauses only) Subject or object CRITICAL

4 Worked Exam-Style Examples

Incorrect

The person which called you is waiting.

Correct

The person who called you is waiting.

Examiner Trap Analysis: "Which" can never refer to a person, no matter how casual the sentence sounds. "Person" always takes "who" or "that."
Incorrect

She is the woman who I met at the conference.

Correct

She is the woman whom I met at the conference.

Examiner Trap Analysis: "I" is already the subject of "met" — so the pronoun must be the object of the clause, which means "whom," not "who." Formal exam papers still test this distinction strictly.
Incorrect

The boy who's bag is missing is crying.

Correct

The boy whose bag is missing is crying.

Examiner Trap Analysis: "Who's" is a contraction of "who is" — it never shows possession. "Whose" is the possessive form and is what this sentence needs.
Incorrect

My father, that is a doctor, works at the city hospital.

Correct

My father, who is a doctor, works at the city hospital.

Examiner Trap Analysis: "That" cannot be used inside a comma-separated (non-defining) clause. Once commas appear around the clause, only "who" or "which" is allowed — never "that."

Two Real Exam Traps from SSC & Banking Papers

Exam Trap 1 — SSC CGL

"That" After a Comma

"That" is banned in non-defining (comma-separated) clauses — a favorite Error Spotting trap.

This book, that I bought yesterday, is excellent.

This book, which I bought yesterday, is excellent.

The book that I bought yesterday is excellent. (no commas — "that" is fine)

Exam Trap 2 — IBPS / SBI

Who vs Whom Under Pressure

Quick trick: if you can answer the clause with "he/she," use "who." If you'd answer with "him/her," use "whom."

The man whom called me is my neighbour.

The man who called me is my neighbour. (He called → who)

The 10-Second Shortcut: The He/Him Test

Mental Speed Technique

The He/Him Test

Mentally answer the relative clause using a pronoun:
1. Is the noun a person? If not → use "which."
2. If it's a person, would you answer with "he/she"? → use "who."
3. Would you answer with "him/her"? → use "whom."
4. Showing ownership? → use "whose," regardless of person or thing.

Extra check: "That" is only safe in defining clauses with no commas around them. The moment you see commas, cross "that" off the options.

Practice Questions with Step-by-Step Explanations

Q1: "The dog ___ barked all night belongs to my neighbour." Fill the blank. Show Answer

Correct Answer: which (or that)

Explanation: "Dog" is an animal, not a person, so "who" and "whom" are ruled out. No commas are present, so both "which" and "that" work.

Q2: "This is the officer ___ I spoke to yesterday." Fill the blank. Show Answer

Correct Answer: whom

Explanation: "I" is already the subject of "spoke," so the officer is the object of the clause — "I spoke to him," not "he spoke." Object + person = whom.

Q3: "Mumbai, ___ is the financial capital of India, is very crowded." Fill the blank. Show Answer

Correct Answer: which

Explanation: "Mumbai" is a place, not a person, and the clause is set off by commas — so "that" is not allowed here. "Which" is the only correct option.

Q4: "The student ___ project won first prize was felicitated." Fill the blank. Show Answer

Correct Answer: whose

Explanation: The blank shows possession — the project belongs to the student — so "whose" is required regardless of "student" being a person.

Frequently Asked Aspirant Questions (FAQ)

Q: Can "that" ever replace "who"?

Yes, but only in defining clauses with no commas — "The man that called you" is acceptable, though "who" is the more formal, exam-preferred choice for people.

Q: Is "whom" outdated? Do exams still test it?

Spoken English often drops "whom," but SSC and Bank papers still test it strictly in formal Error Spotting and Sentence Improvement questions — don't skip it in your preparation.

Q: Can the relative pronoun be omitted completely?

Yes, when it's the object of a defining clause — "The book (that) I read" is correct with or without "that." It cannot be omitted when it's the subject of the clause.
